## 1. Section-by-Section Feynman Explain-Back & Movability Check

### Chapter 0 — How big? How far? How sure?
- **§0.1 From Juhu beach to the Milky Way:** Every step of ten on a ruler multiplies the size by ten instead of adding, which is how a proton (`10⁻¹⁵ m`), a person (`10⁰ m`), and the Milky Way (`10²¹ m`) can all sit on the same 36-step ladder. Scientific notation writes how many tens you multiplied by as a small raised number so you never lose count of zeros. Two invisible things like an atom and a proton are still five steps of ten apart, which is as big a jump as from a grain of sand to a hundred-metre race. *(Movability: PASS — leads directly to why units must be agreed before measuring steps.)*
- **§0.2 A promise in a vault:** A measurement is a number multiplied by a unit, and without the unit the number is just a bare count that nobody in another city can check. For 130 years the kilogram was a platinum-iridium cylinder in a vault near Paris, until scientists noticed it was drifting by about fifty micrograms—the mass of a fingerprint—against its copies. In 2019 the world fixed the kilogram to Planck's constant (`h`) instead, so any good laboratory can build the exact unit from a rule of nature. *(Movability: PASS — once we have units, we can estimate things too big to weigh directly.)*
- **§0.3 Sixty million tonnes of rain:** Even when nobody can weigh all the monsoon rain on Mumbai or count every grain in a sack of rice, you can get a trustworthy rough number by breaking the problem into pieces you can defend. Multiply Mumbai's rough area (`600 km²`) by a `0.1 m` depth of rain and by `1,000 kg/m³`, and the units check out to give about 60 million tonnes of water. You only write the one or two digits your shakiest input has earned, because writing extra digits pretends to precision you do not have. *(Movability: PASS — leads into general Fermi problems and order-of-magnitude checks.)*
- **§0.4 How many piano tuners in Chicago?:** A Fermi problem chains simple guesses—people per city, people per home, pianos per home, and pianos tuned per year—into an order-of-magnitude answer like "about a hundred tuners". Because overestimates and underestimates partly cancel out along the chain, the final power of ten is usually much closer to the truth than you would expect. Doing this in thirty seconds gives you a nonsense detector that catches a misplaced decimal point before you trust a calculation. *(Movability: PASS — moves from back-of-the-envelope estimates to real experimental measurements.)*
- **§0.5 Changing one thing at a time:** When you test a pendulum at home, changing two things at once hides which one caused the difference, so you keep the weight and starting angle fixed while changing only the string's length. Timing ten swings together and dividing by ten shrinks your thumb's reaction-time error tenfold, and repeating the run shows your scatter. Doing that for `25 cm`, `50 cm`, and `100 cm` strings reveals that the swing time depends on the square root of the length, not the mass of the key at the bottom. *(Movability: PASS — combines geometry, measurement, and uncertainty into Eratosthenes' Earth measurement.)*
- **§0.6 Around the world with two sticks:** Two thousand years ago Eratosthenes used the `7.2°` noon shadow in Alexandria on the day a deep well in Syene had no shadow at all to see that the two cities sat one-fiftieth of the way round a curved Earth. Multiplying the `800 km` walk between them by `50` gave `40,000 km` for the whole Earth's circumference. Everything in the chapter—units, geometry, and honest uncertainty—comes together in that one calculation. *(Movability: PASS — closes Ch 0 and sets up measuring motion in Ch 1.)*

### Chapter 1 — Where are you, and how fast?
- **§1.1 The 8:14 from Dadar:** To say where the local train is, you first pick an origin like Dadar station and a positive direction along the track, which gives the train's position (`x`). Distance is the total ground covered along the way, while displacement (`Δx`) cares only about start to finish and includes a plus or minus sign for direction. Dividing distance by time gives average speed, which tells you what steady pace would cover the run in the same time, even though the train stopped at four stations in between. *(Movability: PASS — from average speed over a whole trip to instantaneous velocity at one moment.)*
- **§1.2 How fast *right now*?:** Your speed at a single instant is what you get when you shrink the time window until the speed barely changes inside it, and on a position–time graph that is simply the steepness (slope) of the line right there. Velocity is that speed together with its direction, so walking towards the back of a moving train subtracts from the train's velocity while walking forward adds to it. On a position–time graph, a steeper line means faster, a flat line means stopped, and a crossing of two lines only means two people were at the same place at the same time—not at the same speed. *(Movability: PASS — once we have velocity, we ask how velocity itself changes.)*
- **§1.3 Changing velocity:** Acceleration is how much your velocity changes each second, and in physics any change in speed *or* direction counts as an acceleration. On a velocity–time graph, the slope of the line is the acceleration and the area under the line is the displacement. A minus sign on acceleration does not automatically mean slowing down—it just means the change in velocity points in the negative direction. *(Movability: PASS — applies constant acceleration to Galileo's ramp.)*
- **§1.4 Rolling down a ramp:** Because free fall is too fast to time by hand, Galileo rolled a ball down a gentle ramp where it speeds up by the same amount every beat. Since the average speed grows in proportion to the time (`t`) and the roll lasts for a time `t`, the total distance grows with the square of the time (`1, 4, 9, 16`), and the distances covered in successive equal beats follow the odd numbers (`1, 3, 5, 7`). That means the position–time graph of constant acceleration is a parabola that curves upward, while its velocity–time graph is a straight sloping line. *(Movability: PASS — turns the ramp upright to a ball thrown straight up.)*
- **§1.5 Up, stop, down:** When you toss a ball straight up, its velocity shrinks by `9.8 m/s` every second on the way up, passes right through zero at the highest point, and keeps changing by `−9.8 m/s` every second on the way down. On a velocity–time graph the whole flight is a single straight line sloping downward that crosses zero at the top without ever going flat. Because the line is still sloping at the very top, the ball's acceleration at the peak is still `9.8 m/s²` downward—never zero. *(Movability: PASS — begs the question of *why* the velocity changes, leading to Ch 2.)*

### Chapter 2 — Why things keep going
- **§2.1 A stone on the maidan:** A cricket ball skidding across grassy ground slows down and stops, which led Aristotle to think that rest is natural and that moving things need a continuous push to keep going. Even when Stevin and de Groot dropped two lead balls at Delft and demolished Aristotle's rule for falling, people still believed horizontal motion needed an ongoing cause. To see the truth, you have to notice that the grass is not passive—it is actively pushing backward on the ball. *(Movability: PASS — examines the impetus patch before Galileo's breakthrough.)*
- **§2.2 The arrow and the invisible tank:** Medieval thinkers noticed that an arrow keeps flying after it leaves the bowstring and invented "impetus", an invisible internal supply of push that supposedly gets used up as the arrow flies. Most people today still draw that imaginary forward "force of the throw" on a flying ball, even though nothing is touching the ball to push it forward. The real story is the exact opposite: nothing is keeping the ball going, and it is the air and gravity that are changing its motion. *(Movability: PASS — replaces impetus with Galileo's double-ramp thought experiment.)*
- **§2.3 The ball that never stops:** When Galileo rolled a ball down one ramp and up a facing ramp, it always climbed back to nearly the same height, rolling further whenever the second ramp was made gentler. Extending that measured trend in his head to a completely flat, frictionless second ramp, he saw the ball could never reach its starting height and would roll on forever at a steady speed. You can see the trend in real life from rough concrete to polished tile to a carrom striker on boric-acid powder: less friction means less slowing down. *(Movability: PASS — turns Galileo's ramp insight into Newton's first law and inertia.)*
- **§2.4 The law of "nothing happens":** Newton's first law says that if all the pushes and pulls on an object cancel out to zero, an object at rest stays at rest and a moving object keeps moving in a straight line at the exact same speed. Inertia is not a force that pushes things along; it is just the name for the fact that matter has mass and keeps its velocity unless a net force changes it. When a bus brakes and you lurch forward, nothing threw you—the bus slowed down under your feet while your body kept going at the bus's old speed. *(Movability: PASS — tests the law in deep space where friction truly vanishes.)*
- **§2.5 Voyager in the dark:** Voyager 1 is flying out of the solar system at `17 km/s` with its fuel tanks empty and its thrusters off, and it does not slow down because there is no air in deep space to rub against it. On Earth, friction hides the first law by sneaking an uncounted backward force into every everyday motion, so a steady push on a heavy box only balances friction rather than proving that motion needs a force. Once you count friction honestly, Earth and deep space obey the exact same rule: forces *change* motion, they do not sustain it. *(Movability: PASS — leads directly to Ch 3: how much does a force change motion?)*

### Chapter 3 — Pushes, pulls, and the mosquito on the windscreen
- **§3.1 Adding arrows:** Because forces have both size and direction, they are vectors and add like arrows rather than ordinary numbers. What changes an object's velocity is the net force—the vector sum of every force acting *on that single object*—and when all the arrows on a tug-of-war rope add to zero, the rope stays still even though it is pulled tight from both ends. A diagram that isolates one object and draws only the forces acting *on* it is a free-body diagram, the clearest tool in mechanics. *(Movability: PASS — connects net force to acceleration and mass in §3.2.)*
- **§3.2 How much acceleration do you get for your push?:** Newton's second law (`F_net = ma`) says that the acceleration an object gets is proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass. Double the push on a cart and its acceleration doubles; double the cart's mass and the same push gives half the acceleration. One newton is simply the force needed to speed up a `1 kg` mass by `1 m/s²`, which makes the weight of a `1 kg` bag of atta on Earth about `9.8 N`. *(Movability: PASS — separates mass from weight and explains how surfaces push back.)*
- **§3.3 How a table knows how hard to push:** Mass (`kg`) is how much stuff is in you and stays the same everywhere, whereas weight (`mg`, in newtons) is how hard a planet's gravity pulls on that mass. When you set a book on a table, the book squashes the table's atomic springs by a tiny invisible amount until their upward normal force matches the book's weight. If you could see that microscopic squash—just like a sponge, a spring, or a steel ruler bridging two stacks of books—you would never doubt that a solid table pushes up. *(Movability: PASS — puts the normal force into a moving lift.)*
- **§3.4 Riding the lift on a bathroom scale:** A bathroom scale does not read your gravitational weight; it reads the upward normal force with which the scale's casing pushes on your feet. When the lift accelerates upward at `2 m/s²`, the scale has to push harder than your weight (`472 N`, reading `48 kg` for a `40 kg` person) to accelerate you upward with the lift, and when the lift accelerates downward at the top it pushes less (`312 N`, reading `32 kg`). During the steady-speed middle of the ride, acceleration is zero, so the scale reads your normal `40 kg`. *(Movability: PASS — moves from forces on one object to force pairs between two objects.)*
- **§3.5 The mosquito and the car:** Newton's third law says that every force is one end of a two-sided interaction between two objects: push A with B, and A pushes B back with the exact same force in the opposite direction at the same instant. Because the two forces act on *different* objects, they never cancel each other out, and the weight + normal force on a standing person are *not* a third-law pair because both act on the person. When a `1,000 kg` car hits a `2 mg` mosquito, both feel the exact same `0.04 N` force, which barely changes the car's speed (`4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s²`) while giving the tiny mosquito `2,000 g` of acceleration. *(Movability: PASS — multiplies equal-and-opposite forces by equal contact time to get momentum conservation.)*
- **§3.6 Rockets from a church in Kerala:** Because two colliding or pushing objects feel equal and opposite forces for the exact same time, the momentum (`mv`) one gains is the exact momentum the other loses, keeping total momentum constant whenever no outside force acts. A rocket at Thumba or over the Moon does not push against the air or the ground; it throws hot exhaust gas backward at high speed, and the gas pushes the rocket forward with equal momentum. That works even better in the vacuum of space than in air. *(Movability: PASS — closes Ch 3 and sets up gravity and 2D motion in Ch 4.)*

### Chapter 4 — Falling, flying, and going round
- **§4.1 The single thud at Delft:** When Stevin and de Groot dropped two lead balls—one ten times heavier than the other—from `30 ft` (`9.1 m`) onto a board in Delft in 1586, they heard a single thud after `1.37 s` instead of the two thuds `12 s` apart predicted by Aristotle. The reason every mass falls at the same rate in free fall is a neat cancellation between Chapters 3 and 5: ten times the mass gets ten times the gravitational pull (`F = mg`), and ten times the mass also has ten times the inertia (`a = F/m = g`). Without air, heavy and light fall together. *(Movability: PASS — Kym measures `g` for herself in §4.2.)*
- **§4.2 Timing a fall with your phone:** Using a phone camera in slow motion (`240 fps`) to film a ball dropping `1.00 m` alongside a taped wall lets you count about `108` frames (`0.452 s`) and calculate `g = 2h/t² ≈ 9.8 m/s²` right in a Mumbai flat. Once you know `g`, a `30 cm` ruler becomes a reaction-time stopwatch: catching it after it drops `16 cm` means your brain and hand took `0.18 s` (`t = √(2d/g)`), which is why a thumb on a stopwatch cannot time a one-metre drop accurately. *(Movability: PASS — explains why feathers and raindrops violate the simple vacuum rule.)*
- **§4.3 Why the feather loses, and why raindrops don't kill you:** In air, a falling object feels an upward drag force that grows roughly with the square of its speed until drag balances weight, net force hits zero, and the object falls at a steady terminal velocity. A flat sheet of paper or a `2 mm` monsoon raindrop (`terminal speed ≈ 6.5 m/s`, versus `710 km/h` without air) reaches that balance quickly because its drag is large compared with its weight, whereas a crumpled paper ball or a lead sphere hardly notices the air over a short drop. On the Moon, where there is no air and `g ≈ 1.62–1.67 m/s²`, David Scott's hammer and falcon feather fell side by side and landed together. *(Movability: PASS — adds sideways motion to falling in §4.4.)*
- **§4.4 Two coins at the edge of a table:** When you flick one coin sideways off a table at the exact instant a second coin is dropped straight down from the same edge, both hit the floor with a single clink (`0.40 s` for an `80 cm` table). That proves horizontal and vertical motion are completely independent: the horizontal velocity stays constant (`a_x = 0`, Chapter 2) while the vertical velocity speeds up downward at `9.8 m/s²` (`a_y = g`), tracing a parabola. Even if you aim straight at a target that lets go the moment you throw, both the ball and the target fall the exact same `½gt²` below the aim line and meet in mid-air. *(Movability: PASS — curves the path all the way round into a circle in §4.5.)*
- **§4.5 Whirling a stone, turning a corner:** Anything moving in a circle at steady speed is constantly changing the direction of its velocity, which means it has an inward acceleration of size `v²/r` toward the centre of the circle. That inward acceleration requires a real inward force—string tension, tyre friction, a bucket's bottom, or gravity—and "centripetal force" is just the job title for whichever real force points inward, never an extra arrow of its own. If the inward force stops, the object flies off straight along the tangent (not radially outward), and the "flung outward" feeling on a turning BEST bus is simply your own body carrying straight on while the bus turns under you. *(Movability: PASS — sets up the Moon's orbit in Ch 5 as circular motion under gravity.)*
