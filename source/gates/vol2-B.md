## 1. Section-by-Section Feynman Explain-Back & Movability Audit

### Chapter 5 — Gravity and Orbits (`src/ch05.md`)

| Section | 12-Year-Old's 3-Sentence Explain-Back | Movability / Mechanism Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **5.1 The apple and the Moon** | The Moon does not fly off in a straight line because Earth pulls it inward, bending its path by $1.36\text{ mm}$ every second. The Moon is 60 times farther from Earth's centre than an apple on a tree, and $60 \times 60 = 3600$, so Earth's pull at the Moon is 3600 times weaker than $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$. That makes the apple's $4.9\text{ m}$ fall in one second and the Moon's $1.36\text{ mm}$ fall in one second the exact same pull following the inverse-square rule. | **PASS** — `inverse-square` and `gravity` are earned via the $60^2 = 3600$ numerical match before being named. |
| **5.2 Falling without hitting** | An astronaut in the space station is only $400\text{ km}$ up, where Earth's gravity is still almost $90\%$ as strong as on the ground. She floats because she, her pen, and the whole station are falling together at the same rate, so the floor never pushes up on her feet. You only feel weight when something solid pushes back against you, just like a phone falling onto a pillow reads zero on its sensor while it is in the air. | **PASS** — Dismantles "zero gravity in space" by distinguishing gravitational pull from contact support force (`weightlessness` = `free fall`). |
| **5.3 Newton's cannonball** | If you fire a ball faster and faster horizontally from a high mountain, it falls toward Earth at the same $4.9\text{ m}$ in the first second, but the curved Earth drops away underneath it too. At about $7.9\text{ km/s}$, the ball's path curves down by $4.9\text{ m}$ every $8\text{ km}$, which matches the curve of the Earth, so it falls all the way around in 84 minutes without ever getting closer to the ground. Fire it a bit faster and it swings out into a stretched oval and comes back to the mountain. | **PASS** — `orbit` is constructed geometrically from projectile fall + Earth's curvature. |
| **5.4 Three rules for every orbit** | Planets and satellites travel in ellipses with the central body at one focus, not dead centre, though most planet orbits are almost circles. As a planet swings closer to the Sun, the Sun's pull has a forward part that speeds it up, and as it swings away, the pull has a backward part that slows it down, sweeping out equal areas in equal times. Bigger orbits both have further to go and move more slowly, so the square of the lap time grows with the cube of the orbit's average radius ($T^2 \propto r^3$). | **PASS** — Each of Kepler's three laws is tied to a physical mechanism and tested on Mars, Jupiter, and India's $36{,}000\text{ km}$ geostationary weather satellites. |
| **5.5 Weighing the Earth in a shed** | Newton knew how hard the whole Earth pulls an apple, but he didn't know how hard one kilogram pulls another kilogram, so he couldn't separate Earth's mass from the strength of gravity. A century later, Henry Cavendish hung two small lead balls on a thin wire inside a draught-proof shed and measured how far two heavy lead spheres twisted the wire, finding that $1\text{ kg}$ pulls $1\text{ kg}$ a metre away with $6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N}$. Once you know that tiny number, the $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ fall of an apple tells you the Earth has a mass of $6 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg}$, making it $5.5$ times as dense as water. | **PASS** — Clearly explains why $g$ alone cannot give $M_E$ without $G$, and how $G$ reveals Earth's iron core. |
| **5.6 Leaving, and getting there on a budget** | Because gravity weakens with the square of distance as you climb, you don't need infinite speed to leave Earth for ever: $11.2\text{ km/s}$ ($\sqrt{2}$ times low-orbit speed) is enough that gravity can never quite stop you. If your rocket isn't powerful enough to go straight to the Moon at $11\text{ km/s}$, you can fire the engine briefly at the lowest, fastest point of your orbit each lap to stretch the far end higher step by step. That is how ISRO's Chandrayaan-3 climbed to the Moon in 40 days on a tighter fuel budget. | **PASS** — Connects escape speed and Oberth/perigee-kick orbit raising to Chandrayaan-3. |

### Chapter 6 — Pressure and Fluids (`src/ch06.md`)

| Section | 12-Year-Old's 3-Sentence Explain-Back | Movability / Mechanism Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **6.1 The sharp and the flat** | Your fingers care about how concentrated a push is, not just the total force. Divide the perpendicular force by the area it presses on and you get pressure ($p = F/A$), measured in pascals ($\text{N/m}^2$). That is why a knife edge cuts when the flat of the blade does not, and why a $50\text{ kg}$ dancer balancing on a $1\text{ cm}^2$ stiletto heel puts 25 times more pressure on the floor than a 4-tonne elephant. | **PASS** — `pressure` and `pascal` are motivated by the pencil-thumb experiment and elephant-vs-stiletto calculation. |
| **6.2 An ocean of air** | We live at the bottom of a deep ocean of air whose weight presses on every surface around us with about $101{,}000\text{ Pa}$, roughly $10\text{ N}$ on every square centimetre. In Torricelli's barometer, the outside air pressing on the open dish holds up a $76\text{ cm}$ column of heavy mercury (or a $10.3\text{ m}$ column of water) whose weight per unit area matches the air's push. When Périer carried the tube up a $1{,}000\text{ m}$ mountain, there was less air above him, so the mercury stood $8\text{ cm}$ shorter. | **PASS** — `atmospheric pressure` is proved by Périer's Puy de Dôme ascent. |
| **6.3 Nothing sucks** | A vacuum has zero particles in it, so an empty space can never grab or pull on anything. When you turn a full glass of water upside down with a wet postcard on the rim, or drink through a straw, or press a rubber hook against bathroom tiles, you are lowering the pressure on one side so the ordinary room air on the other side pushes the card, drink, or cup in. Every time someone says "suction pulls", what is really happening is higher-pressure air pushing toward lower-pressure air. | **PASS** — Completely eliminates the "vacuum sucks" misconception. |
| **6.4 Deeper means harder** | At any depth $h$ in a liquid, the pressure comes from the weight of the column of liquid standing above each square metre, which gives $p = \rho g h$. Because the area cancels out when you divide weight by area, the pressure at $2\text{ m}$ depth is $19{,}600\text{ Pa}$ whether you are in a narrow pipe or a $50\text{ m}$ swimming pool. That is why dams are thick at the bottom and why the length of the lake behind the dam makes no difference to the push on the wall. | **PASS** — Derives $p = \rho g h$ from first principles and resolves the hydrostatic paradox. |
| **6.5 Why ships float** | Because water pressure increases with depth, the water under a submerged brick pushes up harder on its bottom face than the water above pushes down on its top face, and the difference equals the weight of the water the brick pushed aside. A solid block of steel is $7.8$ times denser than water, so it displaces less water than its own weight and sinks. Beat that same steel into a hollow hull that traps lots of light air inside, and the hull displaces its own weight of water before the deck goes under, so it floats. | **PASS** — Derives `buoyant force` and `Archimedes' principle` from Section 6.4 depth pressure. |

### Chapter 7 — Energy (`src/ch07.md`)

| Section | 12-Year-Old's 3-Sentence Explain-Back | Movability / Mechanism Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **7.1 What is energy?** | Saying "a ball rolls because it has energy" explains nothing, just like saying "it rolls because it has movability." Energy is really a bookkeeping total: each store — motion, height, stretch, warmth, fuel, light — has an exact formula that gives a number in joules, and whenever anything happens, the sum of all those numbers before and after stays exactly the same. If the sum ever looks like it changed, none vanished; some of it simply moved into a store (usually warmth) that you haven't measured yet. | **PASS** — Explicitly passes the Movability Test in the text itself! |
| **7.2 The energy of moving things** | When you drop a ball from four times the height, it falls for twice the time and hits at twice the speed, yet it makes a dent four times as deep in soft clay or flour. That proves the energy of a moving body grows with the square of its speed, $\text{KE} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, measured in joules ($1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$). Because speed is squared, a scooter or car going three times as fast has nine times the kinetic energy and needs nine times the braking distance to stop. | **PASS** — Distinguishes momentum ($mv$, force $\times$ time) from kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}mv^2$, force $\times$ distance). |
| **7.3 Storing energy by lifting** | When you lift a mass $m$ by a height $h$ against Earth's pull $mg$, you do $mgh$ joules of work, and that energy is stored in the gravitational store of the object and Earth. If you let a marble roll down a smooth track, every centimetre it drops trades $mgh$ for $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ at a strict one-for-one exchange rate, and because $m$ is in both formulas, a heavy marble and a light marble reach the same speed $\sqrt{2gh}$ at the bottom. The gravitational energy stored depends only on the vertical height gained, whether you climb straight up a rope or wind up four flights of stairs. | **PASS** — `work` and `gravitational potential energy` are linked directly to the rollercoaster/bowl exchange. |
| **7.4 Where does it go?** | When a ball dropped from $1.0\text{ m}$ bounces back to only $0.64\text{ m}$, the missing $36\%$ of its energy was not destroyed; friction and squashing turned it into tiny jiggling motions of the molecules in the ball, the floor, and the air. We call that store thermal energy, and James Joule proved with falling weights turning a paddle-wheel in water that mechanical work trades into warmth at a fixed rate. Useful machines always lose some energy into this thermal store, and efficiency is just the fraction of the input energy that comes out in the store you wanted. | **PASS** — Resolves the apparent loss of mechanical energy without hand-waving. |
| **7.5 How fast? Power and the bill** | Energy is the total amount of work done in joules, whereas power is how fast you transfer it, in joules per second (watts). The electricity company sells energy, not power, and counts it in kilowatt-hours ($1\text{ kWh} = 1{,}000\text{ W} \times 3{,}600\text{ s} = 3.6\text{ million joules}$), which is called one "unit" on the monthly bill. When the bill says "320 units consumed", not a single joule was destroyed; your flat took in $320\text{ kWh}$ in the tidy electrical store and leaked all $320\text{ kWh}$ out into the air as low-grade warmth after doing its jobs. | **PASS** — Cleanly answers the chapter's anchor question. |
| **7.6 From the power station to the plug** | Every unit of electricity arriving at your wall socket was moved out of an earlier store at a power station: burning coal or gas (chemical $\to$ thermal $\to$ kinetic $\to$ electrical, about $40\%$ efficient), falling dam water (gravitational $\to$ kinetic $\to$ electrical, up to $90\%$ efficient), wind, nuclear, or sunlight. In Mumbai's grid reports, peak demand is measured in megawatts (power, the size of the pipes needed at 4 p.m.) while annual sales are measured in gigawatt-hours (energy, the total water that flowed all year). | **PASS** — Grounds Sankey diagrams and power-vs-energy in real Mumbai grid data. |
