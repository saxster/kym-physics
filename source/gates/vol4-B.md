## 1. Three-Sentence Explain-Back per Section (12-Year-Old's Words)

### Chapter 12 — Charge, Current and Circuits (`src/ch12.md`)
- **12.1 Two kinds of charge, and why the monsoon spoils the trick:** Rubbing a balloon or comb moves tiny negative electrons from one surface to the other so opposite charges attract and like charges repel, and a charged comb can even pull neutral paper bits by nudging their charges into a closer-and-farther pair. In Mumbai's humid monsoon air, a microscopic film of salty moisture coats everything and lets the extra charge leak away before it can build up. That is why static tricks work best on a dry January afternoon or inside an air-conditioned room.
- **12.2 What flows in a wire:** An electric current in a copper wire is a slow drift of free electrons—about a tenth of a millimetre per second—counted in amperes, where one ampere means $6.24 \times 10^{18}$ electrons passing a point every second. The lamp lights up the instant you flick the switch not because an electron races from the switch to the bulb, but because the electric push travels through the wire near the speed of light and starts every electron moving at once. By historical convention, arrows on circuit diagrams point the way positive charge would move, opposite to the electrons' actual drift.
- **12.3 The push and the choke: voltage, resistance and Ohm's law:** Voltage (in volts) is the energy push given to each coulomb of charge by a cell or socket, while resistance (in ohms) is how hard a narrow or long path opposes that flow. Ohm's law, $I = V / R$, says the current grows when you increase the push and shrinks when you increase the resistance. A real battery also has a tiny internal resistance of its own, so when a car starter motor draws a huge current, much of the voltage drops inside the battery and the headlights dim.
- **12.4 One path or many: series and parallel:** In a series circuit there is only one loop, so the exact same current flows through every bulb, the voltages add up to the battery's push, and unscrewing one bulb kills the whole string. In a parallel circuit each appliance sits on its own branch across the full $230\text{ V}$ supply, so each can be switched on or off independently and adding another branch increases the total current from the supply. Every flat in Mumbai is wired in parallel, with fuses and MCBs in series on the live wire to trip if the total current gets dangerously high.
- **12.5 Power, the meter on the staircase, and why grid lines hum at $11{,}000\text{ V}$:** Electric power $P = V I$ tells you how many joules per second an appliance uses, so a $2{,}000\text{ W}$ geyser or a $1{,}500\text{ W}$ air conditioner running for hours dominates the monthly kilowatt-hour bill compared with $9\text{ W}$ LED bulbs. Power lines lose heat as $I^2 R$ in the cables, which depends on the square of the current. By stepping the voltage up to $11{,}000\text{ V}$ or higher for the journey, the grid shrinks the current nearly fifty-fold and cuts line heating losses more than two-thousand-fold.
- **12.6 Case file — The flat's electricity bill:** Reading the nameplate watts on a flat's LEDs, ceiling fans, fridge, geyser, and AC and multiplying by the hours they run over 30 days predicts the kilowatt-hour units on the BEST or Tata Power bill. Three-quarters of a summer bill comes from the air conditioner alone, because it combines high wattage with six hours of daily use. Checking the sum against the staircase meter turns an opaque bill into simple arithmetic.

### Chapter 13 — Magnets, Motors and Waves (`src/ch13.md`)
- **13.1 Poles that never come solo, and the field around a magnet:** Every magnet has a north-seeking and a south-seeking pole, and snapping a magnet in half never isolates a single pole because magnetism comes from aligned electron currents inside the iron's domains, creating new poles at the break. The space around a magnet carries a magnetic field whose direction at any point is the way a tiny compass needle points and whose strength is where the field lines crowd closest together. Unlike charges, magnetic poles always come in north–south pairs whose field lines form closed loops.
- **13.2 The Earth is a magnet (mostly pointed the wrong way):** A compass needle points north because the churning liquid iron in Earth's outer core acts as a giant dynamo whose magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is actually a *south* magnetic pole. At Alibag and Mumbai ($19^\circ\text{ N}$), the Earth's field of about $43{,}400\text{ nT}$ points almost due north horizontally and dips $27^\circ$ down into the ground. Because the core flows shift slowly, magnetic north drifts from year to year and has even flipped direction many times in Earth's rock record.
- **13.3 Ørsted's twitch and the Bombay trams:** In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that an electric current in a wire creates a circular magnetic field around the wire, linking electricity and magnetism for the first time. Winding the wire into a coil concentrates that field into an electromagnet you can switch on and off. When Bombay electrified its trams in 1907, the $500\text{ A}$ current in the overhead wires created a $500\text{ nT}$ magnetic disturbance two hundred metres away—enough to drown out the Colaba Observatory's delicate geomagnetic readings and force the observatory to move across the harbour to Alibag.
- **13.4 The push that spins the ceiling fan:** When a wire carrying current $I$ sits across a magnetic field $B$, the field pushes the wire sideways with a force $F = B I L$. Loop the wire so current goes out on one side and back on the other, and the opposite sideways pushes twist the loop into a spinning electric motor. Every ceiling fan, mixer-grinder, and local-train traction motor in Mumbai works by keeping that magnetic twist going round after round.
- **13.5 Faraday's ring: how a moving magnet makes a current:** Faraday showed in 1831 that a stationary magnet inside a coil does nothing at all, but a *changing* magnetic field through a coil induces a voltage that drives a current in a direction opposing the change. Spinning a coil in a magnetic field at a power station generates alternating current (AC), and two coils sharing an iron core form a transformer that steps AC voltage up or down in proportion to the turns ratio $N_2 / N_1$. That is why the grid uses AC: transformers need a continuously changing magnetic field to work.
- **13.6 Maxwell's leap and Bose's five-millimetre waves:** James Clerk Maxwell realised that a changing electric field also makes a magnetic field, so the two can leapfrog through empty space as a self-sustaining wave travelling at $3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$—the exact speed of light. Light, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and radio waves are all the same electromagnetic wave at different wavelengths and frequencies ($c = f\lambda$). In 1895 in Calcutta, Jagadish Chandra Bose shrank these waves to $5\text{ mm}$ ($60\text{ GHz}$) and beamed them through walls, bridging the gap between long radio waves and light.

### Chapter 14 — The Turning Earth, the Moon and the Tides (`src/ch14.md`)
- **14.1 Aryabhata's boat: why the sky seems to turn:** The Sun, Moon, and stars appear to rise in the east and set in the west not because the sky orbits us, but because the Earth spins eastward on its axis once every $23\text{ hours } 56\text{ minutes}$. Just as Aryabhata compared us to passengers on a smoothly moving boat watching the riverbank slide backwards, we do not feel the $1{,}580\text{ km/h}$ spin at Mumbai because our bodies only sense changes in velocity. Léon Foucault made the Earth's rotation visible indoors in 1851 by hanging a long pendulum whose swing plane stays fixed in space while the floor slowly turns underneath it.
- **14.2 Why June is hot and December is mild: the $23.4^\circ$ tilt:** Seasons are not caused by the Earth getting closer to the Sun—in fact, Earth is closest to the Sun in early January. Instead, Earth's spin axis is tilted by $23.4^\circ$ relative to its orbit, so in June the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun, making noon sunlight hit more squarely from high overhead and lengthening the daylight hours. Because Mumbai sits at $19^\circ\text{ N}$ inside the tropics, the noon Sun stays fairly high all year ($85.6^\circ$ in June vs $47.6^\circ$ in December), whereas at London ($51.5^\circ\text{ N}$) the winter Sun skims just $15^\circ$ above the horizon.
- **14.3 Why the Moon changes shape (and why it is not Earth's shadow):** Half of the Moon's ball is always lit by the Sun, and the phases we see from new crescent to full Moon are simply how much of that sunlit half faces us as the Moon orbits the Earth every $29.5\text{ days}$. Earth's shadow has nothing to do with monthly phases—when the Moon is half-lit at first quarter, it sits at a $90^\circ$ angle from the Sun where Earth's shadow cannot possibly reach it. Hold a ball at arm's length in a sunny beam and turn on the spot, and you see the exact same cycle of phases on the ball in ten seconds.
- **14.4 When the shadows really line up: solar and lunar eclipses:** Eclipses happen only when the Sun, Earth, and Moon line up straight—at new Moon for a solar eclipse (Moon's shadow on Earth) or full Moon for a lunar eclipse (Moon passing through Earth's shadow). We do not get an eclipse every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted by $5.1^\circ$ relative to Earth's orbit, so most months the Moon passes slightly above or below the shadow line. By a cosmic coincidence, the Sun is about $400$ times wider than the Moon and also $400$ times farther away, so both look half a degree wide in our sky and the Moon can just blot out the Sun's disc.
- **14.5 Two high tides a day at Haji Ali:** The Moon's gravity pulls hardest on the ocean water on the side of Earth closest to the Moon, weakest on the water on the far side, and with an intermediate pull on the solid Earth in between. That difference in pull stretches the oceans into two bulges—one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side of the Earth—so as the Earth spins through both bulges, Mumbai's shore sees two high tides and two low tides every lunar day of $24\text{ hours } 50\text{ minutes}$ ($12\text{ hours } 25\text{ minutes}$ apart). At new and full Moon the Sun's tidal stretch adds to the Moon's to make extra-high spring tides, while at quarter Moons they partially cancel to make gentler neap tides.
- **14.6 Case file — The Haji Ali walk:** If low tide uncovers the Haji Ali causeway at 10:30 a.m. on a Saturday, the next day's low tide is about $50\text{ minutes}$ later because the Moon has moved along its orbit while the Earth spun. Over seven days, the low-tide time shifts by nearly six hours, so returning at 10:30 a.m. the following Saturday puts you right near high tide with the causeway underwater. Tracking the $12\text{ h } 25\text{ min}$ rhythm lets you predict safe crossing times for a whole week.

### Chapter 15 — Photons, Nuclei and the Stars (`src/ch15.md`)
- **15.1 Light arrives in packets: Einstein and S. N. Bose:** Even though light travels and interferes like a wave, it delivers its energy in individual packets called photons whose energy $E = hf$ depends only on frequency. That explains why dim violet light (big packets) can knock electrons out of calcium immediately while bright red or infrared light (small packets below the binding fee) never can, no matter how bright. In 1924 in Dacca, Satyendra Nath Bose showed how to derive the laws of radiation by counting photons as completely indistinguishable particles, now called bosons.
- **15.2 Radiation, half-life, and why an X-ray does not leave you glowing:** Only high-frequency radiation at the top of the electromagnetic ladder—far ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays, plus particles from unstable nuclei—carries enough energy per packet to ionise atoms and break molecular bonds. Having a chest X-ray is *exposure* (the photons pass through in an instant and leave nothing radioactive behind in your body), whereas *contamination* means carrying actual radioactive dust whose unstable nuclei keep decaying with a fixed half-life. You can hug your family safely right after an X-ray because being hit by light or X-rays never turns your own atoms radioactive.
- **15.3 Why the Sun has shone for $4.5\text{ billion}$ years:** Chemical burning could keep the Sun shining for only a few thousand years, and gravitational shrinking for only about a hundred million, whereas Earth's rocks show the Sun has shone for $4.5\text{ billion}$ years. In the Sun's $15\text{-million-kelvin}$ core, four hydrogen nuclei fuse into one helium nucleus that weighs $0.71\%$ less, and that missing mass becomes energy according to Einstein's $E = mc^2$. Fusing $600\text{ million tonnes}$ of hydrogen per second converts $4\text{ million tonnes}$ of mass into sunlight every second, yet has used up only $0.03\%$ of the Sun's mass over its entire lifetime.
- **15.4 Reading a star's barcode: Meghnad Saha and Cecilia Payne:** Dark lines in a star's rainbow spectrum are the fingerprints of elements, but Meghnad Saha proved in Calcutta in 1920 that whether an element's lines show up depends critically on temperature, which strips electrons off atoms. Using Saha's ionisation equation on Harvard's glass plates in 1925, Cecilia Payne showed that stars of different spectral classes are not made of different elements—they are all made of about $73\%$ hydrogen and $25\%$ helium by mass, at different surface temperatures.
- **15.5 How stars end: Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar's limit:** When a star like the Sun runs out of core fuel, it shrinks into an Earth-sized white dwarf held up against gravity by the quantum degeneracy pressure of crowded electrons. On a ship out of Bombay in 1930, nineteen-year-old Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar included Einstein's relativistic speed limit for those electrons and proved that no white dwarf can be heavier than about $1.4$ solar masses. Stellar cores heavier than the Chandrasekhar limit collapse further into city-sized neutron stars or black holes, blasting their outer layers away in supernova explosions that seed space with heavy elements.
- **15.6 From Bessel's triangle to Hubble's stretching universe:** Astronomers measure the distances to nearby stars by their tiny six-month parallax shift as Earth orbits the Sun, and step out to distant galaxies using Henrietta Leavitt's Cepheid variable stars, whose blink period reveals their true brightness. In 1929 Edwin Hubble combined Cepheid distances with spectral redshifts to show that distant galaxies recede with speeds proportional to their distance ($v = H_0 d$). Rather than galaxies flying through fixed space from a central explosion, space itself is stretching everywhere between the galaxies, and running that expansion backwards gives a universe about $13.8\text{ billion}$ years old.
