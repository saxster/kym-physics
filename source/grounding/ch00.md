## 0.1 What we see and what we think

**Concept sequence**
1. Science starts from observation. It must describe the world as it is, not as we would like it to be. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
2. An observation raises a question. A hypothesis is a testable guess about how something works. An experiment collects data that supports or rejects it. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
3. A model represents something too hard (or impossible) to study directly. It is built from earlier experiments and is usually right only in limited situations. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction
4. A law is a short statement of a pattern that holds everywhere it has been tested. A theory is a well-tested explanation, which is not the same as a guess. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction
5. Laws can never be proved with absolute certainty, because nobody can run every possible experiment. If an experiment disagrees with a prediction, the theory is wrong, however elegant it is. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction
6. In Newton's method, physical models are built from experimental observations and then generalised. He rejected hypotheses that had no experimental basis ("Hypotheses non fingo"). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter1.pdf
7. Every model has limits. Newtonian mechanics fails for very fast motion (above about 0.1 c), for atoms (quantum mechanics) and for the large-scale universe (general relativity). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter1.pdf
8. Worked example of seeing versus modelling, from Greek astronomy:
   - Observations: Earth's shadow on the Moon in a lunar eclipse is always round; travellers going south see new stars, and the North Star sinks lower; departing ships disappear hull first. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
   - Model: a spherical Earth. Only a sphere always casts a round shadow; a disc seen edge-on would cast a line. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
9. The same section includes a cautionary case. The Greeks looked hard for stellar parallax and found none. Most then kept an Earth-centred model, rather than accept that the stars are immensely far away. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy

**Definitions**
- *Observation:* where an investigation starts; noticing a pattern or trend in nature. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
- *Hypothesis:* a testable statement about how something in nature works. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
- *Model:* "A model is a representation of something that is often too difficult (or impossible) to study directly." https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
- *Law:* a description of a pattern that holds in all circumstances studied, often a single equation. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
- *Theory:* an explanation of patterns in nature, supported by much evidence and checked many times by different groups. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction
- *IGCSE 0625:* has no observation/model topic. Topic 1.1 is "Physical quantities and measurement techniques" (source/research/school-wording.md, quoting https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697209-2026-2028-syllabus.pdf). For MYP, "Models" and "Evidence" are among the Physics related concepts (source/research/school-wording.md §3).

**Warnings the sources give**
- A theory is not "just a guess"; to a scientist, "it's just a theory" makes no sense. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction
- Models usually describe a phenomenon only partially, or only in some situations. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods
- Laws and theories can still change when new technology lets us see what was invisible before. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-2-the-scientific-methods (Teacher Support)

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Scientists prove things" is false. Evidence supports a law; it never proves it absolutely (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction). True minimal version: "Every test so far agrees. One good test that disagreed would sink it."
- "The scientific method is a fixed 5-step recipe" is false. OS College says the method "can be modified to suit the situation" (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-1-physics-an-introduction). Use "a habit", not "the steps".
- "The Greeks didn't know the Earth was round" is false. Educated people in the eastern Mediterranean knew it at least 2000 years before Columbus (https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy).
- "Pythagoras showed the Earth is round" is false. He *believed* it because spheres were "perfect forms". That was an aesthetic belief, not evidence (https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy). This contrast is a ready-made example of model versus evidence.

## 0.2 Measuring anything

**Concept sequence**
1. A measurement is a number times a unit; units are standardised values. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
2. SI has seven base quantities: length (m), mass (kg), time (s), current (A), temperature (K), amount of substance (mol), luminous intensity (cd). Mechanics needs only the first three. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
3. Derived units are combinations of base units: m/s, m/s², N = kg·m·s⁻², J = kg·m²·s⁻², W = kg·m²·s⁻³. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
4. The definitions moved from objects to constants of nature.
   - The second went from 1/86,400 of a mean solar day to a count of caesium vibrations (1967).
   - The meter went from 1/10,000,000 of the equator-to-pole arc (1791), to a platinum–iridium bar (1889), to krypton light (1960), to the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 s (1983).
   - The kilogram went from a metal cylinder to the Planck constant (2019).
   - Sources: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
5. Since 20 May 2019, all seven base units are defined by seven fixed constants. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
6. Every measurement has uncertainty, so the next ideas are accuracy, precision, percent uncertainty and significant figures. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units

**Definitions**
- *Units:* standardised values in which physical quantities are expressed. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
- *Base vs derived units:* base units are defined by a measurement process; all others are algebraic combinations of them. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
- *Accuracy:* how close a measurement is to the correct value. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
- *Precision:* how closely repeated measurements agree with one another. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
- *Uncertainty:* the range of values consistent with the data; every measurement has some. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
- *Kilogram (2019):* defined by fixing the Planck constant h at exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, with the meter and second defined through c and the caesium frequency. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- *IGCSE 0625:* mass is "a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer"; weight is a gravitational force; mass is a scalar, weight a vector (source/research/school-wording.md, quoting https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697209-2026-2028-syllabus.pdf). Topic 1.1 is "Physical quantities and measurement techniques".

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Caesium hyperfine frequency Δν_Cs = 9 192 631 770 Hz (exact). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- c = 299 792 458 m/s (exact). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- h = 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (exact). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- e = 1.602 176 634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (exact; defines the ampere). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- k = 1.380 649 × 10⁻²³ J/K (exact; defines the kelvin). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- The notes write the kilogram as 1 kg = 1.4755214 × 10⁴⁰ · hΔν_Cs/c². Check: `python3 -I -c 'h=6.62607015e-34;d=9192631770;c=299792458;print(1.4755214e40*h*d/c**2)'` → 1.00000000018. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf

  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf

- Percent uncertainty = δA/A × 100%. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units

## 0.3 Powers of ten

**Concept sequence**
1. Physics spans an immense range of sizes, from a proton (~10⁻¹⁵ m) to the edge of the known universe (~10²⁶ m). OS Physics Table 1.3 lists benchmark lengths, masses and times. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
2. Metric prefixes step in powers of ten (kilo 10³, centi 10⁻², milli 10⁻³, micro 10⁻⁶, nano 10⁻⁹ …), so converting means moving the decimal point. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
3. Scientific notation, x × 10ʸ, writes huge and tiny numbers compactly. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
4. The order of magnitude is the power of ten. Numbers with the same power (800 and 450, both ~10²) have the same order of magnitude. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-2-physical-quantities-and-units
5. On a log scale, each mark is ten times the one before. That lets one axis cover a huge range; the Richter scale is an example. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units
6. Counting gives way to estimating when there are too many to count, as with the grains of sand in a bucket. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf

## 0.4 Guess like a physicist

**Concept sequence**
1. Physicists often need a rough figure ("guesstimate"). Making one takes practice, some knowledge of units, and willingness to take a risk. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-4-approximation
2. These are named Fermi problems, after Enrico Fermi's "back of the envelope" estimates. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
3. Method: (1) pick quantities you can estimate or calculate; (2) find a relationship between them and the target. Check the dimensions. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
4. Many routes can lead to a reasonable answer, and the answer is only as good as the facts you start from. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
5. Afterwards, compare the estimate with your first gut guess. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-4-approximation

**Definitions**
- *Fermi problem:* an order-of-magnitude estimate of a quantity that is hard to count or measure exactly. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- *Approximation:* an estimate from inputs known only roughly. Its use is to rule out unrealistic numbers. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/1-4-approximation

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- mass = density × volume, with water ≈ 1.0 g/cm³ as the reference density. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf
- Volume of a thin shell over a sphere ≈ 4πR²d (valid when d ≪ R). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

## Case: The shadow at Syene

**Concept sequence** (all from https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy)
1. The Sun is so far away (150 million km) that its rays reaching Earth are essentially parallel. If everyone on Earth pointed at the Sun, their fingers would be parallel.
2. Eratosthenes (276–194 BCE), a Greek living in Alexandria, was told that at noon on the first day of summer, sunlight reached the bottom of a vertical well at Syene (near modern Aswan). So the Sun was directly overhead there.
3. At the same time and date in Alexandria, a column cast a shadow. The Sun's rays made an angle with the vertical of about 1/50 of a circle (7°).
4. Parallel rays meeting the ground at different angles mean "straight up" points in different directions in the two cities: the ground is curved.
5. So Alexandria is 1/50 of the circumference north of Syene. The two had been measured 5000 stadia apart, so the circumference is 50 × 5000 = 250,000 stadia.
6. This was the first fairly accurate measurement of Earth's size, made around 200 BCE.

**Definitions**
- *Stadium (stadion):* a Greek unit of length, taken from the length of the racetrack in a stadium. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Angle ÷ 360° = distance ÷ circumference. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
  - `python3 -I -c 'print(360/7.2, round(360/7,2), 50*5000)'` → 50.0 51.43 250000.
  - 1/50 of a circle is exactly **7.2°**; the source's "7°" is rounded.
- Modern circumference used by the source: 40,000 km. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
  - The same figure appears in the 8.01SC notes: the meter was first defined as 1/10,000,000 of the equator-to-pole meridian arc, so the circumference was once 4 × 10⁷ m by definition (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter2.pdf).
  - OS Physics also gives the 1791 definition (https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/1-3-the-language-of-physics-physical-quantities-and-units).
  - This is a lovely bridge from 0.2 to the Case.

**What the source says is uncertain**
- How accurate Eratosthenes was cannot be judged precisely, because it is unclear which Greek stadium he used. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
- With the common Olympic stadium, his result is about 20% too large.
  - The stadion length that implies: `python3 -I -c 'print(40000*1.2/250000*1000)'` → 192 m.
  - https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
- With another reading, a stadium of about 1/6 km, the source says he was "within 1%". https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-2-ancient-astronomy
  - **Source error: the figures given do not support "within 1%".**
  - `python3 -I -c 'print(250000/6, (250000/6/40000-1)*100)'` → 41,667 km, which is 4.2% high.
  - Writers must not repeat "1/6 km, within 1%". Safe wording: "depending on which stadion he used, his answer was somewhere between a few percent and about 20 percent too big."
- Figuring For Yourself Q42 asks what stadium length would make him exactly right for a 12,740 km diameter.
  - `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(math.pi*12740/250000*1000)'` → 160.1 m.
  - https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-figuring-for-yourself

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Q41 variant:* if the angle had been 30°, the circumference would be 12 × 5000 = 60,000 stadia. `python3 -I -c 'print(360/30*5000)'` → 60000.0. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/2-figuring-for-yourself
