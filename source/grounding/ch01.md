## 1.1 Where and when

**Concept sequence**
1. Position only means something relative to a chosen reference frame. Earth is common, but a classroom, a plane or a moving person can be the frame. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
2. A coordinate system with an observer at the origin is a reference frame. Position depends on where the origin is; displacement, velocity and acceleration do not. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter11.pdf
3. Galileo's ship: in a windowless ship moving steadily on calm water, nobody inside can tell whether it moves. Motion only means something relative to a stated frame. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
4. Two frames moving at constant velocity V relative to each other measure different positions and velocities, v′ = v − V, but the **same** acceleration. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/
5. An object at rest in one frame is moving in the other. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/
6. When a train starts gently, it can feel as if the station is moving backwards. At constant velocity there is no physical way to tell which one is "really" moving. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
7. Distance is the length of the path. Displacement is the change in position: a vector, final minus initial, which can be zero for a round trip. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement

**Definitions**
- *Kinematics:* the study of motion without its causes. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
- *Position:* an object's location at a given time, specified relative to a reference frame. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
- *Reference frame:* the coordinate system and observer from which motion is described; the viewpoint, not the background (OS Physics Teacher Support misconception note). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
- *Displacement:* Δd = d_f − d₀, a vector. *Distance:* the path length, a scalar. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement ; notes Ch 4 §4.2.3 https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf
- *Scalar / vector:* magnitude only / magnitude and direction. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
- *IGCSE 0625:* topic 1.2 "Motion". Weight is listed as a vector and mass as a scalar (source/research/school-wording.md). Our extract has no 0625 sentence on reference frames.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- r′ = r − R; v′ = v − V; a′ = a when V is constant (the Galilean transformation). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter11.pdf ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Cyclist:* 3 km west, then 2 km east. Displacement −1 km (taking east as +), distance 5 km, magnitude of displacement 1 km. `python3 -I -c 'print(-3+2, 3+2)'` → -1 5. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement
- *Walking in a local train* (all speeds **ASSUMED**):
  - Train 80 km/h relative to the track; Kym walks at 1.4 m/s relative to the train (1.40 m/s is OS Physics's own walking speed, https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity).
  - `python3 -I -c 'print(round(80/3.6,1), round(80/3.6+1.4,1), round(80/3.6-1.4,1))'` → 22.2 23.6 20.8 m/s relative to the platform (walking forward / backward), by v = v′ + V (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter11.pdf).
  - The local-train speed needs a source (Western Railway) before gating.

**Concept questions**
- You stand on a platform as a train passes. How do your description and a passenger's compare? (Each sees the other moving past.) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement

**Warnings the sources give**
- Students may think a reference frame is the *background* rather than the viewpoint of the observer. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement (Teacher Support, "Misconception")
- Do not assume the initial position is always zero; motion can start anywhere relative to the origin. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement (Teacher Support)
- The frames in this chapter are inertial (not accelerating). Accelerating frames behave differently. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement (Teacher Support); https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-4-non-inertial-reference-frames/

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The ground is the real frame; the train's view is an illusion" is false. All inertial frames are equally valid (https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-1-relative-motion-distance-and-displacement Teacher Support; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/).
- "You can never tell if you're moving" is false *for a braking or accelerating train*. You can feel acceleration; you cannot feel steady velocity. The 8.01SC claim is only for constant velocity (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf). Keep "steady" in every sentence.
- "Everything is relative, even acceleration" is false at this level. Inertial frames agree on acceleration (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/).
- "Displacement is the shortest *path*" is loose. It is the straight-line change in position, with direction; it is not a path you travel.

## 1.2 How fast, and which way

**Concept sequence**
1. Time interval Δt = t_f − t₀. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
2. Average speed is distance ÷ time; it is a scalar. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
3. Velocity is the vector version: average velocity is displacement ÷ time, with a direction. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
4. Average velocity depends on which time interval you pick. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/1-4-average-velocity-in-1d/
5. Shrink the interval and you approach the instantaneous velocity, the velocity at one moment. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/1-5-instantaneous-velocity-in-1d/
6. On a position–time graph, the slope of a straight line is the velocity; the intercept is the starting position. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs
7. For a curved graph, the slope of the tangent at a point is the instantaneous velocity there; a steepening curve means rising velocity. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs

**Definitions**
- *Speed:* how fast an object changes its location; a scalar. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Average velocity:* "Average velocity is displacement divided by the time over which the displacement occurs." https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Instantaneous velocity:* the velocity at a specific instant, the limit of the average velocity as Δt → 0. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/1-5-instantaneous-velocity-in-1d/
- *IGCSE 0625* (topic 1.2) is not quoted word for word in our research file. Cambridge Lower Secondary 0893 Stage 8 has "speed = distance/time" and distance–time graphs (source/research/school-wording.md §2). Use "distance–time graph" in the `:::school` line.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- v_avg = distance/time (speed); v_avg = Δd/Δt (velocity); SI unit m/s. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- d = d₀ + vt for constant velocity, from y = mx + b. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Car:* 150 km in 3.2 h → 47 km/h. `python3 -I -c 'print(150/3.2)'` → 46.875. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Marble:* 5.2 m in 1.8 s → 2.9 m/s. `python3 -I -c 'print(5.2/1.8)'` → 2.888…. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Round trip to the store:* 6 km in 30 min → average speed 12 km/h, average velocity 0. `python3 -I -c 'print(6/0.5)'` → 12.0. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
  - Mumbai recast: an auto-rickshaw round trip to the market. Same numbers, **ASSUMED**.
- *Cassie's walk:* 205 m at 1.40 m/s → 146 s. `python3 -I -c 'print(205/1.40)'` → 146.4. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Student:* 304 m north in 180 s → 1.7 m/s north. `python3 -I -c 'print(304/180)'` → 1.69. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- *Cricket recast* (**ASSUMED**): a 22-yard pitch is 20.1 m. If a ball crosses it in 0.5 s, its average speed is `python3 -I -c 'print(20.1168/0.5*3.6)'` → 144.8 km/h. Bowling speeds need a source before printing "fast bowler".

**Concept questions**
- Two runners start and finish together but have different velocities at halfway. Can their average velocities be equal? (Yes; average velocity depends only on the net displacement.) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity (CYU 13)

**Warnings the sources give**
- Average speed is *not* the average velocity with the direction dropped; they differ whenever direction changes. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- Average velocity hides what happened in between: stopping, backing up. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity
- An x–t graph's horizontal axis is *time*, not space. A curved x–t graph is not a curved path. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects ("Misconception Alert!")

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Velocity is just a fancy word for speed" is false. Velocity includes direction, and a round trip has zero average velocity at non-zero average speed (https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-2-speed-and-velocity).
- "Speed can be negative" is false. Speed is a magnitude; only a velocity *component* carries a sign (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-conceptual-questions Q4).
- "The graph shows the path" is false (see the warning above). The graph-walker widget must label its x-axis "time".
- "If two things are at the same place at the same time, they have the same speed" is false. Held by over 30% of n=478 (source/research/misconceptions.md §2). On an x–t graph, crossing lines mean the same position, not the same slope.

## 1.3 Speeding up and turning

**Concept sequence**
1. We can *see* velocity. Acceleration is harder to see and more abstract. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/2-1-introduction-to-acceleration/
2. Average acceleration is the change in velocity ÷ the time interval. Instantaneous acceleration is its limit, the slope of the v–t graph. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/2-2-acceleration-in-1d/ ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration
3. Velocity is a vector, so it changes if its size changes *or* its direction changes. A car turning a corner at constant speed is accelerating. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-4-acceleration ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration
4. Acceleration points in the direction of the *change* in velocity, which is not always the direction of motion. When something slows, its acceleration is opposite to its velocity. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-4-acceleration
5. The sign of an acceleration depends on the chosen axis. "Negative" is not the same as "slowing down". https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-4-acceleration

**Definitions**
- *Acceleration:* "Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by a period of time during which the change occurs." https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration ; SI unit m/s² https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf
- *Deceleration:* acceleration opposite to the velocity; it always reduces speed. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-4-acceleration
- *Negative acceleration:* acceleration in the negative direction of the chosen axis. It may or may not be deceleration. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-4-acceleration
- *IGCSE 0625:* "a resultant force may change the velocity of an object by changing its direction of motion or its speed" (source/research/school-wording.md). That is the Chapter 2–3 link.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- ā = Δv/Δt = (v_f − v₀)/(t_f − t₀). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration
- v = v₀ + at for constant acceleration. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Subway train speeding up:* 0 to 30.0 km/h in 20.0 s → +0.417 m/s². *Slowing:* 30.0 km/h to 0 in 8.00 s → −1.04 m/s².
  - `python3 -I -c 'print(30/3.6, 30/3.6/20, -30/3.6/8)'` → 8.333 0.4167 -1.0417.
  - https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-1-acceleration
  - Mumbai recast: a local train leaving Dadar. The inputs (0 to 60 km/h in 40 s) are **ASSUMED**; they happen to give the same 0.417 m/s². `python3 -I -c 'print(60/3.6/40)'` → 0.4167.

**Demonstrations**

## 1.4 Galileo's ramps

**Concept sequence**
1. For constant acceleration, v is a straight line in time: v = v₀ + at. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf
2. The area under a v–t graph is the displacement. For constant acceleration from rest it is a triangle, so x = ½at². https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf
3. Kinematic equations for constant acceleration; free fall is the standard case of constant acceleration. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs
4. From x ∝ t², the distances covered in successive equal time intervals go 1 : 3 : 5 : 7. This step is our own computation; neither source states the odd-number rule.

**Definitions**
- *Constant (uniform) acceleration:* the average acceleration equals the instantaneous acceleration at every moment. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- x = x₀ + v₀t + ½at². https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter4.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs
- Odd-number rule: `python3 -I -c 'print([n*n-(n-1)**2 for n in range(1,6)])'` → [1, 3, 5, 7, 9]. Computed from x ∝ t², not quoted.
- On a frictionless incline, a = g sin θ (OS College 4.5 skier example: g sin 25° = 4.14 m/s²). `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(9.8*math.sin(math.radians(25)))'` → 4.14. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-5-normal-tension-and-other-examples-of-forces
  - This is *why* a ramp slows the fall: a smaller θ gives a smaller a. A rolling ball also has rotation, which reduces a further; that is not covered at this level.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Ramp marks* (all inputs **ASSUMED**): a ramp angle of 3° gives a = 9.8 × sin 3°, for a sliding object. Positions at 1, 2, 3, 4 s:
  - `python3 -I -c 'import math;a=9.8*math.sin(math.radians(3));print([round(0.5*a*t*t,2) for t in (1,2,3,4)])'` → [0.26, 1.03, 2.31, 4.1] m.
  - These are frictionless and non-rolling, and a real rolling ball is slower. Use them only to show the *ratio* 1:4:9:16, not as a prediction.

**Demonstrations**
- *OS Physics 2.3 Snap Lab "Graphing Motion":* a ball off a book ramp, timed at marked distances. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/2-3-position-vs-time-graphs
  - Mumbai feasible: a plank or a large hardcover book on a stack of textbooks, with masking tape marks.
  - To show 1:4:9, place tape marks at 10, 40, 90 cm and listen for even beats. That design is ours and needs the will-it-work gate.

**Concept questions**
- Which four variables appear in the kinematic equations? https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs (CYU 10)
- A body starts from rest at 4 m/s² for 2 s. Is its final velocity 8 or 16 m/s? https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs (CYU 12)

**Warnings the sources give**
- The kinematic equations hold only for *constant* acceleration. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Galileo timed falling balls" is false. Falling was too fast, so he slowed it with ramps (source/research/history.md, Padua c. 1604, TRUE for ramps).
- "Galileo sang to keep time and moved frets until the clicks were even" is **HEDGE** (Stillman Drake's interpretation; source/research/history.md). Tell it as "one historian thinks…".
- "Distance grows steadily with time on a ramp" is false. Distance grows as time²; it is the *velocity* that grows steadily.
- "The ball on the ramp accelerates at g" is false. It accelerates at less than g sin θ (rolling).

## 1.5 The top of a throw

**Concept sequence**
1. 8.01SC poses exactly this question: throw a marker up, and at its highest point, is its velocity zero? Is its acceleration zero? https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/2-1-introduction-to-acceleration/
2. Its answer runs through forces. Gravity was not switched off at the top, so the force is non-zero and, by F = ma, the acceleration is non-zero throughout the flight. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/2-1-introduction-to-acceleration/
3. Kinematics view: for a rock thrown up at 13.0 m/s, the velocity is zero at 1.33 s, but the acceleration is −9.80 m/s² the whole trip, going up, at the top and coming down. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
4. Velocity changes linearly with time, so the v–t graph is a straight line crossing zero, and the acceleration graph is flat. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects (Fig 2.39 caption)
5. Free fall covers upward motion as well as downward. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects

**Definitions**
- *Free fall:* motion under gravity alone, once the object has left whatever threw it, with air resistance neglected. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/2-1-introduction-to-acceleration/
- *g:* acceleration due to gravity; 9.80 m/s² is used as the average; it points down. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *IGCSE 0625:* "acceleration of free fall g … is approximately 9.8 m/s²". g is also field strength in N/kg (source/research/school-wording.md).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- g ranges from 9.78 to 9.83 m/s² with latitude, altitude, geology and topography; 9.80 m/s² is the textbook average. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- v = v₀ − gt; y = y₀ + v₀t − ½gt²; v² = v₀² − 2g(y − y₀) (up positive). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Rock at 13.0 m/s:*
  - `python3 -I -c 'g=9.8;print(round(13/g,2),[round(13-g*t,1) for t in (1,2,3)],[round(13*t-0.5*g*t*t,2) for t in (1,2,3)])'` → 1.33 [3.2, -6.6, -16.4] [8.1, 6.4, -5.1].
  - Top at 1.33 s; v = 3.2, −6.6, −16.4 m/s; y = 8.10, 6.40, −5.10 m.
  - https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Kym's 1 m toss* (rise **ASSUMED** 1 m, as in source/research/misconceptions.md §2):
  - v₀ = √(2g·1 m) = 4.43 m/s; time to the top 0.45 s. `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(math.sqrt(2*9.8), math.sqrt(2/9.8))'` → 4.43 0.452.
  - Height every 0.1 s: `python3 -I -c 'import math;v=math.sqrt(19.6);print([round((v*t-4.9*t*t)*100,1) for t in [i/10 for i in range(10)]])'` → [0.0, 39.4, 68.9, 88.7, 98.7, 98.9, 89.2, 69.8, 40.6, 1.5] cm.
  - The step sizes are 39.4, 29.5, 19.8, 10.0, 0.2, −9.7, −19.6, −29.2 cm. They fall by **~9.8 cm every 0.1 s, straight through the top.** That is the visible signature of a non-zero acceleration at the top.
- *Cricket-ball recast:* m = 0.16 kg (**ASSUMED**, the BOOKPLAN figure). Weight = mg = 1.57 N, acting at every instant including the top. `python3 -I -c 'print(0.16*9.8)'` → 1.568.
