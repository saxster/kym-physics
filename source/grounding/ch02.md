## 2.1 Aristotle's world

**Concept sequence**
1. Everyday experience: we make things move by pushing or pulling them, and ordinary language calls that "force". https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
2. Experience also suggests that a moving object slows down and stops unless something keeps it going. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
3. OS Physics admits openly that Newton's first law "may seem to contradict your everyday experience". https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
4. But some motion happens with no push at all: things roll down slopes, or fall when released. Galileo described a force acting on them. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
5. The resolution comes later (2.3–2.5): the "stopping" is caused by an outside force, friction. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia

**Definitions**
- *Force (everyday):* a push or a pull; it has magnitude and direction. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-1-development-of-force-concept
- *IGCSE 0625:* no Aristotle content. This section exists to name Kym's intuition before testing it.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- None.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Prevalence, from research, not OCW or OpenStax: in Halloun & Hestenes (n=478), 65% held impetus-type views, 18% Aristotelian and 17% Newtonian (source/research/misconceptions.md §3).

**Demonstrations**
- *Push a book across the table, then stop pushing:* the elicit step. The observation that sliding things stop is the source's starting point (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia). Feasible.

**Concept questions**
- Before Galileo and Newton, people thought objects tend to stop on their own, because one force was not yet understood. Which one? (Friction.) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
- Which is correct: (a) net force causes motion, or (b) net force causes *change* in motion? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.3) — the best `:::predict` for this chapter.

**Warnings the sources give**
- Only the first-law "contradicts experience" caveat (above).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **Do not caricature Aristotle.** OS College dismisses the Greek approach with a "nature of the beast" line (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia). BOOKPLAN 2.1 says her intuition is respectable. True minimal version: "Aristotle described what really happens on Earth, where friction and air are everywhere. He was right about the facts and wrong about the cause."
- **The sources themselves slip into Aristotle's view.**
  - OS Physics 4.1: "Force is the cause of motion" (https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-1-force).
  - OS College 4.1: dynamics studies "the forces that cause objects and systems to move" (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-1-development-of-force-concept).
  - These are the very misconception this chapter targets. **Never reuse those sentences.** Write "force changes motion".
- "Aristotle said heavier things fall faster" belongs in Ch 4; do not front-load it here.

**Gaps**
- **Neither source describes Aristotle's actual physics** (natural and violent motion, the role of the medium). Use source/research/history.md (Philoponus entry: Aristotle on falling speed). Anything more needs a history source; cite only, no MacTutor text (licence).

## 2.2 The arrow problem

**Concept sequence**
1. Puzzle: once the arrow leaves the bow, nothing is pushing it, so why does it keep flying? The puzzle comes from the history file, not from OCW or OpenStax (source/research/history.md, Philoponus; Ibn Sina and Buridan).
2. 8.01SC's rule: forces always involve real physical interactions; something must be acting on the object. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/
3. Newton's commentary on his first law, paraphrased: projectiles keep moving except as air resistance slows them and gravity pulls them down. Planets meet less resistance and keep moving far longer. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
4. So the arrow carries no "force inside". It carries motion, which the later "quantity of motion", momentum (Ch 3.5), measures. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf (§7.3)

**Definitions**
- *Quantity of motion (Newton):* measured by velocity and quantity of matter together; today's momentum p = mv. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
- *Impetus (Buridan) / mayl (Ibn Sina):* in source/research/history.md only. A forerunner of momentum, "but not the same idea".

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- None new.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- 65% of 478 US university students held impetus beliefs before instruction and 44% after (source/research/misconceptions.md §3).

**Demonstrations**
- *Throw a paper ball:* ask what pushes it after it leaves the hand. Elicit only; the resolution is 2.3. No source.

**Concept questions**
- A system can have non-zero velocity with zero net external force. Describe one. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.3)
- If a constant non-zero force acts on an object, what can you say about its velocity and acceleration? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.3)

**Warnings the sources give**
- For any force you name, you should be able to name what exerts it (its interaction partner). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-2-newtons-third-law/

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Impetus was just wrong" is too harsh.** history.md calls it a forerunner of momentum. True minimal version: "Buridan was right that a moving thing *carries* something. He was wrong that it's a force, and wrong that it runs out by itself."
- **"Momentum is the force of motion" is false.** Momentum is not a force. Force is the *rate of change* of momentum, F = dp/dt (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/).
- "The air behind the arrow pushes it", Aristotle's idea, is false. Air slows the arrow (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf, Newton's commentary: projectiles are "retarded by the resistance of air").

**Gaps**
- **The Philoponus → Ibn Sina → Buridan chain is in neither OCW nor OpenStax.** Writers rely on source/research/history.md (TRUE, with the caveat that "Latin scholars probably did not read Philoponus directly").
- An arrow demonstration safe for a flat is not in either source.

## 2.3 The ball that never stops

**Concept sequence** (the OpenStax extrapolation argument)
1. A sliding object on a rough floor stops quickly. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
2. Dust the floor with talcum powder and it slides farther. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
3. Oil the floor and it slides farther still. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
4. Extrapolate to no friction and it would slide in a straight line indefinitely. So friction is the cause of the slowing. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
5. Air hockey: air off, the puck soon stops; air on, there is almost no friction and it glides at nearly constant speed. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
6. An isolated body, one with zero net force, keeps a constant velocity, which might be zero. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/

**Definitions**
- *Isolated body:* one on which the net force is zero, cut off from interactions as far as possible. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/
- *IGCSE 0625:* "an object either remains at rest or continues in a straight line at constant speed unless acted on by a resultant force" (source/research/school-wording.md).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Coefficients of kinetic friction (OS College Table 5.1, https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction):

| Surfaces | μ_k |
|---|---|
| Steel on ice | 0.02 |
| Ice on ice | 0.03 |
| Steel on steel, oiled | 0.03 |
| Wood on wood | 0.3 |
| Rubber on dry concrete | 0.7 |

## 2.4 Newton's first law

**Concept sequence**
1. Statement: a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion keeps a constant velocity (straight line, constant speed), unless a net external force acts. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
2. Newton's own Law 1, in the Motte–Cajori translation: every body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless forces impressed on it compel a change. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
3. The idea was first grasped by Galileo; Newton did not acknowledge Galileo's contribution. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter7.pdf
4. Inertia is the tendency to stay at rest or keep moving in a straight line at constant speed. The law is called the law of inertia. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
5. Mass measures inertia: more mass makes motion harder to change (boulder versus basketball). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
6. The law holds in *inertial* frames. Another statement of the first law is that such frames exist. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/
7. In an accelerating frame (a braking train), objects seem to be pushed by a "fictitious force", −mA. No physical interaction is behind it; it is an artefact of the accelerating frame. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-4-non-inertial-reference-frames/

**Definitions**
- *Inertia:* the tendency of an object at rest to stay at rest, or of a moving object to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
- *Mass:* the amount of matter in an object, set by the number and types of its atoms. It is the same on Earth, in orbit or on the Moon. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
- *Inertial frame:* one in which an isolated body moves at constant velocity. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-3-reference-frames/
- *Fictitious force:* an apparent force, −mA, that acts on everything in an accelerating frame and matches no physical interaction. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-4-non-inertial-reference-frames/
- *IGCSE 0625:* "an object either remains at rest or continues in a straight line at constant speed unless acted on by a resultant force". Mass is "a measure of the quantity of matter in an object at rest relative to the observer" (source/research/school-wording.md). The bridge: resultant = net = unbalanced force.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- F_net = 0 ⇔ constant velocity (ΣF = 0). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Box pushed at constant velocity with +50 N:* friction must be −50 N. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
  - Mumbai recast: pushing a loaded steel trunk across a tiled floor at a steady pace. The force needs measuring (luggage scale), so it is **ASSUMED** until she does.
- *Kilogram of cotton vs kilogram of gold:* equal mass; they differ in volume and density. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
  - Recast: 1 kg of puffed rice (kurmura) vs 1 kg of steel bolts.

**Demonstrations**
- *Braking local train* (elicit: "what pushed them?"):
  - In the train's frame, passengers lurch forward under a "fictitious force". In the platform frame, the train slows and the passengers simply keep going.
  - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-4-non-inertial-reference-frames/
  - Thought experiment only on a real train; at home, use a toy on a tray that is jerked to a stop.
- *Two carts, one loaded* (OS Physics Teacher Support): which needs more force to start, and which keeps going longer? https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia — Feasible with two toy cars, one loaded with ₹5 coins.

**Concept questions**
- What does Newton's first law state? (Distractor: "constant *acceleration*".) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia (CYU 5)
- A sliding object slows and stops. Why, if the first law says it should keep going? https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia (CYU 6)
- What is inertia? (Distractor: "tendency to maintain its mass".) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia (CYU 7)
- How are inertia and mass related? Which of weight and mass is intrinsic? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.2)
- Taking off in a jet, you feel pushed back into the seat. Is there really a backward force on you? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.4) — the mirror image of the braking-train lurch.
- When an elevator going up stops suddenly, can a passenger's hat fly off? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/4-conceptual-questions (section 4.7)

**Warnings the sources give**
- Weight and mass are used interchangeably in everyday speech; that is incorrect. Weight is a force. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia
- Not every useful coordinate system is inertial. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-1-newtons-first-and-second-laws/

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Inertia is a force that keeps things moving" is false.** Inertia is a property, measured by mass; it is not a push (https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia). Movability test (BOOKPLAN §2): "inertia made it keep going" explains nothing unless you say *nothing stopped it*.
- **"The braking train throws you forward" is false in the platform frame.** No forward force acts on you; your feet are slowed by friction with the floor and your upper body carries on (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/4-4-non-inertial-reference-frames/). Name it "fictitious force" only if you also say it is not a real interaction.
- **"Things at rest have no inertia" is false.** The law covers rest and motion alike.
- **"A body in motion tends to stay in motion"** (OS Physics wording) can be read as "tends, but eventually stops". Write "keeps the same velocity *exactly*, unless a net force acts".

- **The coin-on-card-on-glass trick is in neither source.** It needs a will-it-work gate (flick speed; card stiffness). Physics justification: the 2.5 friction and Case impulse numbers.
- Indian coin masses are in no source.

## 2.5 Friction is a force

**Concept sequence**
1. Push something at constant speed across a surface: the surface must push back along it, spread over the contact. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-1-contact-forces/
2. The surface's total contact force has two parts: perpendicular (the normal force) and along the surface (friction). These are two components of one contact force. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
3. Kinetic friction:
   - it is roughly proportional to the normal force, f_k = μ_k N;
   - it is roughly independent of the apparent contact area;
   - it is roughly independent of speed, for ordinary speeds.
   - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-1-contact-forces/
4. History of those rules: da Vinci recorded them between 1493–4 and about 1515; Amontons rediscovered them and published in 1699; Coulomb found the speed independence. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
5. Static friction:
   - it is zero if you don't push;
   - it grows to match your push, up to a maximum μ_s N;
   - past that, the object slips, and friction drops to the slightly smaller kinetic value.
   - https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-2-static-friction-lesson/
6. Why the area doesn't matter: the *real* contact area (the touching high points) differs from the apparent area. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/dd-1-1-friction-at-the-nanoscale/
7. Surfaces are rough close up, and part of friction is molecules sticking (adhesion), so even perfectly smooth surfaces are not friction-free. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction
8. Friction "also allows us to move" (walking on ice). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction

**Definitions**
- *Friction:* "Friction is a force that opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact." https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction
- *Kinetic friction:* friction between surfaces sliding over each other. *Static friction:* friction between surfaces not sliding, which responds to the push. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction
- *Normal force:* the perpendicular part of the contact force. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- *IGCSE 0625:* "solid friction as the force between two surfaces that may impede motion and produce heating" (source/research/school-wording.md). The bridge: "may impede"; it is not always against the direction you are going (walking).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- f_k = μ_k N; 0 ≤ f_s ≤ μ_s N; μ_s > μ_k (usually). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction
- OS College Table 5.1 (μ_s / μ_k), https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction:

| Surfaces | μ_s | μ_k |
|---|---|---|
| Rubber on dry concrete | 1.0 | 0.7 |
| Rubber on wet concrete | 0.7 | 0.5 |
| Wood on wood | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| Steel on steel, dry | 0.6 | 0.3 |
| Steel on steel, oiled | 0.05 | 0.03 |
| Shoes on wood | 0.9 | 0.7 |
| Shoes on ice | 0.1 | 0.05 |
| Ice on ice | 0.1 | 0.03 |
| Steel on ice | 0.04 | 0.02 |

  - The source notes that the one- or two-digit values reflect how approximate the friction "laws" are.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Coin-on-book (OS College take-home):* tilt a book until a coin slides at constant velocity; then μ_k = tan θ. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction
  - `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.tan(math.radians(15)),2), round(math.tan(math.radians(20)),2))'` → 0.27 at 15°, 0.36 at 20°.
  - The angles are **ASSUMED**; Kym measures them with a phone inclinometer.
  - Mumbai recast: a ₹5 coin on a school atlas.
- *Ice cube, thali vs cloth:* see 2.3 (2.55 m vs 0.17 m for v₀ = 1 m/s, with ASSUMED inputs).

**Demonstrations**
- *Hand on table* (8.01SC notes §8.3.1): press down and the table pushes back up; slide forward and you feel friction backward; push down *and* forward. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf — Feasible, needs no equipment.
- *Static friction ramp-up* (8.01SC 6.2 simulation): pull gently with a luggage scale; the reading climbs and climbs, then the object suddenly slips and the reading drops. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-2-static-friction-lesson/ — Feasible with a luggage scale and a bag of atta on the floor.
- *Same mass, different area:* a brick-shaped box on its big face vs its narrow face needs the same force at constant speed. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-1-contact-forces/ — Feasible with a book on its cover vs its spine, using the luggage scale. Expect only rough agreement (the source says "approximate").
- *Ice cube on a wet steel thali vs on cloth:* source/research/misconceptions.md §3. Physics from Table 5.1. Feasible.

**Concept questions**
- Define normal force. How does it relate to friction when friction behaves simply? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-conceptual-questions (Q1)
- Why must you ease off the brake as a car comes to a stop? (Static vs kinetic.) https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-conceptual-questions (Q3)
- Why does chalk screech? (Stick–slip, because kinetic < static.) https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-conceptual-questions (Q4); also noted in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf (chalk, fingernails, violin bow)
- Can the glue on tape be simple friction, given that tape sticks to ceilings? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-conceptual-questions (Q2)

**Warnings the sources give**
- Friction is complicated and "still not completely understood"; we lean on observation. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction ; 8.01SC 6.1 says there is still no very good theoretical model https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-1-contact-forces/
- The direction of static friction is not always obvious; it depends on the other constraints. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-2-static-friction-lesson/

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Friction always acts against the direction you move" is false.** When you walk, static friction on your shoe points *forward*. Friction opposes *relative sliding* of the surfaces (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction "also allows us to move"; IGCSE "may impede").
- **"f = μN always"** (OS Physics 4.2 writes f = μN without the inequality, https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/4-2-newtons-first-law-of-motion-inertia) is false for static friction, which is anything from 0 up to μ_s N. A book resting on a level table has *zero* friction.
- **"Friction destroys energy"** is said in the 8.01SC Deep Dive lecture (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/dd-1-1-friction-at-the-nanoscale/). It is false as physics: friction transfers energy to the internal (thermal) store. IGCSE says "produce heating". **Never copy "destroys energy".**
- **"μ is always less than 1"** is false. OS College says "usually between 0 and 1.0" (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction), and its own table has rubber on dry concrete at μ_s = 1.0.
- **"Smooth surfaces have no friction"** is false: adhesion (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-1-friction).
- **"Friction depends on area"** is false *to a first approximation* (https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-2-newtons-laws/6-1-contact-forces/). Keep "roughly".

**Gaps**
- μ for ice on cloth, steel thali on tile, or a coin on a book cover is in no source. Kym measures them; the values are ASSUMED until she does.
- MOSART's "friction not recognised as a force" (grades 9–12) is from source/research/misconceptions.md §3, not OCW or OpenStax.

## Case: The tablecloth trick

**Concept sequence** (assembled from sources; the trick itself is in none)
1. While the cloth slides under the plate, the only horizontal force on the plate is kinetic friction, μ_k N = μ_k mg. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
2. The plate's change of velocity is set by force × time (impulse = change in momentum). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-5-momentum-and-impulse/15-1-momentum-and-impulse/
3. A quick pull makes the time short, so the plate gains little speed and moves only a little. Its inertia (mass) does not decide this, because the friction force is itself ∝ m; what matters is the *short time* and a *small μ*.

**Worked numbers we can reuse** (all inputs **ASSUMED**)
- μ_k = 0.3 (cloth on ceramic; no source), pull time 0.1 s:
  - plate speed = μ g t; slip distance = ½ μ g t².
  - `python3 -I -c 'print(0.3*9.8*0.1, 0.5*0.3*9.8*0.01*100)'` → 0.294 m/s and 1.47 cm. The plate then slides on the bare table and stops.
- Halve the time to 0.05 s and the slip drops to a quarter: `python3 -I -c 'print(0.5*0.3*9.8*0.05**2*100)'` → 0.37 cm.

**Fidelity traps**
- **"Heavy plates work better because they have more inertia" is false at this level.** The friction force grows with m too, so the acceleration μg does not depend on mass (from f_k = μ_k N, https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf).
  - The speed and smoothness of the pull, and a slippery cloth, are what matter. This is computed, so writers must not tell the popular "heavy is better" version.
- "Inertia holds the plate still" is the movability-test failure. Say "the cloth pulls on the plate for so short a time that the plate barely gets going".

**Gaps**
- Neither OCW nor OpenStax describes the tablecloth trick or gives μ for cloth.
- **Safety gate:** use plastic plates and a towel.
