## 4.1 Heavier falls faster?

**Concept sequence**
1. Start with the surprise. If air resistance and friction are negligible, all objects at one place fall toward Earth's centre with the same constant acceleration, whatever their mass. It is unexpected because air resistance usually makes light things fall slower. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
2. In real air, a lighter object of the same size can fall slower: a tennis ball lands after a baseball dropped with it, though the gap is hard to see over a short drop. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
3. "Free fall" is defined as falling with no air resistance or friction. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
4. The acceleration is given the symbol g. It varies a little from place to place; the books use 9.80 m/s². https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
5. Free fall includes going up. A rock thrown up has the same downward acceleration on the way up, at the top (where its velocity is momentarily zero) and on the way down. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
6. Newton's law of gravitation explains the observation: the weight force is proportional to mass, so mass cancels and the acceleration is the same for every object. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-5-newtons-universal-law-of-gravitation ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf
7. The 8.01SC notes say this confirms "Galileo's Law of Free Falling Bodies", and that the claim rests on the model force law; if later measurements showed a non-constant acceleration, the model would have to change. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf

**Definitions**
- *Free fall:* motion of an object falling without air resistance or friction. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Acceleration due to gravity (g):* the acceleration of free-falling objects, directed toward Earth's centre; its direction defines "vertical". https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Weight:* the magnitude of the gravitational force on an object, mg. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/3-2-representing-acceleration-with-equations-and-graphs
- *IGCSE 0625 wording:* "acceleration of free fall g … is approximately 9.8 m / s²"; g is also defined as gravitational field strength, force per unit mass, in N/kg. Weight is "a gravitational force on an object that has mass". (source/research/school-wording.md §1, quoting https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/697209-2026-2028-syllabus.pdf) Bridge: the same 9.8 can be read as N/kg or m/s².

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- g = 9.80 m/s² (average used in OS College); it ranges from 9.78 to 9.83 m/s² with latitude, altitude and local geology. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- g from the law of gravitation, g = GM/r². With OS College inputs (6.67×10⁻¹¹, 5.98×10²⁴ kg, 6.38×10⁶ m): `python3 -I -c 'print(round(6.67e-11*5.98e24/6.38e6**2,2))'` → 9.8. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-5-newtons-universal-law-of-gravitation
- Same with the 8.01SC data sheet (G = 6.67384×10⁻¹¹, M = 5.97219×10²⁴ kg, R = 6.371009×10⁶ m): `python3 -I -c 'G=6.67384e-11; M=5.97219e24; R=6.371009e6; g0=G*M/R**2; print(round(g0,3), round(G*M/(R+400e3)**2,3))'` → 9.82 (and 8.694 at 400 km, used in Ch 5). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_data.pdf ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_constants.pdf
- Kinematics for a drop from rest: y = ½gt², v = gt. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Measuring g (OS College worked example "precise g"):* a ball falls 1.0000 m in 0.45173 s. `python3 -I -c 'print(round(2*1.0/0.45173**2,4))'` → 9.801 m/s². https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Mumbai version, 1 m drop time:* `python3 -I -c 'print(round((2*1.0/9.80)**0.5,4))'` → 0.4518 s (the phyphox acoustic-stopwatch target in misconceptions.md, "≈ 0.45 s", is consistent). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Ice off a glacier (OS College worked example):* 30.0 m drop. `python3 -I -c 'print(round((2*30.0/9.80)**0.5,3))'` → 2.474 s ("about 2.5 seconds"). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- *Rock thrown up at 13.0 m/s (OS College Example 2.14):* `python3 -I -c 'print(round(13*1-0.5*9.8*1**2,2), round(13-9.8*1,2), round(13/9.8,3))'` → 8.1 m and 3.2 m/s after 1 s; top reached at 1.327 s, where v = 0 but a = −9.80 m/s². https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects

**Warnings the sources give**
- OS College "Misconception Alert": a position-vs-time graph of a rock thrown straight up looks like a projectile's path, but the horizontal axis is time; the rock goes straight up and straight down. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- The sign of g depends on which way you call positive; it is not "negative gravity". https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Heavy and light things always fall together." FALSE in air. True minimal version: *with air out of the way*, they speed up at the same rate; in air, a light object can fall slower than a heavy one of the same size. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- "At the top of a throw nothing is happening / acceleration is zero." FALSE. Speed is zero for an instant; the acceleration is still g downward. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- "g is the same everywhere." FALSE. It varies from about 9.78 to 9.83 m/s² across Earth, and it is different on the Moon. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/2-7-falling-objects
- "Galileo dropped balls off the Leaning Tower." Do not state as fact. OS College 5.2 itself asks students to imagine how "Galileo is said to have" timed a Pisa drop (https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces); source/research/history.md marks Pisa as LEGEND and Stevin at Delft, 1586, as TRUE.

**Gaps**
- Neither source has Stevin, the Delft tower or Aristotle's theory of falling. Use source/research/history.md (Delft, 1586; Pisa legend).
- Neither source gives a local value of g for Mumbai. Use 9.8 m/s² and say it varies slightly.

## 4.2 Air gets in the way

**Concept sequence**
1. Drag is a force on an object moving through a fluid (air or water). Like friction it opposes the motion, but unlike simple friction it grows with speed. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces
2. You can feel it: move your hand faster through water and it is harder; turn the hand edge-on and the drag drops, because less area faces the motion. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces
3. The force depends on the object's speed, size and shape, and on the fluid's density, viscosity and compressibility. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
4. For large objects not moving too slowly (cyclists, cars, balls), drag is roughly proportional to speed squared. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
5. A falling object: weight stays the same, drag grows as it speeds up, and when drag equals weight the net force is zero, so the speed stops changing. That steady speed is the terminal velocity. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
6. At release the drag is zero and the acceleration is greatest; the acceleration shrinks to zero as terminal velocity is approached. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
7. Bigger mass for the same shape means a higher terminal velocity; bigger area means a lower one (skydiver head-first vs spread-eagle). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces
8. Very small, slow objects (dust, pollen, bacteria) feel a drag proportional to speed, not speed squared (Stokes' law). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf

**Definitions**
- *Drag force:* the resistive force on a solid moving through a fluid, opposing its motion. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- *Drag coefficient C:* a dimensionless number for the object's shape, found by experiment (usually in a wind tunnel). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces
- *Terminal velocity:* the constant speed reached when drag balances weight and the net force is zero. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces
- *IGCSE 0625:* 1.5.1 covers "air resistance" only as a resistive force; terminal velocity wording was not captured in source/research/school-wording.md. The general rule there: an object keeps a constant speed in a straight line "unless acted on by a resultant force". (source/research/school-wording.md §1)

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- F_D = ½CρAv² (high speed). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- Terminal velocity v_t = √(2mg/(ρCA)). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- Drag coefficients: sphere 0.47, cube 1.05, streamlined body 0.04 (8.01SC notes Table 8.1). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- Air density: 1.21 kg/m³ (OS College 5.2 example), 1.225 kg/m³ (8.01SC notes Table 8.3), 1.20 kg/m³ at sea level (8.01SC notes Table 27.1). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/5-2-drag-forces ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
- Stokes' law for a small sphere: F = 6πηrv; viscosity of air 1.81×10⁻⁵ kg/(m·s) at 15 °C. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
- **Raindrop terminal speed (the number BOOKPLAN asks for):**
  - 8.01SC notes Table 8.3 (C = 0.5, ρ = 1.225 kg/m³): raindrop of mass 4×10⁻⁶ kg and area 3×10⁻⁶ m² → 6.5 m/s; hailstone 20 m/s; osprey 14 m/s; human 60 m/s. Recomputed: `python3 -I -c 'print(round((2*4e-6*9.8/(0.5*3e-6*1.225))**0.5,2), round((2*4e-3*9.8/(0.5*3e-4*1.225))**0.5,1), round((2*1.5*9.8/(0.5*0.25*1.225))**0.5,1), round((2*75*9.8/(0.5*0.6*1.225))**0.5,1))'` → 6.53, 20.7, 13.9, 63.2 (the table rounds). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter8.pdf
  - That raindrop is about 2 mm across: `python3 -I -c 'import math; r=(3*4e-6/1000/(4*math.pi))**(1/3); print(round(r*1000,3),"mm radius", round(2*r*1000,2),"mm diameter", round(math.pi*r*r*1e6,2),"e-6 m^2")'` → 0.985 mm radius, 1.97 mm diameter, area 3.05×10⁻⁶ m² (matches the table's area). Input: water density 1000 kg/m³ from https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-2-density
  - NASA GPM: in still air a raindrop's terminal speed increases with drop size, up to a maximum of about 10 m/s for the largest drops; from 4000 m such a drop takes at least 400 s. https://gpm.nasa.gov/resources/faq/how-fast-do-raindrops-fall — check: `python3 -I -c 'print(4000/10, round(4000/10/60,1))'` → 400.0 s, 6.7 min.

## 4.3 On the Moon

**Concept sequence**

## 4.4 Thrown sideways

**Concept sequence**
1. Projectile motion: after the launch, air resistance is negligible and the only force is gravity. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/3-4-projectile-motion/
2. The key idea: with air resistance ignored, horizontal and vertical motions are independent. A ball launched sideways falls at the same rate as one simply dropped. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion
3. There is no horizontal force, so the horizontal velocity never changes; the vertical acceleration is g. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf
4. So treat the motion as two one-dimensional motions that share only the time. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion
5. The two combine into a curved path (a parabola in the no-air model). Air drag makes the real path shorter and steeper on the way down. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf
6. Payoff: aim straight at a target that is dropped at the instant you fire, and you hit it, whatever the launch speed (Shooting the Apple / Hitting the Bucket). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-1-kinematics/ps-1-2-shooting-the-apple/

**Definitions**
- *Projectile:* an object thrown into the air that, after launch, feels only gravity (air resistance negligible). Its path is its *trajectory*. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion
- *Independence of motions:* horizontal and vertical motions don't influence each other (when air resistance is ignored). https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion
- *IGCSE 0625:* projectile motion as such was not captured in source/research/school-wording.md; the relevant line is that a resultant force may change velocity "by changing its direction of motion or its speed" (school-wording.md §1).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- a_x = 0, a_y = −g; v_x constant. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/5-3-projectile-motion ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf
- 8.01SC notes Fig 5.1: with air drag b = 0.01 N·s²/m², v₀ = 30 m/s and launch angle 21°, the real horizontal distance was 71.7% of the no-air parabola. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter5.pdf

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

## 4.5 Going round

**Concept sequence**
1. In uniform circular motion the speed is constant but the velocity keeps changing direction, so the object is always accelerating. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
2. The change in velocity, and so the acceleration, points toward the centre: centripetal ("centre-seeking") acceleration. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/pages/week-3-circular-motion/
3. By Newton's second law there must be a net force toward the centre. Any force can supply it: string tension, Earth's gravity on the Moon, road friction on tyres, the track pushing a roller-coaster car. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
4. The acceleration is v²/r: four times bigger at 100 km/h than at 50 km/h on the same curve. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
5. If the inward force stops, the object goes off in a straight line along the tangent; nothing throws it outward. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force
6. The outward "flung" feeling in a turning car is your body carrying on straight while the car turns; the car pushes you inward. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force
7. The honest extra (for a box, not the main line): someone who insists on using the turning car or merry-go-round as their frame must add a made-up "centrifugal force" to make Newton's laws work there. It has no physical source; physicists call it fictitious. In a non-rotating (inertial) frame it does not exist. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/931cf35a0f2fbf25b3f0c303ab020c5b_z0xyQKalezI.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force

**Definitions**
- *Uniform circular motion:* travel around a circle at constant speed. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- *Centripetal acceleration:* the acceleration of uniform circular motion, pointing to the centre, size v²/r. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- *Centripetal force:* the name for whatever net force points to the centre; it is a role, not a new kind of force. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- *Inertial frame:* one in which all forces have an identifiable physical origin. *Fictitious force:* a force that appears only because the observer's frame accelerates or rotates; it has no physical origin. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/931cf35a0f2fbf25b3f0c303ab020c5b_z0xyQKalezI.pdf
- *IGCSE 0625:* "motion in a circular path due to a force perpendicular to the motion"; the word "centrifugal" appears 0 times. Bridge sentence (school-wording.md §6): in exams, name the inward force (tension, friction or gravity). source/research/school-wording.md

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- a_c = v²/r = rω²; F_c = mv²/r. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- Doubling speed on the same curve: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(100**2/50**2,1))'` → 4.0. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- In a rotating frame, the centrifugal term is mω²r directed away from the axis (8.01SC notes Ch 31, eq. 1.37 and Example 6). https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter31.pdf

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

- In uniform circular motion, what is the angle between acceleration and velocity? (90°.) https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Centrifugal" is not to be confused with "centripetal". https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force
- 8.01SC confines itself to inertial frames and real forces; rotating frames are for advanced courses. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/931cf35a0f2fbf25b3f0c303ab020c5b_z0xyQKalezI.pdf
- OS College: either view can describe nature, but the inertial-frame view is "the simplest and truest", because every force has a real origin. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Centrifugal force doesn't exist, full stop." Too strong. True minimal version: *there is no outward push from anything; seen from the ground, the thing just carries on straight. People riding along sometimes invent an outward force to explain what they feel, and physicists call that a pretend (fictitious) force.* https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/931cf35a0f2fbf25b3f0c303ab020c5b_z0xyQKalezI.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force
- "When released, it flies outward (away from the centre)." FALSE. It leaves along the tangent, the direction it was moving at that instant. OS College's centrifuge passage says inertia carries particles "away from the center", but in the same paragraph clarifies "along a line tangent to the circle"; keep the tangent wording. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-4-fictitious-forces-and-non-inertial-frames-the-coriolis-force
- "Centripetal force is an extra force on the diagram." FALSE. It is one of the real forces (tension, gravity, friction) doing the inward job. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- "Moving at constant speed means no acceleration." FALSE for circles. https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/6-2-uniform-circular-motion
- "Toilets swirl opposite ways in each hemisphere because of Coriolis." OS College itself says that in most toilets the rotation comes from directional water jets. Avoid. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/6-conceptual-questions
