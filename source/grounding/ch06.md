## 6.1 Pressure

**Concept sequence**
1. The same force can have very different effects depending on the area it is spread over: a finger poke vs a needle with the same force. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure
2. Pressure is force divided by the area perpendicular to the force. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure
3. Unit: the pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Other units: millibar, psi, mm Hg. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
4. Fluids (liquids and gases) flow because they cannot resist sideways (shear) forces; solids can. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-1-what-is-a-fluid ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
5. So a fluid at rest pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches, on all sides (tyre walls, a swimmer). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure
6. The push comes from molecules of the fluid colliding with the surface. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf

**Definitions**
- *Pressure:* force per unit area perpendicular to the force, P = F/A. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure
- *Fluid:* a substance that yields to shearing forces, so it flows; liquids, gases and plasmas. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-1-what-is-a-fluid
- Pressure itself has no direction (a scalar); the forces it produces are perpendicular to surfaces. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure
- *IGCSE 0625:* "pressure as force per unit area… p = F/A"; gas pressure (Supplement) as "forces exerted by particles colliding with surfaces, creating a force per unit area". Cambridge Lower Secondary 0893: "force, exerted by a substance, on an area". source/research/school-wording.md §1, §6

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- P = F/A; 1 Pa = 1 N/m² = 10⁻⁵ bar. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

Any real elephant foot area must be sourced at the gate before print.

**Demonstrations**
- *Finger vs needle (OS College Fig 11.5)* as a thought demo; at home, press a pencil's flat end and then its point gently into a palm or into clay. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure — Feasible (our adaptation).
- *Tyre and swimmer pictures* (OS College Figs 11.6, 11.7) for "pressure acts on all sides". https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure

**Concept questions**
- How is pressure related to the sharpness of a knife? Why does a dull needle hurt more than a sharp one? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-conceptual-questions
- Toe dancing in ballet is much harder on the toes than walking. Explain with pressure. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-conceptual-questions
- How do soft ground and padded shoes reduce the pressure on feet? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-conceptual-questions
- Why is the force from a static fluid always perpendicular to a surface? https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-conceptual-questions

**Warnings the sources give**
- Pressure is a scalar; only the force it produces has a direction. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Pressure is a force." FALSE. It is force *per area*; the same force can give tiny or huge pressure.
- "Pressure pushes downward." FALSE in a fluid: it pushes on every surface, sideways and upward too. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-3-pressure

## 6.2 The ocean of air

**Concept sequence**
1. Air has weight. The air pressure on you at Earth's surface is caused by the weight of the air above you, and it falls as you climb. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
2. On average a column of air 1 cm² in cross-section, from sea level to the top of the atmosphere, has a mass of about 1.03 kg and weighs about 10.1 N. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
3. Pressure varies a little with weather (highs and lows: more or less air overhead). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
4. Air is very compressible, so it is densest near the ground and thins rapidly with height. The atmosphere's average density (to ~120 km) is about 15 times less than at sea level. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
5. A mercury barometer: a tube with a near vacuum at the top, standing in mercury. The air pushes the mercury up until the mercury column's own pressure, hρg, equals atmospheric pressure. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
6. Because mercury is dense, the column is short (about 76 cm); a water column would need to be about 10.3 m. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
7. A barometer can work as an altimeter, because average pressure falls with height. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement

**Definitions**
- *Atmospheric pressure:* the force per unit area on a small surface from the weight of the air above it. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
- *Standard atmosphere:* 1 atm = 1.01325×10⁵ Pa = 1.01325 bar. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
- *Barometer:* a device that measures atmospheric (absolute) pressure. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
- *IGCSE 0625:* ∆p = ρg∆h (topic 1.8). source/research/school-wording.md §1

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- 1 atm = 1.01325×10⁵ Pa (8.01SC notes); OS College rounds to 1.01×10⁵ Pa. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
- 1.0 mm Hg = 133 Pa. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement — check: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(13.6e3*9.80*1e-3,1))'` → 133.3 Pa.
- Densities: mercury 13.6×10³ kg/m³, water 1.000×10³, air 1.29 kg/m³ (0 °C) (OS College Table 11.1); mercury 13.55×10³, air at sea level 1.20 (8.01SC notes Table 27.1). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-2-density ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
- Water is about 775 times as dense as air: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(1000/1.29))'` → 775. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid

## 6.3 Nothing sucks

**Concept sequence**
1. A fluid's pressure comes from molecules hitting a surface; it can only push. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
2. In most cases absolute pressure in a fluid cannot be negative: fluids push rather than pull, so the lowest possible absolute pressure is zero (a vacuum). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
3. Gauges usually read pressure relative to the atmosphere (gauge pressure), so "negative" readings just mean less than atmospheric. The most negative possible gauge pressure is −1 atm. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
4. So whenever something seems "sucked", look for a place where the air pressure is lower on one side; the higher pressure on the other side pushes. Example from OS College: a vacuum-packed jar's rigid walls stop atmospheric pressure reaching the peanuts; open a manometer to it and the outside air pushes the liquid up by hρg. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement

**Definitions**
- *Gauge pressure:* pressure relative to atmospheric pressure; positive above it, negative below it. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
- *Absolute pressure:* gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure, P_abs = P_g + P_atm. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement
- *IGCSE 0625:* the words "suction" and "upthrust" appear 0 times. Bridge: "Nothing 'sucks'. In an exam, explain that the air pressure outside is bigger and pushes." source/research/school-wording.md §1, §6

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- P_abs = P_g + P_atm; tyre example 34 psi gauge → 48.7 psi absolute (336 kPa). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- *Card on an upside-down glass, Mumbai version:* air pushes up on a 10 cm × 10 cm card (**ASSUMED** size) with `python3 -I -c 'print(round(101325*0.1*0.1), round(101325*0.1*0.1/9.81,1))'` → 1013 N, the weight of about 103 kg, far more than the water's weight. (Why the card still needs a seal: water and the card's surface tension details are not covered by the sources; the demo itself is from source/research/misconceptions.md §5.) https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf

## 6.4 Deeper means more

**Concept sequence**
1. Your ears feel it: pressure rises as you dive. Under water you carry the weight of the water above and of the atmosphere above that. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
2. The pressure from the weight of a fluid at depth h is P = hρg. It holds even without a container, because the surrounding fluid supplies the same support. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
3. It depends only on depth and density, not on the width or length of the lake: a dam holding a huge reservoir feels only the pressure set by the water's depth at the dam. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
4. Dams are thicker at the bottom, where the pressure is greater. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
5. Pressures add: at 10.3 m in a pool the total is 2 atm, half from water, half from air. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
6. Pascal's principle: a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is passed on undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the container walls. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-5-pascals-principle
7. A manometer (U-tube) uses hρg to measure pressure; with both sides open, levels are equal whatever the tube widths. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-6-gauge-pressure-absolute-pressure-and-pressure-measurement

- *Pressure due to the weight of a fluid:* P = hρg, ρ the average density, h the depth below the surface. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-4-variation-of-pressure-with-depth-in-a-fluid
- *Pascal's principle (OS College):* "A change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to all portions of the fluid and to the walls of its container." https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-5-pascals-principle

## 6.5 Why ships float

**Concept sequence**
1. Pressure grows with depth, so the water pushes up on the bottom of an object harder than it pushes down on the top. The difference is a net upward force, the buoyant force. Sideways pushes cancel. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
2. Every object in a fluid feels it, whether it floats, sinks or hangs in between (even a sinking anchor is partly supported). https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
3. How big is it? Imagine the object removed and its space filled with fluid. That fluid would just be supported, so the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid the object displaces: Archimedes' principle. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
4. Buoyant force bigger than weight → it rises and floats; smaller → it sinks; equal → it hangs at that depth. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
5. So density decides: an object floats if its *average* density is less than the fluid's. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
6. A steel ship floats because its hull encloses a large volume of air: the water it displaces is mostly displaced by air inside the hull, not by steel. A lump of clay sinks; the same clay as a boat floats. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
7. A floating object sinks until it displaces its own weight of fluid; the fraction submerged equals its average density divided by the fluid's. A loaded ship sits lower; the same ship floats higher in denser (salt) water. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle ; https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-conceptual-questions

**Definitions**
- *Buoyant force:* the net upward force a fluid exerts on any object in it. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
- *Archimedes' principle (OS College):* "The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces." https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
- *Density:* mass per unit volume, ρ = m/V; SI unit kg/m³; water is 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-2-density ; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter27.pdf
- *Specific gravity:* an object's density divided by water's (at 4 °C); less than 1 floats. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
- *Apparent weight:* the reading when submerged; it is lower by the weight of fluid displaced. https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/11-7-archimedes-principle
- *IGCSE 0625:* topic 1.4 Density is in the syllabus; "upthrust" appears 0 times in 0625 (source/research/school-wording.md §1). Use "buoyant force" (OpenStax), and mention that some books say "upthrust".

**Laws, equations, standard values**
