## 8.1 Hot and cold aren't substances

**Concept sequence**
1. Heat and temperature are different things; we feel heat *flow*, not temperature ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy)).
2. Heat is energy that moves *because of* a temperature difference, and it stops when the temperatures are equal ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).
3. Work can produce the same effect as heating: Joule's falling weights churned water and warmed it ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).
4. So an object has internal energy, but no fixed "heat content" ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).

**Definitions**
- Heat: "Heat is the spontaneous transfer of energy due to a temperature difference." ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat))
- Thermal energy (8.21): the total energy from the relative motion and excitations of particles above absolute zero; heat is transferred thermal energy, from high T to low T ([8.21 lec04, slide 3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)).
- **IGCSE 0625 wording:** the topic is "Transfer of thermal energy", and "heating" is a process. The noun "heat" survives only in "specific heat capacity" ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)). Cambridge Lower Secondary 9Pf still says "heat" ([school-wording.md §2](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- 1.000 kcal = 4186 J (the 15 °C calorie); 4184 J is the thermochemical calorie ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).
- A food Calorie is 1 kcal ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Rumford's water boiled after 2.5 h of boring with no fire. That story is from [history.md §4](../research/history.md), citing IOP Spark, not from OpenStax.

**Demonstrations**
- Rub an eraser hard on a desk, then touch it to your skin: it is warm. This is an OpenStax teacher demo of work raising temperature ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy)). It works in a Mumbai flat.
- Joule's paddle apparatus is a schematic only ([CP 14.1, Fig 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)).

**Concept questions**
- Sample A gets 10 kJ of heating and sample B gets 10 kJ of work. Can you tell which was heated? No: both raise internal energy the same way ([CP 14.1 Check Your Understanding](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat)). Seed for `:::predict`.
- When heat flows into a system, is the energy stored *as heat*? ([CP 14 Conceptual Q3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-conceptual-questions))

**Warnings the sources give**
- "A system has a well-defined internal energy, but we cannot say that it has a certain 'heat content'." ([CP 14.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-1-heat))

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- CP 14.1 also says "Heat is a form of energy, whereas temperature is not", which invites "objects contain heat". **True minimal version:** heat is energy *on the move* from hotter to colder. What an object has is internal (thermal) energy.
- "Cold flows in" is false. **True version:** energy flows out of the warmer thing. There is no cold substance (misconceptions.md §7).

## 8.2 Heat vs temperature

**Concept sequence**
1. Temperature is what a thermometer reads. It is linked to how fast the particles jiggle on average ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy)).
2. Energy flows from higher to lower temperature until the temperatures match, and then net flow is zero ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).
3. The energy needed to warm something depends on three things: the temperature change, the mass, and the substance ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).
4. Q = mcΔT. Water has one of the largest specific heats of any material ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).
5. Land and sea heat differently, and that leads on to sea breezes ([CP 14.2 take-home](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).

**Definitions**
- Specific heat: the heat needed to change the temperature of 1.00 kg of a substance by 1.00 °C; unit J/(kg·K) or J/(kg·°C) ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).
- Heat capacity C = mc. It belongs to an object, whereas specific heat belongs to a material ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).
- **IGCSE 0625:** "the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase; c = ∆E/(m∆θ)". IGCSE also says a temperature rise is "an increase in the average kinetic energies of all of the particles" ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values** (CP Table 14.1, at 25 °C unless noted: [CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity))
- Water (15 °C) 4186 J/(kg·°C); aluminium 900; iron/steel 452; glass 840; wood 1700; copper 387; ice (−50 to 0 °C) 2090; human body 3500; dry air cₚ 1015.
- **There is no plastic entry** in either OpenStax table (CP 14.1, PH 11.2).
- 8.21 lists water 4.18 J/(g·K), air 1.01, concrete 0.88, and wood 0.42 ([8.21 lec04 slide 6](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)). **Conflict:** OpenStax gives wood 1700 J/(kg·°C) = 1.70 J/(g·K) *and* 0.4 kcal/(kg·°C). 8.21's "0.42 J/g·K" looks like a calorie value with the wrong unit. **Use OpenStax's 1700.**
- Ratios: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(4186/900,2), round(4186/452,2), round(4186/1700,2), round(4186/840,2))'` → water/Al 4.65, water/steel 9.26, water/wood 2.46, water/glass 4.98.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example (aluminium pan): 0.500 kg Al with 0.250 kg water, warmed by 60 °C. Water takes 62.8 kJ, the pan 27.0 kJ, and the pan's share is 30.1% ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)). `python3 -I -c 'print(0.25*4186*60, 0.5*900*60, round(0.5*900*60/(0.25*4186*60+0.5*900*60)*100,1))'` → 62790, 27000, 30.1.
- **Bucket vs spoonful** (all inputs ASSUMED): a 10 kg bucket at 40 °C and a 5 g spoonful at 90 °C, each cooling to a 30 °C room. `python3 -I -c 'print(10*4186*10, round(0.005*4186*60), round(10*4186*10/(0.005*4186*60)))'` → bucket gives out 418,600 J, spoon 1,256 J. The bucket gives about 333× more energy, even though the spoon is much hotter. The prompt itself ("bucketful of warm water or a spoonful of boiling water") is the OpenStax teacher prompt in [PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy).
- Land vs sea: equal masses get equal energy, and sand (c ≈ 800, from [PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)) warms `python3 -I -c 'print(round(4186/800,2))'` → 5.23× more than water.

**Demonstrations**
- "Temperature change of land and water": jars of sand and water heated equally, then timed as they cool. OpenStax says this "replicates the phenomena responsible for land breezes and sea breezes" ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity); also a Snap Lab in [PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)). Mumbai-flat version: put two steel tumblers, one of sand and one of water, in May sun on a windowsill and read them with a kitchen thermometer. This is feasible.
- PhET "Energy Forms and Changes" (heat iron, brick and water) is linked from [PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer). Link out to it; don't embed.

**Concept questions**
- 25 kJ warms a block from 25 to 30 °C. How much takes it from 45 to 50 °C? The same 25 kJ ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).
- As winter sets in, does a field or a pond reach 0 °C first? ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer))
- What three factors set the heat needed for a temperature change? ([CP 14 Conceptual Q4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-conceptual-questions))

**Warnings the sources give**
- "It is tempting to say that temperature measures heat, but this is not strictly true." ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy))
- Teacher note: "Dispel any notions that heat content is solely dependent on temperature." ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy))
- Wind chill "can never cool an object to a temperature below the ambient temperature" ([PH 11.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-1-temperature-and-thermal-energy)). This links to 8.6: evaporation *can* go below ambient; wind alone cannot.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- PH 11.1 says "thermal energy … is the average kinetic energy of the particles". That is wrong as written, because an average doesn't grow with the amount of stuff. **True minimal version:** temperature goes with the *average* jiggle of each particle; thermal energy is the *total* over all the particles. That is why the bucket beats the spoon.
- PH 11.1 says absolute zero is "theoretically the point at which there is no molecular motion". IGCSE says particles have the "least kinetic energy" there, not none ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). **Use "least"**, or avoid the claim.
- The typo in source CP Example 14.1, "27.0 × 10⁴ J = 27.0 kJ", should read 10³. PH 11.2 has it right. Don't copy it.

**Gaps**
- Plastic's specific heat isn't in OpenStax or 8.21. If a section needs a plastic figure, a gate must source one.

## 8.3 Why metal feels colder

**Concept sequence**
1. The carpet and the tile are at the same temperature, yet the tile feels colder ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
2. What you feel is the *rate* of heat loss from your skin ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
3. Conduction happens through particle collisions at the contact surface, so energy passes from faster to slower particles ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
4. The rate depends on area, temperature difference, thickness and the material's conductivity k: Q/t = kA(T₂−T₁)/d ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
5. Good electrical conductors are good heat conductors, because of their free electrons ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
6. 8.21 states the same idea as Fourier's law q = −k∇T and compares it to electric current ([8.21 lec04 slides 9–10](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)).

**Definitions**
- Conduction: "heat transfer through direct physical contact" ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).
- Thermal conductivity k is a material property, in J/(s·m·°C), which is the same as W/(m·K) ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
- **IGCSE 0625** heading 2.3.1 is "Conduction" ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values** (CP Table 14.3, near 0 °C: [CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction))
- Silver 420; copper 390; **aluminium 220**; steel (iron) 80; **stainless steel 14**; ice 2.2; glass 0.84; water 0.6; **wood 0.08–0.16**; wool 0.04; air 0.023; Styrofoam 0.010 W/(m·K).
- 8.21 gives steel 52, hardwood 0.16, air 0.026 and fibreglass 0.043 W/(m·K) ([8.21 lec04 slide 11](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)). Steel differs from OpenStax's 80 because "steel" covers many alloys. Say "tens of W/(m·K)" if both are used.
- **No solid-plastic k in either source** (Styrofoam is a foam, not a solid plastic).
- Ratios: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(220/14,1), round(220/0.16), round(220/0.08), round(14/0.16), round(14/0.08))'` → Al/stainless 15.7; Al/wood 1375–2750; stainless/wood 88–175.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: an aluminium pan base 0.800 cm thick and 14.0 cm across, boiling off 1.00 g/s. The temperature difference across the base is only 5.33 °C ([CP 14.5 Ex 14.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)). `python3 -I -c 'import math;A=math.pi*0.07**2;print(round(2256*8e-3/(220*A),2))'` → 5.33. Meaning: aluminium moves energy so easily that it needs almost no temperature difference to do it.
- Source example: a Styrofoam ice box (0.950 m², 2.50 cm walls, 35 °C outside) melts 3.44 kg of ice a day ([CP 14.5 Ex 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)). `python3 -I -c 'q=0.010*0.95*35/0.025*86400;print(round(q/334e3,2))'` → 3.44.
- Ice-cube demo energy: a 10 g cube needs `python3 -I -c 'print(0.010*334e3)'` → 3340 J to melt, whichever plate it sits on. The plate only changes *how fast* that energy arrives.

**Demonstrations**
- OpenStax teacher prompt: "place their hand on their desk or on a metal object. Does it feel colder? Why?" ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).
- Ice cube on aluminium vs on plastic: the cube on the "colder-feeling" metal melts faster (IOP Spark, UCSB and PASCO cited in [misconceptions.md §7](../research/misconceptions.md); PASCO's aluminium block melts a cube in under about 4 minutes). OpenStax has no version of this demo. Mumbai-flat version: an aluminium kadhai base or a thick aluminium plate against a plastic chopping board, with ice from the freezer. Feasible. Stainless steel works less well (k = 14 vs 220), so prefer aluminium.

**Concept questions**
- Why is ceramic, which conducts better than an insulator but worse than metal, ideal for a cooktop surface? ([CP 14 Conceptual Q20](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-conceptual-questions))
- How does the conduction rate change if every dimension doubles? It doubles ([CP 14.5 Check Your Understanding](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
- `:::predict` seed: the thali and the board have sat in the same room all night. Which is colder? Neither. Which will melt an ice cube faster? The metal.

**Warnings the sources give**
- "the carpet and tile floor are both at the same temperature" ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction); [PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Metal is colder" is the misconception itself. **True version:** everything in the room is at room temperature. Metal feels colder because it carries energy away from your warmer skin faster.
- "Metal pulls cold into your hand" is false. Energy goes *out of* the hand.
- A PH 11.2 teacher note calls Formica laminate "a good conductor of heat". That is doubtful (laminates are plastics), so don't reuse it.
- Don't compute a melt time by putting a 2 cm cube into Q/t = kAΔT/d. The plate isn't a slab held between two fixed temperatures, so the formula would give a nonsense time. Use the k *ratio* to say "faster", and the measured "under about 4 minutes" (PASCO via misconceptions.md) for a time.
- How hot or cold a touch *feels* also depends on how much energy the material stores (the ρc part), not on k alone. Neither source discusses this. Keep the text to "carries energy away faster"; that is true, and it is enough.

- Plastic's thermal conductivity is unsourced. Wood (0.08–0.16) can stand in as "a poor conductor like plastic" only if the gate approves.
- The ice-cube-on-plates demo isn't in OpenStax or OCW; it comes from the research file only.

## 8.4 Sweaters don't make heat

**Concept sequence**
1. Thick materials slow conduction, and thickness enters as 1/d ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).
2. Air is a very poor conductor (0.023 W/(m·K)), but it can carry heat well by convection if it's free to flow ([CP 14.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection)).
3. Wool, fur, feathers and fibreglass trap air in pockets too small for convection, so they insulate ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction); [CP 14.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection)).
4. Insulation layers add like resistors in series ([8.21 lec04 slide 12](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)).
5. Sweaters "slow down the transfer of heat away from our bodies" ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).

**Definitions**
- R factor = d/k. The larger it is, the better the insulator ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Wool 0.04, down 0.025, air 0.023 W/(m·K) ([CP 14.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-5-conduction)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(220/0.04))'` → aluminium conducts about 5500× better than wool.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- 8.21 building example: a 0.2 m concrete wall loses about 5 GJ/day; with 3 cm of fibreglass sandwiched in wood, about 160 MJ/day ([8.21 lec04 slides 11–12](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec04/)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(1.4*20/0.2*450*86400/1e9,1))'` → 5.4 GJ/day, matching 8.21's "roughly 5 GJ/day".

## 8.5 Hidden heat

**Concept sequence**
1. While ice melts, its temperature doesn't change, even though heat keeps flowing in ([CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
2. The energy goes into pulling the particles apart against their attraction, not into making them move faster ([PH 11.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
3. Q = mL_f for melting and Q = mL_v for boiling. "Latent" means hidden ([CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
4. On a heating curve the flat stretches sit at 0 °C and 100 °C, and the 100 °C stretch is much longer ([CP 14.3, Fig 14.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
5. The same energy comes back out on freezing and condensing. Florida growers spray their orchards so that freezing water protects the fruit ([CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).

**Definitions**
- Latent heat of fusion / vaporisation: the heat needed per kg to change phase (solid–liquid / liquid–gas), in J/kg ([PH 11.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
- 8.21 calls them "enthalpies of phase change": melting ≈ 334 kJ/kg, vaporisation ≈ 2.26 MJ/kg ([8.21 lec10](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec10/)).
- **IGCSE 0625** sub-topic 2.2.3 is "Melting, boiling and evaporation" ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Water: L_f = 334 kJ/kg (79.8 kcal/kg), L_v = 2256 kJ/kg at 100 °C, and 2430 kJ/kg at 37 °C ([CP 14.3 Table 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)). Body-temperature sweat needs 2428 kJ/kg, "about 10 percent higher" than at 100 °C (same page). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(2430/2256,3))'` → 1.077, which is nearer 8% than 10%. Say "almost 10% more".
- Melting a kilogram of ice takes as much energy as warming that kilogram of water from 0 to 79.8 °C: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(334e3/4186,1))'` → 79.8 ([CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
- L_v/L_f = `python3 -I -c 'print(round(2256/334,2))'` → 6.75.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Heating curve for 1 kg of ice from −20 °C to steam: 41.8 kJ (ice) + 334 kJ (melt) + 418.6 kJ (water 0→100 °C) + 2256 kJ (boil), about 3050 kJ in all. `python3 -I -c 'q=[2090*20,334e3,4186*100,2256e3];print([round(x/1e3,1) for x in q],round(sum(q)/1e3))'`. Shape from [CP 14.3 Fig 14.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat).
- Source example: three 6.0 g ice cubes chill 0.25 kg of soda from 20 °C to 13 °C ([CP 14.3 Ex 14.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round((0.25*4186*20-0.018*334e3)/((0.25+0.018)*4186),1))'` → 13.3.
- Steam comparison: the energy that boils 1 kg of water would heat 1 kg of steam by about 1000 K ([8.21 lec10](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-21-the-physics-of-energy-fall-2009/resources/mit8_21s09_lec10/)).

**Demonstrations**

- PH 11.3 says energy "acts to loosen bonds". That's fine, but avoid "bonds break completely in a liquid". A liquid's particles still attract one another (PH 11.3 says so itself).

## 8.6 The matka and the monsoon

**Concept sequence**
1. Water evaporates below its boiling point. The fastest molecules escape, so evaporation needs energy taken from the liquid and its surroundings ([CP 13.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling); [CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)).
2. We cool by evaporating sweat, and high humidity inhibits evaporation ([CP 13.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling)).
3. Relative humidity is the vapour present compared with the most possible at that temperature; at 100% (saturation) net evaporation stops ([CP 13.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling)).
4. Moving air carries away saturated air, so evaporation continues; this is why a fan feels cool ([CP 14.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection)).
5. So an evaporating surface (a matka, wet skin) can cool *below* air temperature. It stops at a floor set by humidity, called the wet-bulb temperature (this step is not in OpenStax; see Gaps).
6. Convection: warm fluid is less dense and rises, which makes loops ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer); [CP 14.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-6-convection)).
7. Sea breeze: land heats faster than sea by day, the air over land rises, and cooler sea air flows in. At night the flow reverses ([NOAA JetStream](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/sea-breeze)).

**Definitions**
- Percent relative humidity = vapour density ÷ saturation vapour density × 100 ([CP 13.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling)).
- Dew point: the temperature at which the air's vapour would reach 100% RH ([CP 13.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling)).
- Convection: "heat transfer by the movement of a fluid", driven by buoyancy ([PH 11.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/11-2-heat-specific-heat-and-heat-transfer)).
- Sea and land breezes: "These two flows are a result of the difference in air density between the land and sea caused by the sun's heating." ([NOAA JetStream](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ocean/sea-breeze))
- Wet-bulb temperature: no OpenStax definition. Working definition for the gate: the lowest temperature evaporation alone can bring a wet surface to in given air. It is computed below with Stull's fit, which is documented by NCAR ([NCL wetbulb_stull](https://www.ncl.ucar.edu/Document/Functions/Contributed/wetbulb_stull.shtml)).
- **IGCSE 0625** sub-topics 2.2.3 "evaporation" and 2.3.2 "Convection". Cambridge Lower Secondary 9Pf lists "evaporative cooling" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Saturation vapour pressure of water: 25 °C 3170 Pa; 30 °C 4240 Pa; 37 °C 6310 Pa; 40 °C 7340 Pa ([CP 13.6 Table 13.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/13-6-humidity-evaporation-and-boiling)).
- L_v at 37 °C = 2430 kJ/kg ([CP 14.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-3-phase-change-and-latent-heat)); dry-air cₚ = 1015 J/(kg·°C) ([CP 14.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/14-2-temperature-change-and-heat-capacity)).
- **Stull (2011) wet-bulb fit**, T in °C and RH in %, at sea-level pressure. Valid for RH 5–99% and T −20 to 50 °C; error −1 to +0.65 °C, mean absolute error < 0.3 °C ([NCL documentation](https://www.ncl.ucar.edu/Document/Functions/Contributed/wetbulb_stull.shtml)). Coefficients as transcribed in the reskit implementation ([reskit docs](https://ethos-reskit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/util/wet_bulb_temperature.html)):
  `Tw = T·atan(0.151977·(RH+8.313659)^0.5) + atan(T+RH) − atan(RH−1.676331) + 0.00391838·RH^1.5·atan(0.023101·RH) − 4.686035`.
  Citation: R. Stull, *J. Appl. Meteor. Climatol.* 50, 2267–2269 (2011), DOI 10.1175/JAMC-D-11-0143.1. The publisher page returned 403, so the coefficients have not been checked against the paper itself. **The gate must check them.**
- **Independent cross-check** built from OpenStax numbers: the energy balance cₚ(T−T_w) = L_v·(w_s(T_w) − w), using the CP Table 13.5 vapour pressures (log-interpolated), cₚ = 1015 and L_v = 2430 kJ/kg. One extra input isn't from OpenStax: 0.622, the water-to-air molar-mass ratio used to turn vapour pressure into a mixing ratio, which is standard but unsourced here (gate). It agrees with Stull to within 0.5 °C in every case below. Run it as `python3 -I wetbulb.py`:
  ```python
  from math import atan, log, exp
  def stull(T, RH):  # Stull 2011, T in C, RH in %, sea-level pressure
      return (T*atan(0.151977*(RH+8.313659)**0.5) + atan(T+RH) - atan(RH-1.676331)
              + 0.00391838*RH**1.5*atan(0.023101*RH) - 4.686035)
  TAB = [(0,610),(5,868),(10,1190),(15,1690),(20,2330),(25,3170),(30,4240),(37,6310),(40,7340),(50,12300)]  # CP Table 13.5, Pa
  def es(T):
      for (t0,p0),(t1,p1) in zip(TAB,TAB[1:]):
          if t0 <= T <= t1: return exp(log(p0)+(log(p1)-log(p0))*(T-t0)/(t1-t0))
  def psychro(T, RH, P=101325, cp=1015, Lv=2430e3):
      w = 0.622*(RH/100*es(T))/(P-RH/100*es(T)); lo, hi = -10.0, T
      for _ in range(80):
          Tw = (lo+hi)/2; f = cp*(T-Tw) - Lv*(0.622*es(Tw)/(P-es(Tw)) - w)
          lo, hi = (Tw, hi) if f > 0 else (lo, Tw)
      return Tw
  for T, RH in [(20,50),(34.3,65),(29.4,81),(38,20)]:
      print(T, RH, round(stull(T,RH),1), round(psychro(T,RH),1), round(T-stull(T,RH),1))
  ```
  Output: `20 50 13.7 13.9 6.3` · `34.3 65 28.7 28.5 5.6` · `29.4 81 26.7 26.7 2.7` · `38 20 21.3 20.8 16.7`.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

- **Mumbai May vs July wet-bulb.** Inputs are ASSUMED typical values: Santacruz 1991–2020 normals from IMD, as tabulated on [Wikipedia "Climate of Mumbai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_of_Mumbai).   - So the answer to the case "May or July?" is May. May has about twice the possible cooling of July, though both are small next to a dry inland summer. A real matka does less than these limits, because heat also leaks in from the room.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The matka is cold because clay is cold" is the misconception ([misconceptions.md §7](../research/misconceptions.md)). **True version:** water seeps through and evaporates from the outside, and the escaping molecules take energy from the pot.
- "Air soaks up water like a sponge until full" is false. **True minimal version:** at a given temperature water can only evaporate until the vapour reaches a certain amount; damp air is already close to that, so evaporation slows.
- "A matka cools water to the wet-bulb temperature" is false. **True version:** wet-bulb is the floor it can approach, and real pots stay above it.
- "Hot air rises because heat rises" is false. **True version:** warmer air is less dense, and the denser air around it pushes it up (buoyancy). PH 11.2 says "Convection is driven by buoyancy".
- "The monsoon is a giant sea breeze" is Halley's 1686 idea. [history.md §9](../research/history.md) records Gadgil et al. (2021) arguing that it fails tests. Present it as an open question, as BOOKPLAN says.
- The CP 14.3 claim that humid-climate air "rarely go[es] above 35.0ºC" isn't a law, and Mumbai has recorded more.

- **Wet-bulb temperature** is not in OpenStax or 8.21. It is grounded here by Stull 2011 via NCL and reskit, plus an OpenStax-only energy balance. The coefficients still need a check against the paper (publisher 403).
