## 9.1 Things that swing

**Concept sequence**
1. Periodic motion repeats at regular intervals. The time for one repeat is the period T, and f = 1/T ([CP 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-2-period-and-frequency-in-oscillations)).
2. When the restoring force is proportional to the displacement (Hooke's law), the motion is simple harmonic, and its period does not depend on amplitude ([CP 16.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-3-simple-harmonic-motion-a-special-periodic-motion)).
3. 8.03 says there is really "NO 'Hooke's Law'". It is the small-oscillation approximation that works for almost any smooth system near a stable equilibrium ([8.03 Lec 1 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec1/)).
4. Pendulum: the restoring force is −mg sinθ, and for small angles sinθ ≈ θ, which gives SHM ([CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)).
5. T = 2π√(L/g). It depends only on length and g, not on mass ([CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)).
6. Real swings lose energy (damping), so a parent has to keep pushing ([CP 16.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-7-damped-harmonic-motion)). This leads into 9.2.

**Definitions**
- Periodic motion: "a motion that repeats itself at regular time intervals" ([CP 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-2-period-and-frequency-in-oscillations)).
- Simple harmonic motion: oscillation in which the net force obeys Hooke's law ([CP 16.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-3-simple-harmonic-motion-a-special-periodic-motion)).
- **IGCSE 0625** has no pendulum formula in the content quoted in [school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md). IB DP Theme C.1 is "Simple harmonic motion" (same file, §4).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- T = 2π√(L/g), valid for amplitudes below about 15° ([CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)).
- Size of the small-angle error: `python3 -I -c 'import math;[print(d, round(100*(math.radians(d)-math.sin(math.radians(d)))/math.sin(math.radians(d)),2)) for d in (5,10,15,30)]'` gives 0.13%, 0.51%, 1.15% and 4.72% at 5°, 10°, 15° and 30°. That matches CP's "about 1% or less" below 15°.
- Exact period vs the small-angle period (computed with the arithmetic–geometric mean for the complete elliptic integral; pure maths, no source needed): T/T₀ = 1.0019 at 10°, 1.0043 at 15°, 1.0174 at 30°, 1.0732 at 60°, 1.1803 at 90°.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: L = 75.000 cm and T = 1.7357 s give g = 9.8281 m/s² ([CP 16.4 Ex 16.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)). `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(4*math.pi**2*0.75/1.7357**2,4))'` → 9.8281.
- A 1 m pendulum: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(2*math.pi*math.sqrt(1/9.8),3))'` → 2.007 s (the "seconds pendulum" ticks once per swing).
- A building-society swing with ASSUMED 2.5 m chains: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(2*math.pi*math.sqrt(2.5/9.8),2))'` → 3.17 s. A rope four times longer swings twice as slowly.

**Demonstrations**
- OpenStax take-home: a 1 m string with a nut or key, released from under 10°, timed over 10 swings to find g ([CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)). Mumbai flat: feasible with a phone stopwatch.
- 8.03 demos: "Physical Pendulum" and "Oscillating Steel Ball on a Track" ([8.03 resource](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy4_of_lecture-2-video/), [8.03 resource](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy2_of_lecture-2-video/)). Describe them; don't embed.
- PhET "Pendulum Lab", linked from [CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum). OpenStax notes "the anharmonic behavior at large amplitude".

**Concept questions**
- Two pendulums of the same length, one with a 10 kg bob and one with 100 kg, both pulled back 12°. How do they differ? They don't ([CP 16.4 Check Your Understanding](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)). Strong `:::predict` seed, since many children expect the heavy one to be faster or slower.
- Move a pendulum clock to a city with slightly larger g. Should you lengthen or shorten the pendulum? ([CP 16 Conceptual Q8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-conceptual-questions))
- If the frequency of an oscillation isn't constant, can it be simple harmonic? ([CP 16 Conceptual Q3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-conceptual-questions))

**Warnings the sources give**
- For precise work the angle must stay below about 0.5° ([CP 16.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-4-the-simple-pendulum)).
- "The law breaks down at some point." ([8.03 Lec 1 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec1/))

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "A pendulum's period never depends on how far you pull it" is false. **True minimal version:** for small swings it hardly depends on it; big swings take a little longer (about 7% longer at 60°).
- "Heavier bobs swing faster" is the misconception. Mass really doesn't matter for an ideal pendulum (CP 16.4).
- The cathedral-lamp story is a legend (BOOKPLAN 9.1; [history.md](../research/history.md)). Don't tell it as fact.

**Gaps**
- The Galileo / Pisa cathedral-lamp history is not in OpenStax or OCW; see history.md and the BOOKPLAN legend list.

## 9.2 Resonance

**Concept sequence**
1. Every oscillator has a natural frequency: how it swings with no driving and no damping ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)).
2. If you drive it slowly it just follows; driven near its natural frequency the amplitude grows; driven much faster it barely moves (the paddle-ball example in [CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)).
3. Less damping gives a taller, narrower resonance peak ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)). 8.03 makes this quantitative: at resonance the amplitude is Q times the drive's displacement, where Q = ω₀/Γ ([8.03 Lec 3 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec3/)).
4. Pushing a swing at its own rhythm is resonance ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)).
5. Not every bridge failure is resonance. Tacoma Narrows was **flutter**, a self-excited oscillation in which the wind's push depends on the bridge's own motion ([18.03 Appendix B](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/63b26053bec64e7c32b93900b15d7fad_MIT18_03S10_Appendix_B.pdf)).

**Definitions**
- Resonance: driving a system at its natural frequency ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)).
- Natural frequency: the frequency at which a system oscillates freely, with no driving force or damping ([PH 14 concept item 12](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-concept-items)).
- Flutter / self-excitation: the driving force grows with the motion itself, so the effective damping becomes negative and the oscillations grow exponentially ([18.03 Appendix B](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/63b26053bec64e7c32b93900b15d7fad_MIT18_03S10_Appendix_B.pdf)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- A(ω_d) = f₀/√((ω₀²−ω_d²)² + ω_d²Γ²); A(ω₀) = QΔ ([8.03 Lec 3 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec3/)). This is for the gate only, not for the text.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- No source example. For a widget: swing period 3.17 s (9.1, ASSUMED 2.5 m), so push once every 3.2 s.

**Demonstrations**
- 8.03 Lec 3 ends with a rubbed wine glass ringing at one of its resonant frequencies (lecture transcript), plus demos titled "Breaking Glass with Sound", "Driven Mechanical Oscillator", "Ball on String Pendulum" and "Driven Cart on Air Track" ([Breaking Glass](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy4_of_lecture-3-video/), [Driven Mechanical Oscillator](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy3_of_lecture-3-video/), [Ball on String Pendulum](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy2_of_lecture-3-video/)). Mumbai-flat version: a wet finger round a steel or glass tumbler rim. Feasible, with adult care over thin glass. **Glass-breaking is not a home demo.**
- Pendulum-on-a-string resonance: hold a ball on a string and move your hand at different rhythms. Only the right rhythm builds a big swing (after the 8.03 "Ball on String Pendulum"; the OpenStax paddle ball in [CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)). Feasible.
- Sing at a piano with the dampers off and it sings the note back ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)). Feasible only if a piano or harmonium is to hand. A tanpura or guitar string answering a matching note is an easier swap, though unsourced.
- Coupled pendulums: "Two Rigid Pendulums Coupled with a Spring" ([8.03 resource](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-6-video/)).

**Concept questions**
- Why are soldiers ordered to break step on a bridge? ([CP 16 Conceptual Q13](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-conceptual-questions))
- When does a yo-yo on a moving finger travel farthest? When the finger's frequency equals the yo-yo's resonant frequency ([PH 14 concept item 9](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-concept-items)).
- `:::predict`: a singer shatters a glass. Must the note be loud, or must it be *the right note*? ([CP 16.8 Check Your Understanding](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance))

**Warnings the sources give**
- 18.03 says many textbooks call Tacoma resonance "the gusts of wind just happened to match the natural frequency of the bridge", and that "the wind was not gusting… This explanation is worthless." ([18.03 Appendix B](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/63b26053bec64e7c32b93900b15d7fad_MIT18_03S10_Appendix_B.pdf); it cites Billah & Scanlan, *Am. J. Phys.* 59, 118–124, 1991.)

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **OpenStax is wrong here.** CP 16.8's caption says Tacoma's "heavy cross winds drove the bridge into oscillations at its resonant frequency" ([CP 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-8-forced-oscillations-and-resonance)). **Don't follow it.** True minimal version, from 18.03 App B: a steady wind made the bridge twist, and the twisting changed how the wind pushed, so each twist fed the next. That is flutter, the same thing that makes a flag flap. It is not wind gusts in time with the bridge.
- CP 16.8 also says the Millennium Bridge closed "for the same reason". It didn't: London's wobble was pedestrian-driven. The true minimal version: the bridge swayed slightly sideways, walkers adjusted their steps to the sway, and their footfalls pushed it further. This is called synchronous lateral excitation. Source: [Wikipedia, Synchronous lateral excitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronous_lateral_excitation) (cite only, BY-SA; it cites Ingenia and a *Guardian* letter). The gate should find an engineering source (Dallard et al. 2001 / Arup); aisc.org returned 403.
- "Resonance makes energy from nothing" is false. Each push adds a little energy, and resonance only makes the pushes add up instead of cancelling.

**Gaps**
- Millennium Bridge (2000): no OpenStax or OCW source beyond CP's misleading line. Dates and damper counts conflict across web sources (opened 10 June 2000; closed 12 June 2000; reopened February 2002). Gate.
- The Tacoma dog "Tubby" (BOOKPLAN tales list): 18.03 App B says only "One car and a dog were lost". The name is unsourced here.

## 9.3 What a wave carries

**Concept sequence**
1. A wave is a disturbance that propagates away from where it was made ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
2. The disturbance moves; the water does not. A gull bobs as the wave passes ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
3. Waves carry energy without necessarily carrying mass ([PH 13.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/13-1-types-of-waves)).
4. Wavelength, period and frequency are linked by v = fλ, which holds for all waves ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
5. Transverse waves move the medium across the direction of travel; longitudinal waves move it along that direction ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
6. A wave's energy goes as amplitude squared; intensity is power per area ([CP 16.11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-11-energy-in-waves-intensity)).
7. Two waves passing through each other add up (superposition) and then carry on unchanged ([CP 16.10](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-10-superposition-and-interference); [PH 13.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/13-3-wave-interaction-superposition-and-interference)).

**Definitions**
- Wave: "a disturbance that propagates, or moves from the place it was created" ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
- Mechanical waves "require a medium to travel through"; light does not ([PH 13.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/13-1-types-of-waves)).
- Intensity: I = P/A, in W/m² ([CP 16.11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-11-energy-in-waves-intensity)).
- **IGCSE 0625** topic 3.1 is "General properties of waves" ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)). BOOKPLAN's Big Idea 3 is "waves carry energy without carrying stuff".

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- v_w = λ/T = fλ ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)).
- Energy ∝ amplitude² ([CP 16.11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-11-energy-in-waves-intensity)). Doubling the amplitude gives `python3 -I -c 'print(2**2)'` → 4× the energy.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: crests 10.0 m apart and a gull bobbing every 5.00 s give v = 2.00 m/s ([CP 16.9 Ex 16.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves)). `python3 -I -c 'print(10.0/5.00)'`.
- Sunlight of 700 W/m² on 0.500 m² for 4.00 h delivers `python3 -I -c 'print(700*0.5*4*3600)'` → 5.04 × 10⁶ J ([CP 16.11 Ex 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-11-energy-in-waves-intensity)).

**Demonstrations**
- OpenStax "Waves in a Bowl": drop a cork into a still basin and estimate wavelength and period ([CP 16.9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-9-waves); Snap Lab in [PH 13.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/13-2-wave-properties-speed-amplitude-frequency-and-period)). Mumbai flat: a bucket or parat with a paper scrap (BOOKPLAN 9.3). Feasible.

## 9.4 Sound is a push passed on

**Concept sequence**
1. A vibrating object squeezes and stretches the air next to it, making compressions and rarefactions that travel outward ([CP 17.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-1-sound)).
2. In air, sound is longitudinal, because fluids have almost no shear strength ([CP 17.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-1-sound)).
3. Sound needs a medium. It is a mechanical wave ([PH 13.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/13-1-types-of-waves)).
4. Its speed is set by how stiff and how dense the medium is: stiffer means faster. Solids and liquids are faster than air ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)).
5. In air the speed rises with temperature as √T ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)).
6. 8.03: Newton assumed the air's temperature stays fixed and got a speed that was too low. Laplace realised the squeezing is too quick for heat to flow (adiabatic, γ = 1.4) and got about 342 m/s, which matches the measurement ([8.03 Lec 11 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec11/); [Lec 11 page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-i-mechanical-vibrations-and-waves/lecture-11/)).
7. Echoes: the delay is proportional to distance, as bats show ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)).

**Definitions**
- Sound: "a disturbance of matter that is transmitted from its source outward" ([CP 17.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-1-sound)).
- **IGCSE 0625** topic 3.4 is "Sound". Cambridge Lower Secondary 7Ps: "particle vibration in sound; no sound in a vacuum; echoes" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- v = 331 m/s × √(T/273 K) for air ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)).
- **Air at 30 °C:** `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(331*math.sqrt(303/273),1))'` → **348.7 m/s**, which is exactly CP Example 17.1's value at 30.0 °C. At 20 °C it is 342.9 m/s (CP rounds to 343).
- CP Table 17.1: air (0 °C) 331; fresh water (20 °C) **1480**; sea water 1540; **steel 5960**; aluminium 5120; glass (Pyrex) 5640; lead 1960; polyethylene 920; vulcanised rubber 54 m/s ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)).
- Ratios: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(5960/331,1), round(1480/331,2))'` → steel is 18× and water 4.5× air at 0 °C.
- 8.03: v = √(γP₀/ρ) with P₀ ≈ 10⁵ Pa and ρ = 1.2 kg/m³. `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.sqrt(1.4e5/1.2),1), round(math.sqrt(1e5/1.2),1))'` → Laplace 341.6 m/s, Newton 288.7 m/s.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Echo off a building 50 m away (ASSUMED) at 30 °C: `python3 -I -c 'v=331*(303/273)**0.5;print(round(2*50/v,3))'` → 0.287 s. That agrees with misconceptions.md's "about 0.3 s", and phyphox's acoustic stopwatch can time it.
- Source example at 30 °C: the audible extremes 20 Hz and 20 kHz have wavelengths of 17 m and 1.7 cm ([CP 17.2 Ex 17.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)). `python3 -I -c 'v=331*(303/273)**0.5;print(round(v/20,1), round(v/20000*100,2))'` → 17.4 m, 1.74 cm.
- 8.03 classroom measurement: a 34 cm wavelength in a glass tube gave 340 m/s, "within 1%" of Laplace (Lec 11 transcript, demo "Standing Sound Waves in a Glass Tube": [8.03 resource](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-11-video/)).

**Demonstrations**
- Spoon tied to a string, string ends held to the ears: a loud bell sound comes through the string ([misconceptions.md §8](../research/misconceptions.md)). Not in OpenStax or OCW. Mumbai flat: feasible.
- OpenStax take-home: hang a sheet of paper by its top edge and speak softly, then loudly, at it ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)). Feasible.
- Fireworks: the flash comes before the bang ([CP 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength)). Diwali fireworks seen from a Mumbai balcony are a natural version.
- Bell jar: no home vacuum is good enough ([misconceptions.md §8](../research/misconceptions.md)). The research file found no bell-jar video URL. Gap.

**Concept questions**
- When sound passes into a medium where its speed is different, does its frequency or its wavelength change? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions))

- You see one firework and hear it at once, while another's bang comes later. Why? ([CP 17.2 Check Your Understanding](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-2-speed-of-sound-frequency-and-wavelength))

**Warnings the sources give**

## 9.5 Loud and high

**Concept sequence**
1. Perceived frequency is pitch. We can tell apart frequencies only about 0.3% apart ([CP 17.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).
2. Bigger vibrations carry more energy (∝ amplitude²) and sound louder ([CP 16.11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-11-energy-in-waves-intensity)).
3. The ear covers about 10¹² in intensity, so we use the decibel scale: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) with I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² ([CP 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level)).
4. Loudness depends on intensity *and* frequency, because the ear is less sensitive at the extremes ([CP 17.6, Table 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).
5. Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; below is infrasound and above is ultrasound ([CP 17.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).

**Definitions**
- Pitch: "The perception of frequency is called pitch." ([CP 17.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing))
- Loudness corresponds to intensity and frequency; timbre to the number and relative intensity of the frequencies present ([CP 17.6 Table 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).
- **Cambridge Lower Secondary 9Ps:** "waveforms (loudness ↔ amplitude, pitch ↔ frequency)" ([school-wording.md §2](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- dB table: 0 dB is the threshold at 1000 Hz; 20 a whisper at 1 m; 60 normal conversation; 70 busy traffic ([CP 17.3 Table 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level)).
- Each +10 dB is 10× the intensity. 70 dB vs 40 dB: `python3 -I -c 'print(10**((70-40)/10))'` → 1000×.
- Doubling the amplitude: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(10*math.log10(4),2))'` → +6.02 dB.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Mumbai traffic sits in the 70 dB "busy traffic" row (CP Table 17.2). CP Fig 17.11's photo even shows Delhi traffic, "credit: Lingaraj G J, Flickr" (a photo; redraw only).

**Demonstrations**
- **Sensor lab:** a microphone oscilloscope of her own voice (BOOKPLAN). Humming louder makes taller waves at the same spacing; humming higher packs the waves closer together. The physics is CP 17.6 Table 17.4 plus CP 16.11.
- "Feeling sound": music through a speaker on a light table; turn it up and feel the table vibrate ([CP 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level); [PH 14.2 Snap Lab](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-2-sound-intensity-and-sound-level)). Feasible.

**Concept questions**
- What is the amplitude of a sound wave perceived as? Loudness ([PH 14 concept item 1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-concept-items)).
- The mayor says new trains will add "only 30 dB". Should people worry? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions)) That is 1000× the intensity.
- `:::predict`: if you sing the same note louder, does the pitch go up?

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Sound intensity level is not the same as intensity." ([CP 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-3-sound-intensity-and-sound-level))
- "Pitch perception is directly related to frequency and is not greatly affected by other physical quantities" ([CP 17.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**

- "Loudness *is* amplitude" is near enough for school (0893 says so), but CP adds that loudness also depends on frequency. **True minimal version:** a bigger wave sounds louder, *at the same pitch*.
- "10 dB louder sounds 10 times as loud" is false as perception. 10 dB is 10× the *intensity* (CP 17.3).

## 9.6 Why a tabla sings

**Concept sequence**
1. A string fixed at both ends has standing waves with λ₁ = 2L, so f₁ = v/2L, and its overtones are 2f₁, 3f₁ and so on ([CP 16.10](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-10-superposition-and-interference)).
2. Any shape of the string can be built from these modes (8.03's Fourier decomposition: [Lec 9](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-i-mechanical-vibrations-and-waves/lecture-9/)).
3. Air columns do the same. A tube closed at one end has only odd multiples, fₙ = (2n−1)v/4L ([PH 14.4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-4-sound-interference-and-resonance)); an open tube has all multiples.
4. The mix of overtones is timbre. It is why a violin and a piano playing the same note sound different ([CP 17.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-5-sound-interference-and-resonance-standing-waves-in-air-columns); [CP 17.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-6-hearing)).
5. Most drums' overtones are **not** whole-number multiples, so they don't sound a clear note. The tabla is the exception (Raman & Kumar 1920; [history.md §5](../research/history.md)). This step is not in OpenStax.

**Definitions**
- Fundamental: the lowest resonant frequency. Overtones are all the higher ones. Harmonics are the resonances that are whole-number multiples of the fundamental ([CP 17.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-5-sound-interference-and-resonance-standing-waves-in-air-columns)).
- Timbre: the quality that tells sounds apart beyond pitch and loudness ([PH 14 concept item 5](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-concept-items)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- String: fₙ = n·v/2L ([CP 16.10](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/16-10-superposition-and-interference)).
- Closed tube: fₙ = (2n−1)v/4L ([PH 14.4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-4-sound-interference-and-resonance); 8.03 Lec 11 notes derive the same boundary conditions: [notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec11/)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- A 30 cm bottle-like tube closed at one end (ASSUMED) at 30 °C: `python3 -I -c 'v=331*(303/273)**0.5;L=0.30;print(round(v/(4*L),1),[round((2*n-1)*v/(4*L)) for n in (1,2,3)],round(v/(2*L),1))'` → fundamental 290.6 Hz with overtones at 872 and 1453 Hz (odd multiples only). The same tube open at both ends has a fundamental of 581.2 Hz.

**Demonstrations**
- Blow across a bottle to get a toot (named in [CP 17.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-5-sound-interference-and-resonance-standing-waves-in-air-columns)). Add water and the note rises. Feasible.
- **Live:** a Web Audio harmonic builder (BOOKPLAN). Whole-number overtones sound "tabla-like"; non-whole-number ones sound like a tin drum. The physics is CP 17.5 plus the research for the drum part.
- 8.03 demos "Chladni Figures (Speaker Driven)" and "Rijke Tube" show 2D plate modes and a singing tube ([Chladni](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-16-video/), [Rijke](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy3_of_lecture-9-video/)). Chladni patterns from salt on a speaker-driven plate are drum-like modes, and are a strong visual for "a drumhead has its own odd set of modes". A home version is possible with a speaker, a tray and salt, but needs adult setup.

**Concept questions**
- What's the difference between an overtone and a harmonic? Are all overtones harmonics? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q10](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions)) This is the right question for the tabla.
- Two wind instruments of the same length, one open and one closed at one end: which can play the lower note? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q9](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions))
- Why is an unamplified guitar so much louder than a string on a stick? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions))

**Warnings the sources give**
- The overtone mix "is what gives musical instruments (and human voices) their distinctive characteristics, whether they have air columns, strings, or drumheads" ([PH 14.4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-4-sound-interference-and-resonance)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **OpenStax overstates this.** CP 17.5 says "All resonant frequencies are integral multiples of the fundamental" ([CP 17.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-5-sound-interference-and-resonance-standing-waves-in-air-columns)). That is true for ideal strings and open tubes. It is not true for closed tubes (odd multiples only) or for drums and plates, which are mostly *not* whole-number multiples. That is exactly why the tabla is remarkable. **True minimal version:** strings and pipes ring at neat multiples; most drums don't, and the tabla's black patch tunes it until it nearly does.
- CP 16.10 calls f₂ = 2f₁ "the first harmonic". In CP 17.5's own scheme it is the *second* harmonic, which is the first overtone. Use CP 17.5's scheme.
- Pythagoras and the hammers is a LEGEND: hammer weight doesn't set pitch ([history.md §5](../research/history.md)). Use Vincenzo Galilei's strings (an octave needs four times the weight), which is TRUE.
- Vincenzo's "four times the weight" follows from v ∝ √tension. That relation isn't in the pages fetched here, so a gate should ground v = √(T/μ) before the text explains it.

- The wave speed on a string, v = √(T/μ), isn't in the pages fetched; it's probably in CP 16.9's later part or *University Physics* (not fetched).

## 9.7 The moving whistle

**Concept sequence**
1. A passing motorbike's pitch drops as it goes by ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
2. Each compression spreads out from the point where it was emitted, but a moving source keeps moving to new points. So the waves bunch up ahead of it and spread out behind ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
3. Sound has a fixed speed in the air, so a shorter wavelength means a higher frequency ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
4. A moving listener gets the same kind of shift ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
5. If source and listener move together there is no shift; the train driver hears the true horn ([CP 17.4 Ex 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
6. At the speed of sound the waves pile up, which is the start of the sonic boom ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).

**Definitions**
- Doppler effect: "an alteration in the observed frequency of a sound due to motion of either the source or the observer" ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
- **IGCSE 0625** has no Doppler for sound in the quoted content; redshift appears in 6.2.3 ([school-wording.md §1](../research/school-wording.md)). IB DP C.5 is "Doppler effect" (§4). This sets up Ch 15.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Moving source, stationary listener: f_obs = f_s · v_w/(v_w ∓ v_s), with − for approaching ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).
- Moving listener: f_obs = f_s · (v_w ± v_obs)/v_w ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: a 150 Hz horn at 35.0 m/s with v = 340 m/s gives 167 Hz approaching and 136 Hz receding. The shifts aren't symmetric (+17, −14 Hz) ([CP 17.4 Ex 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms)). `python3 -I -c 'f=150;v=340;s=35;print(round(f*v/(v-s),1), round(f*v/(v+s),1))'` → 167.2, 136.0.
- **Mumbai local train** (ASSUMED: 300 Hz horn, 80 km/h, 30 °C so v = 348.7 m/s): `python3 -I -c 'import math;v=331*(303/273)**0.5;f=300;s=80/3.6;a=f*v/(v-s);r=f*v/(v+s);print(round(a,1),round(r,1),round(a/r,3),round(12*math.log2(a/r),2))'` → **320.4 Hz approaching, 282.0 Hz receding**. The ratio is 1.136, a drop of about **2.2 semitones** (a bit more than a whole tone) as the train passes. A gate should check the horn frequency and train speed; both are unsourced.
- These formulas hold when the motion is along the line joining source and listener, so far from the track (CP 17.4 discussion). Right beside the track the change is quick but smooth.

**Demonstrations**
- **Live:** Doppler wavefronts with audio (BOOKPLAN). The basis is CP Figs 17.13–17.15, redrawn as our own figure.
- Stand on a platform (behind the yellow line) and listen to a through train's horn. Hum the before and after notes into the phone tuner. Feasible, with **platform-safety wording required**.
- Buys Ballot's 1845 horn players on a train wagon ([history.md §5](../research/history.md)).

**Concept questions**
- Is the Doppler shift real, or just a sensory illusion? ([CP 17 Conceptual Q5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-conceptual-questions))
- Two sources at the same frequency move towards you, one faster. Which sounds higher? The faster one ([PH 14 concept item 6](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/14-concept-items)).
- What does the train's own driver hear? (CP Ex 17.4 part b) A good `:::predict`.

**Warnings the sources give**
- "There is no Doppler shift in the frequency of conversations between driver and passenger on a motorcycle." ([CP 17.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/17-4-doppler-effect-and-sonic-booms))

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **OpenStax misattributes the experiment.** CP 17.4 says Doppler "had musicians play on a moving open train car". [history.md §5](../research/history.md) records that *Buys Ballot* ran the horn-player test in 1845, and that Doppler's own 1842 main example (double-star colours) was wrong. **Follow history.md.**
- "The horn gets louder, so it sounds higher" mixes pitch with loudness (9.5). Pitch rises because the waves arrive more often, not because they are stronger.
- "The pitch slides down smoothly the whole time" is wrong. It sits nearly steady while the train is far off, then drops fast as it passes. This follows from the CP 17.4 caveat about motion along the line of sight.
