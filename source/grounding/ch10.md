## 10.1 Does anything leave your eyes?

**Concept sequence**
1. Euclid and Ptolemy believed the eyes sent out rays ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
2. Ibn al-Haytham experimented and argued that light reflected from objects enters the eye ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
3. Light reaches us straight from a source, through a medium, or after reflection ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)).
4. Rough surfaces scatter light in every direction (diffuse reflection), and that is how we see a page, a wall or a hand from any angle ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
5. So with no light source there is nothing to reflect into the eye, and you see nothing. This step follows from 3–4; OpenStax never states it outright.

**Definitions**
- Diffused light: light reflected in many directions from a rough surface ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
- **IGCSE 0625** has "Draw and use ray diagrams" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). NCERT Class 7 says light "reflects off non-luminous objects" but never says it must enter the eye, so the bridge sentence is "…and then that reflected light has to reach your eye" ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- None needed.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- The eye copes with intensities from the faintest it can detect up to 10¹⁰ times brighter ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)). That huge range is why "a bit of light from somewhere" is enough to see. A truly dark room still gives nothing.

**Demonstrations**
- Windowless bathroom at night, lights off: she can't see her hand ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)). Feasible. Wait about a minute first so the eyes adapt (CP 26.3 take-home on rods and cones: [CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)), so that "I just needed time" isn't the explanation.
- A torch beam through mist from a plant sprayer is visible only where droplets send light into the eye ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)). Feasible.
- OpenStax take-home: shine a torch at paper, then at a mirror, and compare diffuse and mirror reflection ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)). Feasible.

**Concept questions**
- How does face powder take the shine off a nose? (Diffuse reflection: [CP 25 Conceptual Q1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions))
- Propose a way to study rods alone, given that they work in light about 1000× dimmer than cones need ([CP 26 Conceptual Q13](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-conceptual-questions)).
- `:::predict`: in a sealed, perfectly dark room, will your eyes "get used to it" enough to see your hand?

**Warnings the sources give**
- Extramission is held by 86% of students on a drawing task and 69% of adults asked directly, and refutation teaching faded within 3–5 months (Winer et al. 2002 in [misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)). That is why BOOKPLAN uses `:::recall`.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Your eyes need light to work, like a torch needs batteries" puts light in the eye's job. **True minimal version:** light has to travel *from* something *into* your eye.
- "Seeing in the dark just takes time" is false for a sealed room. Adaptation needs *some* light to collect.
- CP 26.1 says Ibn al-Haytham "recognized that light reflected from objects entered the eye through the lens and was passed to the optic nerve", and that he "did not fully understand the mechanisms involved". Don't make him into a modern optics textbook.

**Gaps**
- No OCW coverage of vision or extramission.
- Euclid, Ptolemy and Ibn al-Haytham in Cairo are fuller in [history.md §5](../research/history.md). The madness story is HEDGE there.

## 10.2 Straight lines and shadows

**Concept sequence**
1. Light is modelled as travelling in straight lines called rays ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)).
2. This works when light meets objects much larger than its wavelength (under a micron), which covers almost everything visible to the eye ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light); [UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).
3. Light changes direction at a mirror or a boundary, then goes straight again ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)).
4. 8.03 shows that the ray laws follow from waves when the wavelength is small ([8.03 Lec 16](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-ii-electromagnetic-waves/lecture-16/)).
5. Straight-line travel explains sharp shadows and the upside-down image in a pinhole camera (pinhole: gap, see below).

**Definitions**
- Ray: "a straight line that originates at some point" ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)).
- Geometric optics: the part of optics where the ray aspect dominates ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)).
- **School bridge:** "A ray isn't a thing that exists. It's a pencil line showing the direction light goes" ([school-wording.md §6](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Visible light wavelength is "less than a micron" ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)). The range is 400–750 nm ([CP 24.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)) or 400–700 nm ([AS 5.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-2-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). The books differ at the red end; say "about 400 to 700 nm".

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Pinhole image size by similar triangles (ASSUMED: a 2 m doorway 5 m away, a 30 cm box): `python3 -I -c 'print(round(2*0.30/5*100,1))'` → a 12 cm upside-down image. This is geometry only; no optics source needed.

**Demonstrations**
- Pinhole camera / camera obscura at home (BOOKPLAN). Feasible with a shoebox, foil and tracing paper. **Never point it at the Sun** (PH 16.3 Snap Lab eye-safety line: [PH 16.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-3-lenses)).
- Al-Farisi put a water-filled glass sphere inside a camera obscura to study rainbows ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)). That is a sourced historical camera obscura.

**Concept questions**
- Geometric optics describes light meeting large objects. Why can we still use it to analyse a microscope's image? ([CP 26 Conceptual Q14](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-conceptual-questions))
- `:::predict`: make the pinhole bigger. Is the image brighter, blurrier, or both?

**Warnings the sources give**
- Rays only work when objects are "several times as large as its wavelength" ([CP 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-1-the-ray-aspect-of-light)). This sets up diffraction in Ch 11.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Light always travels in straight lines" is false at tiny gaps and edges (diffraction, Ch 11), and false where it bends between materials (10.4). **True minimal version:** in one material, and past things much bigger than its wavelength, light goes straight.
- "A ray is a thin beam of light" mixes the drawing with the thing. Use the bridge sentence above.

**Gaps**
- **The pinhole camera is not in any OpenStax or OCW page fetched.** It needs a source at the gate; history.md has Ibn al-Haytham's dark-room images.
- Shadows (umbra/penumbra) are not covered either.

## 10.3 Mirrors

**Concept sequence**
1. Angles are measured from the normal (the perpendicular) ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
2. Law of reflection: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)). 8.03 derives it from the wave picture ([8.03 Lec 16 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec16/)).
3. Rays from a point reflect into the eye. Traced back, they seem to come from a point *behind* the mirror, and that is the image ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
4. The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
5. It is a virtual image: the rays only appear to come from it. It can still be photographed ([PH 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-1-reflection)).

**Definitions**
- Law of reflection: "The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence." ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection))
- Virtual image: one "formed when light rays appear to diverge from a point without actually doing so" ([PH 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-1-reflection)).
- **IGCSE 0625** 3.2.1 Reflection: "Define and use the terms normal, angle of incidence…" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- θ_r = θ_i ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Standing 1.0 m from a mirror, your image is 1.0 m behind the glass and 2.0 m from you ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).
- To see yourself head to toe you need a mirror half your height, whatever your distance. `python3 -I -c 'print(1.50/2)'` → 0.75 m for a 1.50 m person. This answers [CP 25 Conceptual Q28](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions).

**Demonstrations**
- OpenStax take-home: draw the incident and reflected rays of a torch (or laser pointer) on paper at a mirror and measure the angles ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)). Feasible. Warn not to shine a laser into eyes.
- PH Snap Lab: images in the back and front of a shiny spoon (convex vs concave) ([PH 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-1-reflection)). Feasible with a steel spoon.
- **Live:** ray mirror (BOOKPLAN).

**Concept questions**
- Can you see a virtual image? Can you photograph one? Yes and yes ([PH 16 concept item 1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-concept-items); [CP 25 Conceptual Q20](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions)).
- How tall must a mirror be to show your whole body, and does it depend on distance? ([CP 25 Conceptual Q28](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions))
- A flat mirror has "infinite focal length". Where does it form an image? ([CP 25 Conceptual Q29](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions))

**Warnings the sources give**
- Mirror images "are not figments of our imagination" and can be photographed ([CP 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-2-the-law-of-reflection)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The image is on the mirror surface" is the misconception (IOP Spark, in [misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)).
- **Wrong option in the source:** PH 16 concept item 1 lists the distractor "an image from a flat mirror cannot be photographed". Only use the correct key.
- "Step back and you'll see more of yourself" is false for a flat mirror. The half-height rule doesn't depend on distance (CP 25 Q28).

**Gaps**
- None.

## 10.4 Bending light

**Concept sequence**
1. A fish in a tank can seem to be in two places ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)).
2. Light bends at a boundary *because it changes speed* there ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)).
3. Index of refraction n = c/v, which is always ≥ 1 ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)).
4. Slowing down bends the ray toward the normal; speeding up bends it away. The path is reversible. OpenStax uses a lawnmower running from footpath onto grass as the picture ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)).
5. Snell's law: n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂. Ibn Sahl found it in 984 ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)). 8.03 derives it from waves and notes it works for water waves, sound and light ([8.03 Lec 16 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec16/)).
6. Going from slow to fast past the critical angle, all the light reflects (total internal reflection) ([CP 25.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-4-total-internal-reflection)).

**Definitions**
- Refraction: "The changing of a light ray's direction (loosely called bending) when it passes through variations in matter" ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)).
- Critical angle: the incident angle that gives a refracted angle of 90° ([CP 25.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-4-total-internal-reflection)).
- **Cambridge Lower Secondary 8Ps:** refraction "in terms of change of speed" ([school-wording.md §2](../research/school-wording.md)). IGCSE 3.2.2 is Refraction.

**Laws, equations, standard values** ([CP 25.3 Table 25.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction))
- Water (fresh, 20 °C) **n = 1.333**; air 1.000293; ice 1.309; crown glass 1.52; diamond 2.419.
- Speed of light in water: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(299792458/1.333/1e8,3), round(1/1.333,3))'` → 2.249 × 10⁸ m/s, which is 0.75 c.
- Snell, air to water: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.degrees(math.asin(math.sin(math.radians(45))/1.333)),1), round(math.degrees(math.asin(math.sin(math.radians(30))/1.333)),1))'` → 45° goes to 32.0°, and 30° to 22.0°.
- Critical angle, water to air: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.degrees(math.asin(1/1.333)),1))'` → 48.6°.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- **Apparent depth** (the coin in a cup), looking straight down only: depth appears divided by n. `python3 -I -c 'print(round(10/1.333,1), round(8/1.333,1))'` → a coin under 10 cm of water looks 7.5 cm deep; under 8 cm, 6.0 cm. This d/n rule is the small-angle approximation and is **not stated in any source fetched**. It follows from Snell for near-vertical rays, so label it "looking straight down".
- **The disappearing coin** (ASSUMED: an 8 cm deep cup, the eye's line of sight passing the near rim at 45° from vertical). Empty, that line reaches the bottom 8.0 cm from the near wall. Filled to the rim, the ray bends at the surface to 32°, so the eye now sees bottom points only 5.0 cm from the near wall. A coin anywhere in that 3.0 cm strip *appears*. `python3 -I -c 'import math;H=8;t1=math.radians(45);t2=math.asin(math.sin(t1)/1.333);print(round(H*math.tan(t1),1),round(H*math.tan(t2),1),round(H*(math.tan(t1)-math.tan(t2)),1))'` → 8.0, 5.0, 3.0.

**Demonstrations**
- PH Snap Lab "Bent Pencil": a pencil in a glass half full of water, viewed from the side ([PH 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/16-2-refraction)). Feasible.
- The coin in a cup (BOOKPLAN, Case). Feasible with a steel tumbler and a ₹1 coin.
- 8.03 demos "Total Internal Reflection" and "Fiber Optic Bundle" ([TIR](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-17-video/), [fibre](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy2_of_lecture-17-video/)). Home version: a torch shone up through the side of a glass of water toward its surface, which glints silver at steep angles (unsourced; gate).
- **Live:** refraction (BOOKPLAN). PhET "Bending Light" is linked in [sources.md §4](../research/sources.md).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **Source wording to avoid:** PH 13 concept item 13 defines refraction as bending between media "with different densities". The cause is a difference in *wave speed*. For light, the "optically denser" medium need not be denser by mass. **Use "speed".**
- "Light bends because it's pulled into the water" is false. **True minimal version:** it slows down, and the side that slows first swings it round (the lawnmower).
- "The coin moves up" is false. Its *image* moves; the coin stays put.
- The d/n apparent depth only holds looking almost straight down. At a slant the coin looks shallower still.

**Gaps**

## 10.5 Lenses and the eye

**Concept sequence**
1. A convex (converging) lens bends parallel rays to meet at its focal point. A magnifying glass can focus sunlight to a spot ([CP 25.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses)).
2. Every part of the lens helps form the image. Half a lens gives the same image, only fainter ([CP 25.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses)).
3. The eye's cornea and lens act together like one converging lens, making a real, upside-down image on the retina ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
4. The cornea does about two-thirds of the bending, because air to cornea is the biggest speed change ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
5. Accommodation: the ciliary muscle makes the lens thicker and stronger for near objects. The normal near point is 25 cm ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
6. Short-sight (myopia): the eye converges too much and is fixed with a diverging lens. Long-sight (hyperopia): too little, fixed with a converging lens. Glasses don't change the eye ([CP 26.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-2-vision-correction)).

**Definitions**
- Focal point / focal length ([CP 25.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-6-image-formation-by-lenses)).
- Lens power P = 1/f, in dioptres (1 D = 1/m) ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
- Accommodation: "The process of adjusting the eye's focal length" ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
- **IGCSE 0625** 3.2.3 is "Thin lenses" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Eye indices: cornea 1.38, aqueous 1.34, lens 1.41 (average), vitreous 1.34, water 1.33 ([CP 26.1 Table 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).
- Typical lens-to-retina distance 2.00 cm ([CP 26.1 Ex 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: eye power ranges from 50.0 D (far) to 54.0 D (25 cm), an 8% increase ([CP 26.1 Ex 26.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye)). `python3 -I -c 'print(1/0.02, 1/0.25+1/0.02, round(100*(54/50-1)))'` → 50.0, 54.0, 8.
- Why underwater vision blurs: the cornea's index (1.38) is close to water's (1.33), so in water the cornea hardly bends light and loses most of its two-thirds share ([CP 26.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-1-physics-of-the-eye); [CP 26 Conceptual Q5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-conceptual-questions)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(1.38-1.00,2), round(1.38-1.33,2))'` → the index jump at the cornea falls from 0.38 to 0.05.

## 10.6 How fast is light?

**Concept sequence**
1. Galileo found light "extremely fast, perhaps instantaneous" ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction); 8.03 Lec 12 notes: "1638 Galileo: speed of light is large" [notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec12/)).
2. Rømer timed eclipses of Io, Jupiter's moon. The gap between eclipses was longer while Earth moved away from Jupiter and shorter while it moved closer, because light needs time to cover the changing distance ([UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).
3. Fizeau (1849) measured it on Earth by sending light through a spinning toothed wheel to a distant mirror and back ([UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).
4. Foucault (1862) used a rotating mirror, and Michelson refined the method up to 1926 ([UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).
5. Maxwell computed the speed of an electromagnetic disturbance, found it equalled the speed of light, and concluded light is electromagnetic ([AS 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-1-the-behavior-of-light); [8.03 Lec 12](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-ii-electromagnetic-waves/lecture-12/)).
6. Since 1983 c is not measured: it is fixed, and the metre is defined from it ([BIPM CGPM 1983 Res. 1](https://www.bipm.org/en/committees/cg/cgpm/17-1983/resolution-1)).

**Definitions**
- 1983 metre: "The metre is the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299 792 458 of a second." ([BIPM](https://www.bipm.org/en/committees/cg/cgpm/17-1983/resolution-1)) The page notes this wording was abrogated in 2018 by the 26th CGPM (Resolution 1, effective 2019). NIST's current CODATA listing still gives c as exactly 299 792 458 m/s, so "fixed since 1983" stays true for the number.
- Astronomical unit: "A conventional unit of length equal to 149 597 870 700 m exactly." (IAU 2012 Resolution B2, [IAU via Paris Observatory](https://syrte.obspm.fr/IAU_resolutions/IAUResol_2012_0.html))

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- c = 299 792 458 m/s, **exact** ([NIST CODATA](https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/cuu/Value?c); [CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction); [UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).
- Moon: semimajor axis 384,400 km; perigee 363,300 km; apogee 405,500 km ([NASA Moon fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- **Sunlight's age:** `python3 -I -c 'c=299792458;au=149597870700;print(round(au/c,1), round(au/c/60,2))'` → 499.0 s = **8.32 min** (Sun at 1 au).
- **Across Earth's orbit (2 au):** `python3 -I -c 'c=299792458;au=149597870700;print(round(2*au/c,1), round(2*au/c/60,2))'` → 998.0 s = **16.63 min**.
- **Rømer's 22 minutes:** Rømer's figure was about 22 minutes for light to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit. It appears in his 1677 letter to Huygens, not in the 1676 *Journal des sçavans* report ([Wikipedia, Rømer's determination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%B8mer%27s_determination_of_the_speed_of_light); cite only, BY-SA). The speed it implies: `python3 -I -c 'au=149597870700;print(round(2*au/(22*60)/1e8,3), round(100*(1-2*au/(22*60)/299792458),1))'` → 2.267 × 10⁸ m/s, **24.4% low**. That matches CP's "2.26 × 10⁸ m/s (only 25% different)" ([CP 25.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-3-the-law-of-refraction)). The modern answer to "~22 or ~16.6 min?" is: **Rømer said about 22; the true value is 16.6.**
- **Moon radio delay:** `python3 -I -c 'c=299792.458;print([round(d/c,3) for d in (384400,363300,405500)], round(2*384400/c,3))'` → one way 1.282 s (1.212 at perigee to 1.353 at apogee); **round trip about 2.56 s**.
- **Fizeau:** UP3 gives 3.15 × 10⁸ m/s, "only 5% too high" ([UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(100*(3.15/2.99792458-1),1))'` → 5.1%. With [history.md §5](../research/history.md)'s 720 teeth and 8.6 km: `python3 -I -c 'c=299792458;d=8633;t=2*d/c;print(round(t*1e6,1), round(1/(2*720*t),1))'` → round trip 57.6 µs, first eclipse at about 12 turns per second. (8,633 m is an ASSUMED reading of "8.6 km"; UP3 says "8 km".)
- Foucault 2.98 × 10⁸, "within 0.6%": `python3 -I -c 'print(round(100*(1-2.98/2.99792458),2))'` → 0.6% ([UP3 1.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-1-the-propagation-of-light)).

**Demonstrations**
- Lightning flash before thunder. Does that prove light is extremely fast, or only faster than sound? ([CP 25 Conceptual Q4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions)) Monsoon storms make this a Mumbai natural.

**Warnings the sources give**

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **The sources disagree on Rømer's number.** CP 25.3 says "From his 1676 data, a value… 2.26 × 10⁸ m/s". UP3 1.1 says Roemer "calculated that the speed of light was 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s" and dates it to 1675. 8.03 Lec 12 says "1676 Romer: 2.2 × 10⁸ m/s". [history.md §5](../research/history.md) says Rømer gave a *time*, not a speed, and Huygens did the arithmetic; Wikipedia agrees he gave no Earth-unit value. **True minimal version:** in 1676 Rømer showed light takes time; he estimated about 22 minutes to cross Earth's orbit; the right answer is about 16.6 minutes; others turned his time into a speed.
