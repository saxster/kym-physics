# Grounding: Chapter 11 — Colour, and what light really is

Big question (BOOKPLAN §5): *The sky is blue, the sunset over Marine Drive is orange, and the sea is blue too. Is it the same reason every time?*

Grounded 2026-10-28. Every page cited was fetched in this pass, apart from rows marked otherwise. Numbers were checked with `python3 -I -c`. Link abbreviations: **CP** = OpenStax *College Physics 2e*; **PH** = OpenStax *Physics* (high school); **UP3** = OpenStax *University Physics* Vol. 3; **AS** = OpenStax *Astronomy 2e*; **8.03** = MIT OCW 8.03SC *Physics III: Vibrations and Waves*, Fall 2016 (Yen-Jie Lee). All are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The 8.03 demo pages cited below each attach a whole-lecture transcript. RES.8-003 (`techtv.mit.edu`) did not resolve on 2026-10-28.

**Short answer to the big question, as the sources support it.** The blue sky and the orange sunset have the *same* cause: small air molecules scatter short wavelengths much more than long ones (CP 27 Conceptual Q34–35; NASA Space Place). The sea is a *different* story. Raman showed it is not just reflected sky (history.md), and his explanation was scattering by the water itself. Whether that is the whole story isn't settled by any source fetched here (see 11.4 Gaps).

## 11.1 White is a mixture

**Concept sequence**
1. A rainbow and a prism give the same sequence of colours ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
2. White light is a fairly even mixture of all visible wavelengths. Sunlight looks slightly yellow but contains them all ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
3. A material's index of refraction depends on wavelength, and is largest for violet. So violet bends most and white light spreads out (dispersion) ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms); [8.03 Lec 19 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec19/), "n = n(ω) which is usually decreasing versus wavelength").
4. Rainbows: refraction into a drop, reflection off the back, refraction out ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
5. The rainbow is continuous. "Seven colours" is a convention ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
6. Al-Farisi (1267–1319) and Theodoric of Freiberg used giant water drops, building on Ibn al-Haytham ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)). Newton's prism letter of 1672 is in [history.md §5](../research/history.md).

**Definitions**
- Dispersion: "the spreading of white light into its full spectrum of wavelengths". More generally, any process that changes the direction of light by an amount that depends on wavelength ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
- **IGCSE 0625** 3.2.4 is "Dispersion" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Cambridge Lower Secondary 8Ps lists dispersion.

**Laws, equations, standard values** ([CP 25.5 Table 25.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms))
- Water: red (660 nm) 1.331, yellow (580) 1.333, blue (470) 1.338, violet (410) 1.342. Crown glass: 1.512 (red) to 1.530 (violet).
- At 45° incidence into water, red refracts to 32.09° and violet to 31.80°: `python3 -I -c 'import math;r=math.radians(45);print(round(math.degrees(math.asin(math.sin(r)/1.331)),2), round(math.degrees(math.asin(math.sin(r)/1.342)),2))'`. The spread is only about 0.3°, which is why a rainbow needs many drops and a dark background.
- Visible range: "between 400 nm and 750 nm" ([CP 24.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)); "roughly 400 and 700 nm" ([AS 5.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-2-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)); 380–760 nm ([CP 27.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-1-the-wave-aspect-of-light-interference)). Say "about 400 to 700 nm".

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- As above: a 0.3° red–violet split at one water surface.

**Demonstrations**
- A glass of water on a sunny windowsill throws a small spectrum on the floor (a prism substitute; unsourced but standard). Feasible.
- A garden hose or plant-sprayer mist with the Sun behind you makes a rainbow. "You see a rainbow only when you look away from the sun" ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)). Feasible on a balcony.
- **CD rainbow:** BOOKPLAN lists it under 11.1, but its colours come from **diffraction** at the CD's closely spaced tracks, not from dispersion by refraction. PH 17.2 names "diffraction gratings on the surface of CDs" ([PH 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/17-2-applications-of-diffraction-interference-and-coherence)). Either move the CD to 11.5, or keep it in 11.1 with an honest "a different trick, explained in 11.5".

**Concept questions**
- A prism spreads white light to the right. How does its colour order compare with a diffraction grating's on the right? (They are reversed: [CP 27 Conceptual Q15](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions).) This is the right question for the CD.
- Could total internal reflection play a role in rainbows? Is a triple rainbow possible? ([CP 25 Conceptual Q12](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions))
- A clear diamond flashes colours in white light. Why? ([CP 25 Conceptual Q11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-conceptual-questions))
- Order yellow, blue and red by wavelength, then by frequency ([PH 15 concept item 2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-concept-items)).

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Even though rainbows are associated with seven colors, the rainbow is a continuous distribution of colors" ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).
- "There is no dispersion caused by reflection at the back surface" ([CP 25.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/25-5-dispersion-the-rainbow-and-prisms)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The prism adds colour to the light" is the misconception Newton's *experimentum crucis* attacks: a single colour sent through a second prism doesn't change ([history.md §5](../research/history.md)). **True minimal version:** the colours were already in the white light, and the prism only sorts them.
- "White light is pure" is a misconception ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)).
- **CD ≠ prism**, as above. Also avoid PH 15.2's teacher note, which lists "compact discs" as thin-film interference ([PH 15.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-2-the-behavior-of-electromagnetic-radiation)). PH 17.2 correctly calls them diffraction gratings.

**Gaps**
- Newton's 1672 letter isn't in OpenStax or OCW. See [history.md §5](../research/history.md) (Wikisource).

## 11.2 Colour is in the light

**Concept sequence**
1. The retina has three kinds of cone, each sensitive to a different range of wavelengths ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).
2. The thousands of hues we see come from different combinations of how strongly the three cones are stimulated. TV screens use red, green and blue dots ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).
3. An object's colour comes from which wavelengths it absorbs and which it reflects ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).
4. A pure blue object under pure red light looks black, because there is no blue light for it to reflect ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).
5. Mixing *light* (red + green + blue → white) is additive. Mixing *paint* (cyan + yellow → green) is subtractive ([PH 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-1-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
6. Colour constancy: the brain compares an object with its surroundings, so a white cloth looks white under different lamps ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).

**Definitions**
- Subtractive mixing: two pigment primaries (magenta, yellow, cyan) mixed give a secondary colour. Additive mixing has red, green and blue primaries ([PH 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-1-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
- Colour constancy ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision)).
- **Cambridge Lower Secondary 8Ps:** "adding and subtracting colours" ([school-wording.md §2](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Cone ranges as PH gives them: "red" 500–700 nm, "green" 450–630 nm, "blue" 400–500 nm ([PH 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-1-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). They overlap heavily, and the names don't match the peak colours exactly (same page).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- None needed.

**Demonstrations**
- A banana or green leaf under a phone screen set to full red, in the dark ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md); [CP 26 Conceptual Q10](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-conceptual-questions)): "A pure red object on a black background seems to disappear when illuminated with pure green light." Feasible. A phone screen isn't pure red, so expect "dark and odd", not "black".
- Look at a phone or laptop screen through a magnifying glass to see the red, green and blue sub-pixels ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision) describes RGB dots). Feasible.
- PhET "Color Vision": "Make a whole rainbow by mixing red, green, and blue light" ([CP 24.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). Link out.
- **Live:** colour mixer (BOOKPLAN). Show additive and subtractive side by side, as PH 15.1 does with its two colour wheels.

**Concept questions**
- What is colour constancy, and where does it fail? ([CP 26 Conceptual Q11](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-conceptual-questions))
- A feather looks green but has no green pigment. Explain using diffraction ([CP 27 Conceptual Q17](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions)). This links to 11.5; peacock feathers work well in an Indian setting.
- `:::predict`: red light and green light overlap on a white wall. What colour appears?

**Warnings the sources give**
- "An object can appear a different color under non-white illumination." ([CP 26.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/26-3-color-and-color-vision))

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **The section title can mislead.** "Colour is in the light" alone is as wrong as "colour is in the object". CP 26.3 says "the true color of the object is blue, which is independent of illumination." **True minimal version:** an object picks which colours of the incoming light to send back. What you see depends on the light that arrives *and* on what the object does to it.
- "A red shirt is red whatever the light" is the misconception ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)).
- **Source error:** PH 15.1's teacher note says subtractive pigments "absorb all colors but one". A cyan pigment absorbs red and sends back both blue and green, which is why cyan + yellow makes green (that same page's own example). **True minimal version:** each paint takes some colours out, and what's left over is what you see.
- "Red, yellow and blue are *the* primary colours" is wrong for light: the light primaries are red, green and blue (PH 15.1).

**Gaps**
- No OCW coverage of colour vision.

## 11.3 Blue sky, orange sunset

**Concept sequence**
1. Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. It shakes the electrons in air molecules, and they re-radiate like tiny antennas, which is scattering ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization); 8.03 Lec 18 on radiation from an accelerated charge: [Lec 18](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-iii-optics/lecture-18/)).
2. For scatterers much smaller than the wavelength, the amount scattered goes as 1/λ⁴, so short (blue) waves scatter far more ([CP 27 Conceptual Q34](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions)).
3. Look anywhere away from the Sun and you see this scattered light, so the sky looks blue ([CP 27 CQ34](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions); [NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/)).
4. At sunset sunlight crosses much more air. Most of the blue has been scattered out of the direct beam, so reds and yellows reach you ([CP 27 CQ35](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions); [NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/)).
5. Scattered skylight is partly polarised, which you can check by rotating Polaroid sunglasses against the blue sky ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization); 8.03 Lec 20 transcript, sky "roughly a 45 to 90 degree wave from the direction of the sun": [Lec 20](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-iii-optics/lecture-20/)).

**Definitions**
- Polarisation by scattering ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)).
- NASA Space Place: "Blue light is scattered more than the other colors because it travels as shorter, smaller waves." ([NASA Space Place](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/))

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴ for particles much smaller than λ ([CP 27 CQ34](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions)).
- **Blue 450 nm vs red 700 nm:** `python3 -I -c 'print(round((700/450)**4,2))'` → **5.86×** more scattering for blue.
- Violet 400 nm vs blue 450 nm: `python3 -I -c 'print(round((450/400)**4,2))'` → 1.60×. Violet scatters even more, which raises the question of why the sky isn't violet (Gaps).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- How much more air at a low Sun, in the flat-atmosphere approximation (path ∝ 1/sin(elevation)): `python3 -I -c 'import math;print([round(1/math.sin(math.radians(e)),1) for e in (90,30,10,5)])'` → 1, 2, 5.8 and 11.5 times the overhead path at 90°, 30°, 10° and 5°. This approximation overestimates the path very close to the horizon because it ignores Earth's curvature. It is pure geometry and is **not from a source**, so label it if used.

**Demonstrations**
- Milk in water: a few drops of milk in a glass of water, lit by a torch, look bluish from the side and orange when you look through toward the torch ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md); the amount of milk is UNVERIFIED, so adjust). Feasible. Fidelity: milk's fat droplets aren't much smaller than the wavelength, so this *imitates* the sky rather than reproducing Rayleigh scattering exactly. Say "a model of the sky".
- Rotate polarised sunglasses while looking at blue sky about 90° from the Sun: it darkens and brightens ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)). Feasible only with genuinely polarised lenses (misconceptions.md says to test on a phone screen first).
- **Live:** sky-colour shader (BOOKPLAN). The physics to encode is 1/λ⁴ plus path length.

**Concept questions**
- When particles are much smaller than the wavelength, scattering ∝ 1/λ⁴. Is there more scattering at small λ? How does this relate to the blue sky? ([CP 27 CQ34](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions))
- Using the same idea, explain red sunsets ([CP 27 CQ35](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions)).
- `:::predict`: the sky from the Moon's surface (no air) is what colour? This follows from the mechanism.

**Warnings the sources give**
- Multiple scattering can bring light to your eyes from other directions, so skylight is only partly polarised ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The sky is blue because it reflects the sea" is a misconception. **True version:** air itself scatters sunlight, mostly the blue.
- "Blue light bounces off air molecules like balls" is not quite right. **True minimal version:** light makes the tiny charges in air molecules shake, and each shaking molecule sends light out in all directions. Short waves get re-sent much more (CP 27.8, "radiate like small antennae").
- "Only blue is scattered" is false. All colours scatter, blue about 6× more than red.
- "Sunsets are red because the Sun cools down" is false. It is the long path through air.
- "Sunsets are red because of pollution" is not established by any source fetched. Dust and haze can change sunset colours, but the basic orange comes from the long air path. Keep pollution out unless it is sourced.

**Gaps**
- **Why the sky isn't violet** (solar spectrum, eye sensitivity) is not addressed by CP or NASA Space Place, which never mentions violet. If the text raises it, it needs a source.
- Rayleigh's name and the derivation of 1/λ⁴ appear in no OpenStax page fetched (only as Conceptual Q34). 8.03 Lec 18 covers dipole radiation mathematically and would ground it at the gate.

## 11.4 Why the sea is blue

**Concept sequence**
1. Light reflected off water or glass is partly polarised, mostly horizontally. That is the glare ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)).
2. Polaroid sunglasses pass vertical polarisation, so they cut that glare. Rotate them while looking at water and the glare brightens and dims ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization); [PH 15 concept item 7](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-concept-items)).
3. At Brewster's angle the reflected light is completely polarised, and the reflected and refracted rays are at 90° to each other ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization); [8.03 Lec 19 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec19/), "θ_B + θ_T = π/2").
4. A polariser passes I = I₀cos²θ of polarised light ([8.03 Lec 17 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec17/)).
5. Raman, 1921: Rayleigh had said the sea is blue because it reflects the sky. Raman used a Nicol prism (a polariser) to remove the reflected skylight, the sea stayed blue, and he argued the water itself scatters light ([history.md §5](../research/history.md); [misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)).
6. Feynman's Brazil students could recite Brewster's angle but not *see* it in light reflected off the bay ([BOOKPLAN §2](../BOOKPLAN.md), citing *Surely You're Joking*; cite only).

**Definitions**
- Polarisation: "the attribute that a wave's oscillations have a definite direction relative to the direction of propagation of the wave" ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)).
- Brewster's law: tan θ_b = n₂/n₁ ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- **Brewster's angle for water:** `python3 -I -c 'import math;b=math.degrees(math.atan(1.333));print(round(b,1), round(90-b,1))'` → **53.1°** from the normal. That is exactly CP Example 27.9's 53.1° ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)). The refracted ray is at 36.9°. Crown glass gives 56.7° (same example).
- **On the Marine Drive wall:** 53.1° from the vertical normal means a line of sight **36.9° below the horizontal**. Looking at the sea surface about 37° down is where the glare is most polarised and the sunglasses cut it hardest. This is derived from the above with no extra assumption.
- Malus: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.cos(math.radians(45))**2,2), round(math.cos(math.radians(90))**2,3))'` → 0.5 at 45° (CP 27.8 also states "at an angle of 45º, the intensity is reduced to 50%": [CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)) and 0 at 90°. "Unpolarised light through one filter keeps half" is the average of cos²θ over all directions. It is computed here, not stated on the pages fetched: `python3 -I -c 'import math;n=3600;print(round(sum(math.cos(2*math.pi*k/n)**2 for k in range(n))/n,3))'` → 0.5.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example 27.9, as above.
- 8.03 soap-film reflection numbers (n = 1.5): R = −0.2 in amplitude, so 4% of the intensity reflects ([8.03 Lec 20 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec20/)). For water at normal incidence: `python3 -I -c 'r=(1-1.333)/(1+1.333);print(round(r*r*100,1))'` → about 2.0% of light reflects straight back. Most light goes *into* the water, which is why the water's own colour matters.

**Demonstrations**
- **The sunglasses test** (Case): at Marine Drive, look at the sea about 37° below the horizon and tilt your head (or the glasses) 90°. Glare changes; the sea's blue stays ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md); [CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)). Feasible, but **only with genuinely polarised lenses**. Test them first on a phone or laptop screen: LCDs "are based on polarization" ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)), and the screen-test advice is from [misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md). - The "arrow sticks or bounces" picture of why vertical polarisation refracts more is offered only as "a convenient mnemonic"; "the reasons for this phenomenon are beyond the scope of this text" ([CP 27.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-8-polarization)). Don't present it as the mechanism.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "The sea is blue because it reflects the sky" is the misconception, and it was Rayleigh's explanation ([misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md)). Reflection *does* add sky colour, but it isn't the whole story, and Raman's test removed it.
- "Raman proved the sea is blue because of scattering, end of story" goes beyond the sources fetched. **Safe true version:** Raman showed that the blue survives when the reflected skylight is blocked, so the water itself must be sending blue light back, and he argued it was scattering by water molecules. Any statement that water also *absorbs* red light needs a source (Gaps).
- "Polarised sunglasses are just dark glasses" is false. Ordinary tinted lenses won't pass the rotation test, which is why the misconceptions file says to test first.
- "Brewster's angle removes all reflection" is false. It removes all of *one* polarisation (8.03 Lec 19: R = 0 for the in-plane polarisation only).

**Gaps**

- Raman on the *Narkunda* (1921), the Nicol prism, and the letter posted at Bombay: [history.md §5](../research/history.md) (APS, Public Domain Review) and [misconceptions.md §9](../research/misconceptions.md) (Optica OPN, The Wire). Not in OpenStax or OCW.

## 11.5 Light is a wave

**Concept sequence**
1. "The most certain indication of a wave is interference" ([CP 27.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-1-the-wave-aspect-of-light-interference)).
2. Newton's authority kept the particle view in charge. Huygens's principle working wasn't enough to prove light a wave ([CP 27.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-3-youngs-double-slit-experiment); [UP3 3.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/3-1-youngs-double-slit-interference)).
3. Huygens's principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of new wavelets, and this explains the bending of waves round edges, which is diffraction ([CP 27.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-2-huygenss-principle-diffraction); 8.03 Lec 22, "Huygens Principle / Diffraction": [Lec 22](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-iii-optics/lecture-22/)).
4. Young split light into two coherent sources and saw bright and dark fringes ([CP 27.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-3-youngs-double-slit-experiment)). His original was a thin card in a sunbeam, 1803 ([history.md §5](../research/history.md)).
5. Thin films (soap bubbles, oil on a wet road) show colour by interference between their two surfaces ([CP 27.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-7-thin-film-interference); [8.03 Lec 20 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec20/)).
6. Poisson worked through Fresnel's wave theory and showed it predicts a bright spot in the centre of a round shadow, which he took to be nonsense. Arago looked and found it, and Fresnel won the 1819 prize ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/the-spot-in-the-shadow/)).

**Definitions**
- Thin-film interference: interference between light reflected from the two surfaces of a thin film ([CP 27.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-7-thin-film-interference)).
- In a medium the wavelength shrinks (λₙ = λ/n), but the colour doesn't change, because colour goes with frequency ([CP 27.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-1-the-wave-aspect-of-light-interference)).
- **IGCSE 0625** mentions no interference for light in the quoted content. Cambridge Lower Secondary 9Ps lists "interference" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Double slit: d sinθ = mλ for bright fringes ([CP 27.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-3-youngs-double-slit-experiment)).
- Reflection from a higher-index medium flips the wave by half a wavelength (180°) ([CP 27.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-7-thin-film-interference); 8.03 Lec 20 notes, "a flip in amplitude").

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Source example: d = 0.0100 mm, third-order bright fringe at 10.95°, so λ = 633 nm, the He-Ne laser ([CP 27.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-3-youngs-double-slit-experiment)). `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(0.0100e-3*math.sin(math.radians(10.95))/3*1e9))'` → 633.
- Fringe spacing (ASSUMED 550 nm light, 0.5 mm slit spacing, screen 1 m away), small-angle Δy = λL/d: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(550e-9*1/0.5e-3*1e3,2))'` → 1.1 mm. Why fringes are hard to see: they are about a millimetre apart.
- 8.03 soap bubble: a 100 nm film with n = 1.5 is bright at 600 nm (then 200 and 120 nm, both outside the visible) ([8.03 Lec 20 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec20/)). `python3 -I -c 'print([round(4*100*1.5/(2*N-1)) for N in (1,2,3)])'` → [600, 200, 120]. A film about 100 µm thick has so many bright wavelengths that it looks white. As the film gets very thin it goes dark (same notes).
- Visible wavelengths in water: 380/1.333 to 760/1.333 → 285–570 nm ([CP 27.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-1-the-wave-aspect-of-light-interference)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(380/1.333), round(760/1.333))'` → 285, 570.

**Demonstrations**
- Soap bubbles in sunlight. Colours swirl, and a black patch appears just before the bubble pops (the thinnest film) ([CP 27 Conceptual Q27](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-conceptual-questions); [8.03 Lec 20 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/mit8_03scf16_lec20/)). - Oil film on a wet road after monsoon rain ([CP 27.7](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/27-7-thin-film-interference) names oil slicks). Feasible outdoors.
- The CD rainbow belongs here: tracks act as a diffraction grating ([PH 17.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/17-2-applications-of-diffraction-interference-and-coherence)). Feasible with an old CD or DVD.
- Young's card: hold a thin card edge-on in a narrow sunbeam and look for fringes inside its shadow ([history.md §5](../research/history.md)). **Will it work at home?** Unverified. Fringes are about a millimetre apart (as above) and need a small, bright source. A gate should test it before it is printed as an activity.
- Squint at a distant streetlight through two fingers held almost touching, or look through a fine cloth such as a dupatta, to see fringes. Unsourced; gate.
- 8.03 demos "Reflection off Soap Bubbles" and "Single-Slit Diffraction with a Laser" ([soap](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-20-video/), [slit](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/resources/copy_of_lecture-22-video/)).
- **Live:** ripple-tank interference shader (BOOKPLAN §3). UP3 Fig 3.2 shows the ripple-tank photo idea ([UP3 3.1](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/3-1-youngs-double-slit-interference)).

**Warnings the sources give**

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**

## 11.6 The colour that changed

**Concept sequence**
1. Light is an electromagnetic wave. Maxwell's calculated speed matched the measured speed of light ([AS 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-1-the-behavior-of-light); [8.03 Lec 12](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-03sc-physics-iii-vibrations-and-waves-fall-2016/pages/part-ii-electromagnetic-waves/lecture-12/)).
2. The same kind of wave covers radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, all at c in vacuum ([CP 24.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum); [AS 5.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-2-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). This opens the **Ladder of Light**.
3. Ordinary scattering (11.3) gives back the *same* colour. In 1928 Raman and Krishnan found that a small fraction of light scattered by a liquid comes out a *different* colour, and the change depends on the material ([ETHW Milestone plaque](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Raman_Effect)).
4. Raman won the 1930 Nobel Prize "for his work on the scattering of light and for the discovery of the effect named after him" (citation as quoted in [history.md §5](../research/history.md); nobelprize.org returned 403 in this pass).
5. 28 February, the discovery date, is National Science Day in India ([history.md §5](../research/history.md); the date is also on the [ETHW plaque](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Raman_Effect)).

**Definitions**
- Raman effect, plaque wording: "when a beam of coloured light entered a liquid, a fraction of the light scattered was of a different colour, dependent on material property" ([ETHW](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Raman_Effect)).
- EM spectrum bands and their sources ([CP 24.3 Table 24.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
- **IGCSE 0625** topic 3.3 is "Electromagnetic spectrum" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- c = fλ for every EM wave in vacuum ([AS 5.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-1-the-behavior-of-light)).
- Visible light is about 400–700 nm ([AS 5.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-2-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). Ultraviolet starts below 400 nm ([CP 24.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/24-3-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
- Yellow light is about 600 nm ([PH 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-1-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(299792458/600e-9/1e12))'` → about 500 THz.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- J. C. Bose's 5 mm waves (BOOKPLAN Ladder) sit at `python3 -I -c 'print(round(299792458/5e-3/1e9))'` → about 60 GHz. The 5 mm figure is from BOOKPLAN §3, not a fetched source, so a gate must check it.

**Demonstrations**
- See TV-remote infrared through a phone camera (BOOKPLAN 15.1; useful for the Ladder). Feasible.
- No home Raman demonstration is feasible. The shift is a tiny fraction of the light and needs a spectrometer (consistent with the plaque's "a fraction of the light").

**Concept questions**
- Where are gamma rays on the spectrum? ([PH 15 concept item 3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-concept-items))
- Which region is invisible and low-energy? ([PH 15 concept item 4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/15-concept-items))
- `:::predict`: blue light scatters off molecules. Does all of it come back blue?

**Warnings the sources give**
- None specific to the Raman effect in OpenStax or OCW.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- "Raman discovered why the sky is blue" is false. That was scattering that keeps the colour (Rayleigh, 11.3). Raman found the rare scattering that *changes* it.
- "All scattered light changes colour" is false. Only a small fraction does (ETHW plaque, "a fraction").
- "Krishnan just helped" undersells him. history.md records that Raman himself called it the "Raman-Krishnan effect" on 9 February 1928 (Krishnan's diary). Name Krishnan properly.
