## 12.1 Rubbing moves charge

**Concept sequence**
1. There are two kinds of charge. Rubbing some material pairs in controlled ways always leaves one kind on one material and the opposite kind on the other ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
2. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. The names come from Franklin's convention: glass rubbed with silk is called positive ([8.02 notes §2.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap2coulomb_law/)).
3. Matter is made of protons (+e) and electrons (−e). A neutral object holds equal amounts of each ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
4. Charge is conserved. It "can neither be created nor destroyed", but it "can… be transferred from one body to another" ([8.02 notes §2.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap2coulomb_law/)).
5. In solids the electrons are what move. Taking negative charge away leaves the same amount of positive charge behind ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
6. Charging by contact: when two different materials touch, the one that holds electrons more tightly takes some with it. "Rubbing two surfaces together increases the transfer of electrons, because it creates a closer contact between the materials." ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge))
7. Conductors let charge move; insulators don't ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
8. Polarisation: a charged object pushes or pulls the charges inside a neutral one without touching it, so the neutral object is attracted. A water stream bends this way ([PH 18.1 Snap Lab "Polarizing Tap Water"](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).

**Definitions**
- Electric charge: "a property of matter that causes objects to attract or repel each other" ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- Conductor and insulator: charge "can easily move" through a conductor, such as a metal, and "cannot move" through an insulator, such as rubber ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- Polarisation: the separation of charge inside an object that has no net charge ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- **IGCSE 0625** lists "charge" under 4.2 Electrical quantities, per [school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md). Electrostatics wording beyond that is not quoted in our file.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C ([8.02 notes eq 2.1.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap2coulomb_law/); [PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- Electrons in one coulomb: `python3 -I -c 'print("%.3e"%(1/1.602e-19))'` → 6.242e+18. PH 18.1 writes this as 6.25 × 10¹⁸ protons.
- Conservation: q_initial = q_final ([PH 18.1 eq 18.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- PH ink-drop example: a drop carrying −1 × 10⁻¹⁰ C has gained about 6 × 10⁸ electrons. Each extra electron is shared among about 10⁷ atoms ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)). `python3 -I -c 'print("%.2e"%(1e-10/1.602e-19), "%.0e"%(1e16/6e8))'` → 6.24e+08 2e+07. OpenStax rounds the second number to 10⁷.
- Conservation example: +4 C and +8 C spheres touch. Afterwards one has +10 C, so the other has +2 C ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- Mumbai version: there are no sourced numbers for the charge on a balloon. Keep this qualitative.

**Demonstrations**
- PH Snap Lab "Like and Unlike Charges" uses two pieces of tape, about 4 cm each. Peeled side by side, they repel. Stacked and then peeled apart, they attract ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)). Mumbai flat: feasible and safe, and best on a dry day.
- PH Snap Lab "Polarizing Tap Water": rub a comb with a dry cloth and bring it near a thin stream from a tap ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)). A balloon works the same way (BOOKPLAN 12.1). Feasible and safe.
- PH teacher demo: a rubbed glass rod or comb, plus a balloon charged on hair ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- The PhET sim "Balloons and Static Electricity" is linked from [PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge). Link out only.
- Van de Graaff (described only, not for home): it is safe at science fairs "because they deliver relatively little electric current" ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).

**Concept questions**
- Two strips of tape repel after step 4 and attract after step 9. Why? ([PH 18.1 Grasp Check](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)) Good `:::predict`: will the stacked strips attract or repel?
- Why does the water curve towards the charged comb? Because the comb attracts the polarised water molecules and dissolved ions ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- True or false: a polarised object must have a net charge. False ([PH 18.1 Check Your Understanding Q7](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- Do field lines show the paths charges take? No. The 8.02T answer note says "Particles do NOT move along field lines." ([8.02T PRS02](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02t-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2005/resources/prs_w01d2/))

**Warnings the sources give**
- Insulators can be forced to conduct, but "the insulator is often physically destroyed in the process" ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Rubbing makes charge."** False. Rubbing moves electrons from one material to the other. The total stays the same ([8.02 notes §2.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap2coulomb_law/)).
- **"Positive charges move onto the glass."** False in solids. Electrons leave, and the positive charge is what is left behind ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)).
- **OpenStax slip.** PH 18.1 says the child on the slide has "excess positive charge", then two sentences later says "the excess negative charge on each strand" ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)). Don't copy that paragraph. The safe true version: like charges on neighbouring hairs push apart.
- **"Static is a different kind of electricity"** (misconceptions.md §10). It is the same charge, just sitting still instead of flowing.
- **Humidity.** The sources say only this much: excess electrons can be "carried away in humid air", and the carpet spark happens "on a dry day" ([PH 18.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/18-1-electrical-charges-conservation-of-charge-and-transfer-of-charge)). The minimal true version is "in damp air the extra charge leaks away faster, so the effect is weaker". Don't claim a mechanism such as water films or ions in the air. Neither source gives one (see Gaps).

**Gaps**
- No OCW or OpenStax source explains *why* humidity drains static charge, or how fast. BOOKPLAN wants "why it is weaker in Mumbai's monsoon humidity". Find a sourced mechanism, or keep the statement observational.
- There is no triboelectric series in the sources fetched, so we can't say which material ends up positive for balloon and hair.

## 12.2 A loop, not a pipe

**Concept sequence**
1. A battery has two terminals at different potentials. Join them with a conductor and charge flows ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
2. Circuits are "complete, closed pathways through which electric current flows" ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
3. An open circuit has no current, and a closed one does. A switch opens and closes the loop ([8.02 notes §7.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/); [PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
4. The battery's chemistry only runs when the loop is complete. CP says of the lead-acid cell: "the reaction will not take place unless there is a complete circuit" ([CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).
5. The same charges go round and come back: "The electric current is the same at all points in this circuit, including inside the battery" ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).

**Definitions**
- Electric current: "the rate at which electric charge moves", I = ΔQ/Δt, in amperes. 1 A = 1 C/s ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- Conventional current: the direction a positive charge would flow. In metal wires the electrons move the other way ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- **IGCSE 0625 4.2.2**: "electric current is related to the flow of charge". It also says "conventional current is from positive to negative and that the flow of free electrons is from negative to positive" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Cambridge Lower Secondary 7Pe.01 says "a flow of electrons around a circuit" (same file).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- I = ΔQ/Δt ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- PH lightning example: 10²⁰ electrons in 2 ms. `python3 -I -c 'print(1e20*1.60e-19/2e-3)'` → 8000.0 A (PH 19.1 gives the sign as negative; current runs ground → cloud).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- PH pea-straw model: count the peas pushed through a straw in 30 s and divide by the time to get a "pea current" ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)). Mumbai flat: dried chana in a straw. Feasible and safe.

**Demonstrations**
- PH 19.1 Snap Lab (pea straw), as above. Push one pea in and another comes out *at once*. This is the seed of the "the wire is already full" idea.
- One-wire challenge (BOOKPLAN 12.2): an LED, two AA cells, and one lead versus two leads. Safe at AA voltage. This demo is ours, not from a source. Mark it so in provenance.

**Concept questions**
- PH 19.1 Grasp Check: peas charged to 1 nC pass at four per second. What is the current? (4 nC/s = 4 nA) ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- True or false: we can assume the voltage is the same at every point in a given wire ([PH 19.2 Check](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).

**Warnings the sources give**
- PH notes the limit of its own pea model: peas move by bumping, but "electrons push each other along due to mutually repulsive electrostatic forces" ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **The water-pipe picture is OpenStax's own analogy** ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law), [PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)). It also feeds the misconception "electricity flows through hollow wires" (misconceptions.md §10). If we use it, say once where it stops:
  - the wire is solid metal that is already full of movable electrons;
  - nothing pours in from the battery;
  - cut the wire and nothing spills.
- **"The battery is full of electricity/charge that runs out."** Charge is conserved and circulates (8.02 §2.1; PH 19.2). The emf source moves charge "from lower potential to the higher one" ([8.02 notes §7.2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)). What runs out is the chemical energy.
- **OpenStax error on ground.** PH 19.2 calls Earth "a large, essentially neutral object containing an infinite amount of charge" ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)). It is not infinite. Say "so big that adding or removing some charge barely changes it."

**Gaps**
- No OCW or OpenStax page tests the one-wire idea directly. The research file traces it to Fredette & Lochhead 1980 ([misconceptions.md §10](../research/misconceptions.md)).

## 12.3 What gets used up?

**Concept sequence**
1. In a series loop the current is the same at every point: "The electric current neither speeds up in the wires nor slows down in the resistor. This would create points where too much or too little charge would be bunched up" ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
2. "By current conservation, the same current I is flowing through each resistor" ([8.02 notes §7.3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)).
3. What changes around the loop is the potential. It rises across the battery and falls across each resistor ([8.02 notes §7.2, Fig 7.2.3b](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/); [PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
4. Energy is what gets delivered. Crossing a resistor, "the potential energy is converted into thermal energy in the resistor". The source's power equals I²R + I²r, "required by energy conservation" ([8.02 notes §7.2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)).
5. Electrons drift slowly, but the signal travels fast ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)).

**Definitions**
- Drift velocity: "the average velocity of the free charges" ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current); [8.02 notes ch 6 §6.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chapter6current/)).
- **IGCSE** calls the energy that moves "electrical work done" by "electrical currents", one of its energy transfers (IGCSE 1.7.1, [school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- I = nqAv_d ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current); [8.02 notes ch 6](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chapter6current/)).
- CP gives drift "on the order of 10⁻⁴ m/s" and signals "about 10⁸ m/s", so the signal is roughly 10¹² times faster ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)).
- P = IV = I²R = V²/R ([PH 19.4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-4-electric-power)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- CP Example 20.3: a 12-gauge copper wire, d = 2.053 mm, carrying 20.0 A, with one free electron per atom and ρ = 8.80 × 10³ kg/m³. The answer is v_d = 4.53 × 10⁻⁴ m/s ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)). Re-done: `python3 -I -c 'import math;n=8.80e3/63.54e-3*6.02e23;A=math.pi*(2.053e-3/2)**2;print("%.3e"%(20/(n*1.60e-19*A)))'` → 4.529e-04 m/s. At that speed an electron needs 1/4.53e-4 ≈ 2208 s, about 37 min, to drift 1 m.
- Mumbai version (ASSUMED: 15 mA LED current, 0.5 mm copper hook-up wire): `python3 -I -c 'import math;n=8.80e3/63.54e-3*6.02e23;A=math.pi*(0.25e-3)**2;v=0.015/(n*1.60e-19*A);print("%.2e m/s, %.0f h per metre"%(v,1/v/3600))'` → 5.73e-06 m/s, 49 h per metre. So the LED lights at once, but an electron would take about two days to crawl one metre of wire.

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Note that simple drift velocity is not the entire story." The individual electrons move much faster than the drift velocity ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)).
- The fast signal "is a rapidly propagating change in electric field" ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Electrons race round the circuit from the battery to the bulb."** False. Their drift is about 10⁻⁴ m/s or slower. The push travels at about 10⁸ m/s because the wire is already full of electrons ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)). Minimal true version: "every electron in the loop starts moving almost at once, but each one only creeps."
- **"Electrons are standing still and only the signal moves."** Also false. They move fast and randomly, and drift slowly on average ([CP 20.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-1-current)).
- **"The bulb uses up current."** False (PH 19.2). The bulb turns electrical energy into light and heat (8.02 §7.2).
- **"Energy is carried by the electrons like buckets."** Not in our sources, and contested. school-wording.md's "physics truth" column says "the energy is carried by the circuit's fields". Don't introduce a bucket model. Say what the sources say: the battery does work on the charges, and the bulb converts that energy.

**Gaps**
- No OCW or OpenStax source explains the field picture of energy flow in a DC circuit at a level we can use. Keep it to "energy is delivered" and don't describe the mechanism.

## 12.4 Push and resistance

**Concept sequence**
1. The battery is a "charge pump" that moves charges from lower to higher potential. Its emf ε is the work done per unit charge, measured in volts (J/C) ([8.02 Class 12 summary](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/summary_w05d1-1/)).
2. Resistance describes how much a component "opposes the flow of charge through it" ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
3. Ohm found that current is proportional to voltage, which gives V = IR. It is an empirical law that "holds for most materials and at common temperatures" ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)). Some materials are non-ohmic ([8.02 notes §6.2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chapter6current/)).
4. A longer wire has more resistance and a thicker wire less: R = ρL/A ([8.02T PRS10 "Resistance"](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02t-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2005/resources/prs_w04d2/)).
5. A real battery has an internal resistance r in series with its emf. Its terminal voltage V = ε − Ir falls as the current rises, so I = ε/(R + r) ([8.02 notes §7.2](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/); [CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).
6. So a battery sets a *push* (roughly), not a *current*. The current depends on what you connect ([CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).

**Definitions**
- emf: "the potential difference of a source when no current is flowing". "Emf is not a force at all" ([CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).
- Ohm: 1 Ω = 1 V/A ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- **IGCSE 0625 4.2** uses the terms "e.m.f. and p.d." and "resistance" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Our "voltage" is IGCSE's p.d. (potential difference). Cambridge Lower Secondary 9Pe uses "resistance = voltage/current" (same file).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- V = IR; R = V/I ([PH 19.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-1-ohms-law)).
- V_terminal = ε − Ir; I = ε/(R + r) ([CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).
- **AA alkaline cell** (Energizer E91 datasheet): "Nominal Voltage: 1.5 volts". "Nominal IR: 150 to 300 milliohms (fresh)" ([Energizer E91](https://data.energizer.com/pdfs/e91.pdf)). Manufacturer data, not OpenStax or OCW.
- **9 V battery** (Energizer 522): "Nominal Voltage: 9.0 volts" ([Energizer 522](https://data.energizer.com/pdfs/522.pdf)).
- **LED forward voltages** at I_F = 20 mA (min / typ / max):
  - red 1.8 / 2.0 / 2.4 V and blue 2.8 / 3.4 / 4.0 V, from OptoSupply OSRB5131AH-C, 5 mm ([Rapid 56-0352 datasheet](https://static.rapidonline.com/pdf/56-0352.pdf));
  - red 1.8 / 2.1 / 2.6 V and blue 2.9 / 3.1 / 3.6 V ([Rapid 56-0353](https://static.rapidonline.com/pdf/56-0353_v1.pdf));
  - white 2.8 / 3.3 / 3.8 V, from OptoSupply OSPW5111A-Z3, 5 mm ([Rapid 55-2216](https://static.rapidonline.com/pdf/55-2216.pdf));
  - white 2.7 / 3.4 / 4.0 V, from Multicomp MCL053SWC-WH1 ([Farnell datasheet](https://www.farnell.com/datasheets/2861562.pdf)).
  - Absolute maximums on both OptoSupply sheets: DC forward current 30 mA and reverse voltage 5 V (Rapid 56-0352, 55-2216).
- Resistivity table (Ω·m): copper 1.72 × 10⁻⁸, nichrome 100 × 10⁻⁸ ([8.02 notes Table 6.2.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chapter6current/)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**

- **"Emf is a force."** The name is historical: "Emf is not a force at all" ([CP 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-2-electromotive-force-terminal-voltage)).
- **"Voltage is the cause, current the effect, always."** PH 19.1 offers this as a way of viewing Ohm's law. Keep it, but only for ohmic parts.
- **The misconceptions.md series-LED demo fails on 2 × AA** (see the worked numbers). Use 9 V and a resistor.

## 12.5 Series and parallel

**Concept sequence**
1. Series means one after another in the same branch, so the same current flows through each part. R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + … ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
2. Going once round a loop, the voltage drops add up to the battery's rise ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
3. Parallel means both ends of each part are joined, so each has the same voltage. The currents add (conservation of charge), and 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … ([PH 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-3-parallel-circuits)).
4. More paths make the total resistance smaller. Three identical resistors in parallel give R/3 ([PH 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-3-parallel-circuits)).
5. "Generally, loads are connected in parallel across the power supply" ([8.02 notes §7.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)). Power systems use parallel "to allow them to operate independently… The same is true in your house" ([CP 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/21-1-resistors-in-series-and-parallel)).

**Definitions**
- Equivalent resistance: the single resistance that would carry the same current from the same source ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)).
- **IGCSE / Lower Secondary**: 9Pe covers "parallel circuits"; 7Pe covers "measuring current in series" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- PH Example: 12 V across 1.0, 6.0 and 13 Ω in series ([PH 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/19-2-series-circuits)). `python3 -I -c 'R=(1.0,6.0,13);I=12/sum(R);print(sum(R),I,[round(I*r,2) for r in R])'` → 20.0 Ω, 0.6 A, drops [0.6, 3.6, 7.8] V. The drops add to 12 V.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- 8.02T PRS10 "Bulbs and Batteries" (ideal battery, identical bulbs) ([8.02T PRS10](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02t-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2005/resources/prs_w04d2/)):

  - Add a second bulb in series: the current is "Lower", it halves, and each bulb is "¼ as bright".
  - The ¼ comes from P = I²R with I halved: `python3 -I -c 'print(0.5**2)'` → 0.25.
- 8.02 notes §7.9.4: in parallel, "the one with smaller resistance will be brighter since more power is delivered to it" ([8.02 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)).

## 12.6 Safe at home

**Concept sequence**
1. Electricity has two hazards. The **thermal** hazard is overheating; the **shock** hazard is current through a person ([CP 23.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/23-8-electrical-safety-systems-and-devices); [CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).
2. A short circuit is "an undesired low-resistance path across a voltage source", and it gives a large current. A fuse or circuit breaker in series opens when the current is too high ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body); [8.02 notes §7.1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/resources/chap7dc_circuits/)).
3. The effect of a shock depends on the current through the body. Most of the body's resistance is dry skin, and wet skin lowers it a lot ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).
4. The three-wire system: the neutral is earthed, and the metal case of an appliance is earthed too. If a live wire touches the case, current flows to earth and the breaker trips instead of the person carrying it ([CP 23.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/23-8-electrical-safety-systems-and-devices)).
5. A ground fault interrupter compares live and neutral currents and trips if they differ ([CP 23.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/23-8-electrical-safety-systems-and-devices)). India's regulation calls this a residual current device (CEA 2023, below).
6. Indian mains is AC at 50 Hz. Its declared single-phase voltage is 230 V, with the older figure of 240 V written alongside in some documents (see the values below).

**Definitions**
- Fuse: "a metal strip with a low melting point that, when overheated by an excessive current, permanently breaks the connection". Circuit breaker: "an automatic but restorable electric switch" ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).
- Earthing (Indian regulation): "connection of the exposed conductive and extraneous parts of an installation to the main earthing terminal of that installation" ([CEA Safety Regulations 2023, reg 2](https://kseb.in/uploads/Downloadtemsuppy/Central%20Electricity%20Authority%20(Measures%20relating%20to%20Safety%20and%20Electric%20Supply)%20Regulations,%202023-1699594973944568556.pdf)).
- "MCB" (miniature circuit breaker) is the everyday Indian name. It does not appear in the CEA text I searched. Treat it as everyday wording, and use "circuit breaker" (CP) as the physics term.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- **Maharashtra (Mumbai) supply.** Low-voltage supply is "Alternating current single phase or… three phase-Four Wire, 50 cycles". Installations are classed as "Two wires, single phase, 230 / 240 volts" (MERC Supply Code Regulations 2021, §3.1–3.2; [MERC 2021](https://merc.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Supply-Code-Regulations-2021-English.pdf)). The permitted mean r.m.s. band is "Un +10 % / −15 %" (same document, Table 1).
- **BIS standard.** IS 12360:1988 Amendment No. 4 (November 2011) says: "The changeover of system voltage from 240/415 to 230/400 shall be kept in abeyance for the time being." ([IS 12360 via law.resource.org](https://law.resource.org/pub/in/bis/S05/is.12360.1988.pdf))
  - So 230 V is the declared figure in the regulations, while the BIS changeover from 240 V is formally on hold. Write "about 230 V, 50 Hz" and don't claim more precision.
- **RCD in homes**: "in domestic installation, residual current device having residual operating current not exceeding 30 milliampere shall be used" (CEA (Measures relating to Safety and Electric Supply) Regulations 2023, reg 44, notified 8 June 2023; [CEA 2023 via KSEB](https://kseb.in/uploads/Downloadtemsuppy/Central%20Electricity%20Authority%20(Measures%20relating%20to%20Safety%20and%20Electric%20Supply)%20Regulations,%202023-1699594973944568556.pdf)).
- **Earthed terminal**: the supplier must provide "a suitable earthed terminal" on the consumer's premises (CEA 2023, reg 18; same URL).
- **Shock thresholds** (60 Hz, 1 s, through the trunk; [CP 20.6 Table 20.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)):

  | Current | Effect |
  |---|---|
  | 1 mA | threshold of sensation |
  | 5 mA | "maximum harmless current" |
  | 10–20 mA | cannot let go |
  | 100–300 mA | ventricular fibrillation possible |

- Body resistance: about 200 kΩ with dry skin and 10.0 kΩ soaking wet ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).
- CP states that the body "is most sensitive to frequencies near the 50- or 60-Hz frequencies in common use" ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- CP example (US 120 V): wet skin gives 12 mA, "above the 'can't let go' threshold" ([CP 20.6](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-6-electric-hazards-and-the-human-body)).
- Mumbai 230 V, using CP's resistances (ASSUMED transferable): `python3 -I -c 'print(230/10e3*1e3, 230/200e3*1e3)'` → 23.0 mA wet and 1.15 mA dry. Wet is above can't-let-go, and that is why wet hands and mains never mix.
- Peak of 230 V r.m.s. AC: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(230*math.sqrt(2),1), 1000/50)'` → 325.3 V, with a period of 20.0 ms. CP 20.5 gives V_rms = V₀/√2 ([CP 20.5](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/20-5-alternating-current-versus-direct-current)).
- MERC band on 230 V: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(230*1.10,1), round(230*0.85,1))'` → 253.0 V to 195.5 V.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"It's the current that kills, not the voltage."** Half true, and dangerous if left at that. Current does the harm, but voltage is what drives current through the body (CP 20.6, I = V/R). Say both.
- **"An MCB protects people from shocks."** A breaker or fuse protects against overcurrent and heating. The 30 mA RCD (CEA reg 44) and the earthing are what protect against shock ([CP 23.8](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/23-8-electrical-safety-systems-and-devices)).
- **"Indian mains is exactly 230 V."** The regulations allow 230/240 V and a +10/−15 % band (MERC 2021).
- **US numbers.** OpenStax's 120 V / 60 Hz examples must be redone for 230 V / 50 Hz.

## Case: *The LED that wouldn't light*
Two sourced reasons:
1. **Too little push.** A white or blue LED's typical forward voltage (3.3–3.4 V) is above two AA cells' nominal 3.0 V ([Rapid 55-2216](https://static.rapidonline.com/pdf/55-2216.pdf); [Energizer E91](https://data.energizer.com/pdfs/e91.pdf)).
2. **Wrong way round.** An LED is a diode: the datasheets rate it for reverse voltage, not reverse current flow.

Also check for an open loop (12.2). The fix is a red LED with 68 Ω on 2 × AA, or a white LED on 9 V with a resistor (`python3 -I -c 'print((9-3.3)/0.015)'` → 380 Ω for 15 mA, ASSUMED V_F = 3.3 V).
