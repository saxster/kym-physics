## 14.1 Day and night

**Concept sequence**
1. Whether the sky turns or Earth turns can't be told just from watching the stars rise and set ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).
2. From the 17th century, astronomers generally accepted that Earth turns. Foucault gave "an unambiguous demonstration" in the 19th century ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).
3. A pendulum keeps swinging in the same plane while Earth turns beneath it, so the swing seems to rotate ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).
4. Latitude and longitude locate a place, for example Mumbai at 19.08° N. The sky has a matching grid of declination and right ascension ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).

**Definitions**
- Latitude: "the number of degrees of arc you are away from the equator along your meridian" ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.1.1**: Earth "rotates on its axis, which is tilted, once in approximately 24 hours" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Foucault, 1851: "a 60-meter pendulum with a mass of about 25 kilograms from the dome of the Pantheon in Paris" ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)).
- Earth's rotation: 360° in about 24 h is 15° per hour. `python3 -I -c 'print(360/24)'` → 15.0. This is pure arithmetic on the IGCSE "approximately 24 hours".

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Mumbai's speed round Earth's axis (ASSUMED: spherical Earth, R = 6371 km from [NASA EF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html) volumetric mean radius; 24 h day). `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(2*math.pi*6371*math.cos(math.radians(19.08))/24))'` → 1576 km/h. This makes Aryabhata's boat vivid: you don't feel the speed, only changes in it (Ch 2).

**Demonstrations**
- AS Collaborative Group Activity A asks for other evidence that Earth, not the sky, is turning ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky)). Seed for a `:::check`.
- Home Foucault pendulum: not feasible meaningfully in a flat. The effect at 19° N is slow. Describe it, don't build it.

**Concept questions**
- "Can you think of other pieces of evidence that indicate that it is Earth and not the sky that is turning?" ([AS 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-1-earth-and-sky))

**Warnings the sources give**
- None specific.

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **Foucault numbers disagree.** AS 4.1 says 60 m and about 25 kg. history.md's true-stories list says a 67 m wire and a 28 kg bob (Comptes Rendus 2017). Use the history.md primary-research figure or hedge ("about 60–67 m"). The gate decides.
- **"Day and night happen because the Sun goes round us."** That is the alternative AS 4.1 sets up. Seeing alone can't decide it, and that is exactly Aryabhata's boat point.
- **"Earth spins once every 24 hours exactly."** IGCSE says "approximately". The sidereal vs solar day distinction is in AS 4.3, which was not fetched. Avoid exactness.

**Gaps**
- Aryabhata (499 CE) is in history.md §1 only. No OCW or OpenStax source.

## 14.2 Why seasons happen

**Concept sequence**
1. The common belief is that distance from the Sun causes the seasons. But the distance "varies by only about 3%", Earth is closest in January, and the two hemispheres have opposite seasons ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
2. The axis is tilted by 23.5° (AS's rounding) and points the same way in space all year. In June the Northern Hemisphere "leans into" the Sun ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
3. **Two effects** ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)):
   - (a) A high Sun puts its light on a smaller patch of ground. AS uses a flashlight held straight vs slanted.
   - (b) Longer days give more hours of heating.
4. At the solstices the Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer (June) or the Tropic of Capricorn (December). At the equinoxes it is over the equator, with about 12 h of day everywhere ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
5. Near the equator, "all seasons are much the same… Local residents define the seasons by the amount of rain (wet season and dry season) rather than by the amount of sunlight" ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)). Mumbai, at 19° N, is inside the tropics, so this is the bridge to "Mumbai's year is shaped by the monsoon".
6. The warmest months come after the solstice because air and water take time to heat ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).

**Definitions**
- Solstice and equinox, as used in AS 4.2 ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.1.1** links seasons to the yearly orbit: Earth "orbits the Sun once in approximately 365 days and use this to explain the periodic nature of the seasons". **Tilt is not explicitly linked to seasons** in the syllabus ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Bridge sentence (same file): "The syllabus says the yearly orbit explains why seasons repeat. The reason seasons happen at all is the tilt."

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Tilt 23.44° ([NASA EF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html)). AS rounds it to 23.5° and sometimes 23°.
- Perihelion 147.095 and aphelion 152.100 million km ([NASA EF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html)). The timing ("closest to the Sun in January") is from [AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons).
- Noon Sun altitude = 90° − |latitude − solar declination|. AS 4.2 does the equator and Tropic of Cancer cases this way ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- **Distance effect is only about 7%.**
  - Distance ratio: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(152.100/147.095,4))'` → 1.034, so aphelion is 3.4% farther.
  - Sunlight (inverse square): `python3 -I -c 'print(round((152.100/147.095)**2,4))'` → 1.0692. That is about 6.9% *more* sunlight in early January than early July, and it arrives in Mumbai's coolest month.
  - AS's "about 3%" refers to the distance, not the sunlight.
- **Noon Sun at Mumbai (19.08° N)**, with declination ±23.44° at the solstices and 0° at the equinoxes:
  - `python3 -I -c 'p=19.08;e=23.44;print(round(90-abs(p-e),2),round(90-p,2),round(90-(p+e),2))'` → 85.64 70.92 47.48, that is 85.64° (June), 70.92° (equinox) and 47.48° (December).
  - In June the noon Sun stands 4.36° **north** of overhead, because Mumbai is south of the Tropic of Cancer.
  - BOOKPLAN's "86° vs 48°" is confirmed.
- **Same sunlight spread on flat ground**: `python3 -I -c 'import math;print(round(math.sin(math.radians(85.64))/math.sin(math.radians(47.48)),3))'` → 1.353. Noon light per square metre is about 35% stronger in June than in December, before clouds. The monsoon clouds then cut it, which is ASSUMED and qualitative.
- **Mumbai day length**: cos H = −tan φ tan δ (standard spherical astronomy). The formula is not shown in AS 4.2, but AS gives the 15 h and 9 h US examples.
  - `python3 -I -c 'import math;p=math.radians(19.08);d=math.radians(23.44);print(round(2*math.degrees(math.acos(-math.tan(p)*math.tan(d)))/15,2),round(2*math.degrees(math.acos(math.tan(p)*math.tan(d)))/15,2))'` → 13.15 10.85 hours (geometric).
  - With the usual −0.833° allowance for refraction and the Sun's disc (ASSUMED standard value; AS 4.2 confirms that refraction lets us see "over the horizon"), the figures are about 13.28 h and 10.98 h.
  - Either way, June's day is about 2.3 h longer than December's.
- **Zero-shadow days at Mumbai.** The Sun is exactly overhead when its declination equals +19.08°. That happens twice a year, around mid-May and late July.
  - This uses an ASSUMED approximate declination formula δ ≈ −23.44° cos(360°/365 × (N + 10)), good to about 1–2 days.
  - `python3 -I -c 'import math,datetime as D;print([str(D.date(2026,1,1)+D.timedelta(N-1)) for N in range(1,366) if (-23.44*math.cos(math.radians(360/365*(N+10)))-19.08)*(-23.44*math.cos(math.radians(360/365*(N+11)))-19.08)<=0])'` → ['2026-05-16', '2026-07-27'].
  - Confirm against an almanac before printing. A gate should use a published zero-shadow-day list.

**Demonstrations**
- AS: "shining a flashlight" straight down and then slanted on a surface ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)). Mumbai flat: a phone torch on graph paper, counting squares lit. Feasible and safe.
- Shadow-stick log at noon through the year, or on a zero-shadow day. Feasible. Never look at the Sun.
- AS links the University of Nebraska Seasons Simulation and the Columbia Motions of the Sun Simulator ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)). Link out. The Sky explorer is ours.

**Concept questions**
- "Suppose the tilt of Earth's axis were 16°. What, then, would be the difference in latitude between the Arctic Circle and the Tropic of Cancer?" Answer: 58° ([AS 4.2 Check Your Learning](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
- "What is the altitude of the Sun at noon on December 21, as seen from a place on the Tropic of Cancer?" Answer: 44° ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)). Mumbai variant: 47.48°.
- `:::predict` (the hook): "Earth is 5 million km closer to the Sun in January. Should Mumbai's January be hotter?"

**Warnings the sources give**
- The summer solstice "is not the warmest day of the year, even if it is the longest" ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).
- At the equinoxes the Sun is up "a few minutes longer than 12 hours" because of refraction ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Seasons happen because Earth is closer in summer."** False ([AS 4.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-2-the-seasons)). It is "by far the most common explanation" (IOP, via [misconceptions.md §12](../research/misconceptions.md)).
- **"The tilted hemisphere is closer to the Sun, so it's hotter."** A subtler version of the same error. A few thousand km closer is nothing against 150 million km. AS gives the two real effects: angle and day length.
- **"The noon Sun is always in the south."** Not in Mumbai between mid-May and late July, when it passes north of overhead (computed above).
- **"Mumbai has summer and winter like Europe."** AS 4.2 says that near the equator, seasons are defined by rain. Mumbai is tropical but not equatorial. Keep "shaped more by the monsoon" (BOOKPLAN), and note the 2.3 h day-length swing is real but mild.
- **"Tilt 23.5° exactly."** It is 23.44° (NASA). AS rounds.

## 14.3 Phases of the Moon

**Concept sequence**
1. The Moon shines only by reflected sunlight ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
2. Over a month the Sun's direction barely changes, while the Moon goes once round Earth ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
3. Half the Moon is always lit. The phase is how much of that lit half faces us ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
4. The phases run new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full → waning → third quarter → new, in about 29.5 days ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
5. When the Moon rises depends on its phase. The full Moon rises at sunset and is highest at midnight; the first quarter rises around noon ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
6. The Moon spins once per orbit, so the same face always points at us ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).

**Definitions**
- Sidereal month 27.3217 d; solar (phase) month 29.5306 d ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon); [NASA MF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.1.1.3**: "approximately one month for the Moon to orbit the Earth… cycle of phases". "Dark side" is not used ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- The Moon moves about 12° a day against the Sun's direction ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(360/29.5306,2), round(360/27.3217,2))'` → 12.19 13.18 degrees/day, relative to the Sun and to the stars.
- The Moon is "30 Earth-diameters away" ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(384400/(2*6371),1))'` → 30.2.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- The Moon rises later each day by about 24 h × 12.19/360: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(24*60*12.19/360))'` → 49 min. This is approximate: it ignores the Moon's orbital tilt and latitude effects.

**Demonstrations**
- AS: stand about 6 ft from a bright light in a dark room, hold a tennis ball or orange at arm's length, and turn slowly ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)). This matches misconceptions.md §12. Mumbai flat: a single bulb, an orange, a dark room. Safe.
- "Orbit your roommate" to show synchronous rotation ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)). Use a sibling or parent.
- 8.282J PS1 Problem 2: at first quarter the Earth–Moon–Sun angle is 90°. Aristarchus used this to estimate the Sun's distance ([8.282J PS1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/resources/ps1/)). Stretch box only.

**Concept questions**
- `:::predict`: "Is the crescent Moon's dark part Earth's shadow?" (41–44% say yes, [misconceptions.md §12](../research/misconceptions.md)). AS 4.5 says people think phases are "somehow related to the shadow of Earth".
- "When is the full Moon highest in the sky?" At midnight ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).

**Warnings the sources give**
- AS warns that its own Fig 4.14 misleads: at full, the Moon *looks* as if Earth would block it, but the figure is not to scale ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
- "Dark side" is a misunderstanding. Every side gets sunlight in turn ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
- Folklore about the full Moon and behaviour: hospital and police statistics show no correlation ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Phases are Earth's shadow."** False. Earth's shadow on the Moon is an eclipse (14.4) ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
- **"The Moon doesn't rotate."** It rotates once per orbit ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)).
- **"The orbit takes 29.5 days."** The orbit relative to the stars takes 27.3 days. The phases repeat every 29.5 days because Earth has moved meanwhile ([AS 4.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-5-phases-and-motions-of-the-moon)). IGCSE's "approximately one month" covers both.
- **Our own diagrams** must not repeat AS Fig 4.14's scale problem. Show the Moon about 30 Earth-diameters away, or label "not to scale".

**Gaps**
- None for the physics. The phase names in Hindi or Marathi (amavasya, purnima) are not sourced here.

## 14.4 Eclipses

**Concept sequence**
1. A coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so the two look nearly the same size ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
2. Shadows have a dark umbra and a lighter penumbra ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
3. A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow on Earth, and it happens at new Moon. A lunar eclipse is Earth's shadow on the Moon, and it happens at full Moon ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
4. **Why not every month:** "the Moon's orbit is tilted relative to the plane of Earth's orbit about the Sun by about 5°". Most months the Moon passes above or below. Eclipses are possible only in the "eclipse season", when the paths cross, twice a year ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
5. Total vs annular: when the Moon is farther away and looks smaller, it can't cover the Sun completely ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
6. A totally eclipsed Moon looks coppery red, because sunlight is bent into Earth's shadow by Earth's atmosphere ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
7. Eclipses can be predicted centuries ahead ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).

**Definitions**
- Umbra: "a cone where the shadow is darkest". Penumbra: the "lighter, more diffuse region" ([AS 4.7](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-7-eclipses-of-the-sun-and-moon)).
- **IGCSE 0625**: eclipses have "0 mentions" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Lower Secondary 7ESs includes eclipses (same file).

**Laws, equations, standard values**

## 14.5 Tides at Haji Ali

**Concept sequence**
1. Tides follow the Moon. "The daily delay in high tide is the same as the daily delay in the Moon's rising" ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
2. The Moon pulls each part of Earth slightly differently: harder on the near side and more weakly on the far side. Tides come from those *differences* ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
3. Over hours, these tide-raising forces make water *flow* towards the point under the Moon and the point opposite it, so there are **two bulges** ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
4. As Earth turns, a place passes through two bulges a day, giving two highs and two lows ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
5. The Sun also raises tides, "less than half as effective as the Moon". At new and full Moon they add up (spring tides). At the quarters they partly cancel (neap tides) ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
6. Real tides are complicated by land, friction, Earth's rotation, wind and ocean depth, so "tide tables need to be computed and published for each location" ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
7. Tidal friction slows Earth's spin by about 0.002 s per century, and the Moon recedes 3.8 cm a year (Apollo mirrors) ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).

**Definitions**
- Spring tide: the name is "connected not to the season but to the idea that higher tides 'spring up'". Neap tide: lower than usual, at the quarters ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
- **IGCSE 0625**: tides are not in the content quoted in school-wording.md. Lower Secondary 7ESs lists "tides" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Sun vs Moon tidal effect: tidal force goes as M/d³ (standard). AS states only the conclusion, "less than half". `python3 -I -c 'print(round((1.9884e30/1.49598e11**3)/(7.346e22/3.844e8**3),3))'` → 0.459 (masses from [NASA Sun](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html) and [Moon](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html) fact sheets). This agrees with AS.
- An all-water Earth would distort by "up to nearly 1 meter". The solid Earth's tidal distortion is "only about 20 centimeters" at most ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).
- **Mumbai tide type and range**:
  - history.md §9 records that Mumbai's tide is "mixed semi-diurnal (two highs a day of unequal height, larger at new and full moon)". On one sample day the forecast was 3.93 m high and 1.43 m low. The source is a newspaper report ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/mumbai/mumbai-on-alert-as-393-metre-high-tide-expected-today-imd-warns-of-heavy-rain-strong-winds)), not a tide authority.
  - A sailing guide says "The tidal rise for Mumbai Harbor (Bombay Harbor) is 4.4m at MHWS and 3.3m at MHWN" ([sea-seek](https://www.sea-seek.com/en/Mumbai-Bombay-W-India-)). It gives no datum and no primary source. It is a mean high-water height, not a range, so **don't use it as a range**.
  - **GAP**: no INCOIS, Survey of India or Mumbai Port Authority figure for a typical spring range. INCOIS's document server refused connection (`services.incois.gov.in` resolved to a private address, ECONNREFUSED).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Tide timing. The lunar day is 24 h × 29.5306/(29.5306 − 1): `python3 -I -c 'L=24*29.5306/28.5306;print(round(L,3), round(L/2,2), round((L-24)*60))'` → 24.841 h; high tides about 12.42 h apart; 50 min later each day. **Warnings the sources give**
- "The tidal bulges in the oceans do not result from the Moon's compressing or expanding the water, nor from the Moon's lifting the water 'away from Earth.' Rather, they result from an actual flow of water over Earth's surface" ([AS 4.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/4-6-ocean-tides-and-the-moon)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"The Moon lifts the water up."** AS explicitly rejects this. The minimal true version: "the Moon's pull is a tiny bit different across Earth, and over hours that difference pushes water sideways into two heaps."
- **"Tides are only the Moon"** (misconceptions.md §12). The Sun adds about 46% as much (computed).
- **"The far bulge is caused by centrifugal force."** Not in AS. Don't introduce it: Ch 4 rejects centrifugal-force talk, and IGCSE never uses it ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Use "the Moon pulls the far side less than it pulls Earth's centre".
- **"High tide comes exactly when the Moon is overhead."** AS 4.6 lists the complications (land, friction, depth). Real high tide at Mumbai lags. Use a tide table, not the Moon's position.
- **Any Mumbai metre figure** must come from a tide authority. A newspaper high-water height is not a range.
