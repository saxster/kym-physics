## 15.1 Light comes in packets

**Concept sequence**
1. Hot objects glow. As temperature rises, the glow gets brighter and its peak moves to shorter wavelengths (blackbody curves) ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light)).
2. Planck explained the curve by letting each frequency carry energy only in lumps of hf ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light)).
3. The photoelectric effect: "When light strikes certain materials, it can eject electrons from them" ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).
4. The five observations a simple wave can't explain ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)):
   - there is a threshold frequency below which no electrons come out, *regardless of intensity*;
   - there is no delay;
   - the number of electrons goes with intensity;
   - the electrons' maximum energy does *not* depend on intensity;
   - KE = hf − BE.
5. Einstein: light comes in photons of energy E = hf. One photon gives its energy to one electron ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).
6. Photon energy rises up the spectrum. Visible photons are about 1.6–3.3 eV. UV and above can ionise, while IR and below cannot, one photon at a time ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
7. A typical TV remote flashes about 940 nm infrared. "Some digital and cell phone cameras are sensitive to that wavelength" ([NASA Infrared Waves](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/07_infraredwaves/)).

**Definitions**
- Photon: an individual quantum of EM radiation, with energy hf ([PH 21.2 Fig 21.6](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).
- Binding energy / work function: "the minimum amount of energy necessary to eject an electron" ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).
- **IGCSE 0625** does not list the photoelectric effect in the content quoted in school-wording.md. IB DP E.2 "Quantum physics" does ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md) §IB).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- E = hf, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light)).
- KE_e = hf − BE ([PH 21.2 eq 21.6](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).
- Visible photon energies, red to violet: "1.63 to 3.26 eV" ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). Those end-points match 760–380 nm: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(6.626e-34*2.998e8/760e-9/1.602e-19,2), round(6.626e-34*2.998e8/380e-9/1.602e-19,2))'` → 1.63 3.26. CP 24.3 instead uses 400–750 nm for visible, which is a small inconsistency within CP. Quote one range only.
- Ionisation needs "10 to 1000 eV" per ionisation, and molecules are bound by about 10 eV ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- CP Example 29.1: a 420 nm violet photon on calcium (BE = 2.71 eV) ([CP 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-2-the-photoelectric-effect)). `python3 -I -c 'E=6.626e-34*2.998e8/420e-9/1.602e-19;print(round(E,2), round(E-2.71,2))'` → 2.95 eV photon; 0.24 eV electron.
- Mumbai version: a TV-remote photon at 940 nm (NASA): `python3 -I -c 'print(round(6.626e-34*2.998e8/940e-9/1.602e-19,2))'` → 1.32 eV. That is below calcium's 2.71 eV, so the remote's IR ejects no electrons from calcium, *however bright it is*. This is the threshold idea.
- PH 21.1 example: a 100 W incandescent bulb, assumed 10% visible at 580 nm ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light)). `python3 -I -c 'print("%.2e"%(10/(6.626e-34*2.998e8/580e-9)))'` → 2.92e+19 photons/s. "Photons are so small that quantization becomes essentially continuous."

**Demonstrations**
- TV remote and phone camera (misconceptions.md §13; [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/07_infraredwaves/)). Feasible and safe. Note "some" cameras: try the front camera if the rear one shows nothing (NASA says only that *some* cameras are sensitive). The front/rear difference itself is unsourced.
- PhET "Photoelectric Effect", linked from [PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect). Link out. It is the decisive "brighter red light still does nothing" experiment, which can't be done at home.

**Concept questions**
- "What is the mathematical relationship linking the energy of a photon to its frequency?" ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light))
- "Two flames are observed on a stove. One is red while the other is blue. Which flame is hotter?" ([PH 21.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-1-planck-and-quantum-nature-of-light))
- `:::predict`: "Shine a *very bright* red light and a *dim* violet light on the same metal. Which knocks out electrons?" From the PH 21.2 properties: only the violet does, if its photons beat the BE.
- PH Table 21.1 asks the reader to fill in the classical-wave and photon-model columns for the five properties ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)). A good `:::check` grid.

**Warnings the sources give**
- Microwaves and IR are hazardous only through "huge numbers of photons acting together" (heating), not through single-photon ionisation ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Light is a particle, not a wave."** Light shows both behaviours. PH 21.3 is on "The Dual Nature of Light" (title only; not fetched). Minimal true version: "light travels like a wave but delivers its energy in packets."
- **OpenStax slip.** PH 21.2 says Einstein found "the energy of a photon *or photoelectron*" to be E = hf. The electron's energy is hf − BE (the same page, eq 21.6). Don't copy the first sentence.
- **OpenStax overreach.** PH 21.1 says a blue flame is "more dangerous" because each blue photon carries more energy. Its hotness is the danger, not single visible photons. Visible photons don't ionise ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)). Don't use that line.
- **"The phone sees heat."** The remote's 940 nm is near-infrared light from an LED, not heat radiation from a warm object. NASA's page also loosely says humans "detect it as heat". Keep the two apart.
- **"Brighter light gives faster electrons."** False: brighter light gives more electrons of the same maximum energy ([PH 21.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/21-2-einstein-and-the-photoelectric-effect)).

**Gaps**
- S. N. Bose (1924) is in history.md §7 only. No OpenStax or OCW source.
- 8.282J has nothing at a usable level for photons.

## 15.2 The nucleus and radiation myths

**Concept sequence**
1. Rutherford's gold-foil result: only 1 in 8,000 alpha particles bounced back, so the atom is mostly empty space around a tiny, dense nucleus ([PH 22.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-1-the-structure-of-the-atom)).
2. The nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁵ m across, "or 100,000 times smaller than the atom" (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) ([PH 22.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-1-the-structure-of-the-atom)).
3. The nucleus is made of protons and neutrons (nucleons), held by the strong nuclear force, which has a very short range ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity)).
4. In 1896 Becquerel found that uranium ore darkened wrapped photographic plates, because some nuclei emit radiation. Nuclear radiation carries about 10⁶ eV per event, compared with a few eV for atomic processes ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity)).
5. Alpha, beta and gamma radiation all ionise. Alphas have the shortest range, then betas, and gammas the greatest ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity); [CP 31.1](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/31-1-nuclear-radioactivity)).
6. **Exposure is not contamination.** Food irradiated with γ-rays, X-rays or electrons gets no residual radioactivity: "These photons and electrons induce no nuclear reactions". Neutron irradiation can cause it, and is not used for food ([CP 32.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-4-food-irradiation)). CDC: "A person exposed to radiation is not necessarily contaminated with radioactive material". "When a person has an x-ray, he or she is exposed to radiation but is not contaminated" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/contamination-versus-exposure.html)).
7. Background radiation is everywhere, from cosmic rays, rocks and food. "So we are all a little radioactive" ([CP 32.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-2-biological-effects-of-ionizing-radiation)).

**Definitions**
- Radiation exposure: "Another word for radiation exposure is irradiation" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/contamination-versus-exposure.html)).
- Contamination: radioactive material on the skin, hair or clothing (external), or swallowed or breathed in (internal) ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/contamination-versus-exposure.html)).
- Mass number A and atomic number Z: A = Z + N ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity)).
- **IGCSE 0625 5.1** covers "The nuclear model of the atom: 5.1.1 The atom, 5.1.2 The nucleus" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m; nucleus about 10⁻¹⁵ m ([PH 22.1](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-1-the-structure-of-the-atom)). `python3 -I -c 'print(1e-10/1e-15)'` → 100000.0.
- Nuclear radius r = r₀A^(1/3), with r₀ = 1.2 fm ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity)).
- Doses ([CP 32.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-2-biological-effects-of-ionizing-radiation)):

  | Source | Dose |
  |---|---|
  | Cosmic rays, average | about 0.40 mSv/y |
  | A 12-hour flight | 0.02 to 0.03 mSv |
  | Airline crew, average | 2 mSv/y |
  | "Low dose" | below 100 mSv |

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Scale model (ASSUMED 1 cm marble as the nucleus): `python3 -I -c 'print(0.01*1e5)'` → 1000.0 m. The atom would be a kilometre across, roughly the width of a large Mumbai neighbourhood; the comparison place is unsourced. This also resolves misconceptions.md's "atom-to-nucleus ratio about 100,000: UNVERIFIED", which is now verified by PH 22.1.

**Demonstrations**
- None hands-on with radioactive material, ever.
- The TV-remote IR (15.1) shows "radiation" that is harmless and everywhere (misconceptions.md §13).
- Becquerel's plate is a story, not a demo.

**Concept questions**
- `:::predict`: "After a chest X-ray, is the patient radioactive?" No ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radiation-emergencies/infographic/contamination-versus-exposure.html)). 63–75% of students say yes ([misconceptions.md §13](../research/misconceptions.md)).
- `:::predict`: "Does irradiated food become radioactive?" No, for γ, X-ray or electron irradiation ([CP 32.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-4-food-irradiation)).
- "Which travels farthest through material: alpha, beta or gamma?" ([PH 22.2](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-2-nuclear-forces-and-radioactivity))

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Some forms of ionizing radiation, such as neutron irradiation, cause residual radioactivity" ([CP 32.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-4-food-irradiation)). So "radiation never makes things radioactive" is *also* false. The true rule is about which kind.
- Doses from the ground "vary greatly by location", and some places are up to ten times the average ([CP 32.2](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-2-biological-effects-of-ionizing-radiation)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"Radiation makes things radioactive."** False for light, X-rays, γ-rays and electrons. True for neutrons ([CP 32.4](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/32-4-food-irradiation)). Minimal true version: "Being *hit* by X-rays or γ-rays doesn't make you radioactive. Getting radioactive *stuff* on you or inside you does."
- **"All radiation is dangerous."** "Radiation" includes visible light and radio. The danger depends on photon energy and dose ([CP 29.3](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/29-3-photon-energies-and-the-electromagnetic-spectrum)).
- **OpenStax error.** PH 22.4 opens: "nuclear fission is the splitting of an atomic bond" ([PH 22.4](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/22-4-nuclear-fission-and-fusion)). Fission splits a *nucleus*. Don't quote it.
- **"Atoms are tiny solid balls"** (misconceptions.md §13). They are almost entirely empty (PH 22.1).

- No OCW material in the spine covers the nucleus at this level.
- AERB (India's regulator) was not fetched. CDC stands in for "a reliable safety source".
- Bhabha and TIFR are in history.md §7 only.

## 15.3 E = mc² and the Sun's fire

**Concept sequence**
1. The 19th-century candidates were chemical burning and gravitational contraction ([AS 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy)).
2. Burning fails: a Sun made of coal or wood "could not produce energy at its present rate for more than few thousand years". Nothing like solid coal could even survive at the Sun's temperatures ([AS 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy)).
3. Contraction (Kelvin, Helmholtz) fails too. It would last only about 100 million years, but geology shows water on Earth nearly 4 billion years ago ([AS 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy)).
4. Mass is energy, E = mc². When four hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, 0.71% of the mass is converted to energy ([AS 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-2-mass-energy-and-the-theory-of-relativity); 8.282J PS8 uses 0.007).
5. The proton-proton chain in the core needs more than 12 million K. The centre is about 15 million K ([AS 16.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-3-the-solar-interior-theory)).
6. The Sun turns about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium each second. About 4 million tons of that becomes energy ([AS 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-2-mass-energy-and-the-theory-of-relativity)).
7. Energy takes 100,000 to 1,000,000 years to random-walk out as photons, while neutrinos escape in about 2 s ([AS 16.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-3-the-solar-interior-theory)).
8. Aditya-L1 watches the Sun from a halo orbit around L1, "roughly 1.5 million km from earth on the continuously moving Sun – Earth line". Its orbital period is about 177.86 days, and it arrived on 6 January 2024 ([ISRO](https://www.isro.gov.in/ISRO_EN/halo-orbit-insertion-adtya-l1.html)).

**Definitions**
- Fusion: light nuclei combining into a heavier nucleus ([AS 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-2-mass-energy-and-the-theory-of-relativity)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.2.1** is "The Sun as a star" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Energy stores include "nuclear" (IGCSE 1.7.1, same file).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Solar luminosity 382.8 × 10²⁴ J/s, so L = 3.828 × 10²⁶ W. NASA's "mass conversion rate" is 4260 × 10⁶ kg/s ([NASA SF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html)). AS rounds L to 4 × 10²⁶ W ([AS 16.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-1-sources-of-sunshine-thermal-and-gravitational-energy)).
- Sun's mass 1,988,400 × 10²⁴ kg ([NASA SF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html)).
- p-p efficiency: 0.0071 kg of each kg of hydrogen becomes energy ([AS 16.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/16-2-mass-energy-and-the-theory-of-relativity)). "Take the nuclear burning efficiency of the p-p chain to be 0.007" ([8.282J PS8](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/resources/ps8/)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Mass loss: `python3 -I -c 'print("%.3e"%(3.828e26/2.998e8**2))'` → 4.259e+09 kg/s, about 4.26 million tonnes per second. This matches NASA's 4260 × 10⁶ kg/s and AS's "about 4 million tons".

## 15.4 What stars are made of

**Concept sequence**
1. Spectra: an emission spectrum is bright lines, and an absorption spectrum is dark lines on a continuous background ([AS 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-3-spectroscopy-in-astronomy)).
2. Fraunhofer (1815) found about 600 dark lines in sunlight. Kirchhoff (1860) first identified an element in the Sun, sodium, from its lines. Helium was found in the Sun before it was found on Earth ([AS 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-3-spectroscopy-in-astronomy)).
3. Star spectra look different from one another. Astronomers first thought this meant different compositions. "This hypothesis turned out to be wrong. The primary reason that stellar spectra look different is because the stars have different temperatures" ([AS 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs)).
4. Example: hydrogen is the most abundant element, yet its lines are missing in the hottest stars, where it is ionised, and in the coolest, where the electrons sit in the lowest level ([AS 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs)). This temperature-and-ionisation insight is Saha's (history.md §7). AS does not name him.
5. Cannon sorted spectra into O B A F G K M, in order of falling temperature ([AS 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs)).
6. Payne's 1925 thesis showed the Sun and stars are mostly hydrogen and helium. The result was "so unexpected and so shocking that she was persuaded her analysis… must be wrong". It was confirmed three years later ([AS 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun)).
7. Today's figures: "About 73% of the Sun's mass is hydrogen, and another 25% is helium" ([AS 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun)).

**Definitions**
- Emission and absorption spectrum, as quoted above ([AS 5.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-3-spectroscopy-in-astronomy)).
- Plasma: "such a hot ionized gas" ([AS 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Sun by mass: H 73%, He 25% ([AS 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun)). Remainder: `python3 -I -c 'print(100-73-25)'` → 2% for everything else, all the "metals" in astronomers' sense.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- Earth's crust, for contrast: "the three most abundant elements are oxygen, silicon, and aluminum" ([AS 15.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/15-1-the-structure-and-composition-of-the-sun)). This is the expectation Payne overturned.

**Demonstrations**

The demo is ours. 8.282J gave students "a simple spectrometer kit" to view lines "from common light sources as well as from the Sun" ([8.282J syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/pages/syllabus/)). We drop the Sun part for safety.

**Concept questions**
- `:::predict`: "A hot blue star shows almost no hydrogen lines. Does that mean it has no hydrogen?" No: it is too hot, and the hydrogen is ionised ([AS 17.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-3-the-spectra-of-stars-and-brown-dwarfs)).

## 15.5 How far are the stars?

**Concept sequence**
1. Parallax: hold a pen in front of your face and switch eyes. The farther the pen, the smaller the shift ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
2. Earth's orbit is the baseline. A star's parallax is "one half of the total change in direction", over six months ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
3. The parsec is the distance at which the parallax is 1 arcsecond: 206,265 AU, or 3.26 light-years ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
4. Bessel made the first authenticated stellar distance measurement, of 61 Cygni, in 1838 ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
5. Hipparcos measured to about 0.001″. Gaia measures nearly a billion stars "with an accuracy of a few millionths of an arcsecond" ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
6. Beyond parallax: Leavitt found that "brighter-appearing cepheids always have the longer periods". The Small Magellanic Cloud's stars are all at about the same distance, so apparent brightness tracks true brightness ([AS 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-3-variable-stars-one-key-to-cosmic-distances)). She could not calibrate it herself, because the distance to the Magellanic Clouds was then unknown ([AS 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-3-variable-stars-one-key-to-cosmic-distances)).
7. Hubble later used Cepheids in nearby galaxies, which led to the expansion of the universe ([AS 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-3-variable-stars-one-key-to-cosmic-distances)). Link to 15.7.

**Definitions**
- Parsec: "the distance at which we have a parallax of one arcsecond" ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
- Cepheids: "large, yellow, pulsating stars named for the first-known star of the group, Delta Cephei" ([AS 19.3](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-3-variable-stars-one-key-to-cosmic-distances)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.2.2** covers "Stars" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)). Our file does not quote its wording on the light-year.

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec). AS gives 206,265 AU per parsec and 1 pc = 3.26 ly ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)). Check: `python3 -I -c 'import math;au=1.49598e11;pc=180*3600/math.pi*au;print(round(180*3600/math.pi), "%.4e"%pc, round(pc/(2.998e8*3.15576e7),3))'` → 206265 AU; 3.0857e+16 m; 3.261 ly.
- Proxima Centauri is 4.25 ly away ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)). `python3 -I -c 'd=4.25/3.2616;print(round(d,3), round(1/d,3))'` → 1.303 pc, parallax 0.767″.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- AS scale: a US quarter looks 1″ wide from about 5 km ([AS 19.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-2-surveying-the-stars)).
- Mumbai version: a ₹1 coin is 22 mm across (RBI 2011 series; [RBI PR](https://www.rbi.org.in/Commonman/English/scripts/PressReleases.aspx?Id=1124)). `python3 -I -c 'print(round(0.022*206265/1000,2))'` → 4.54 km for it to look 1″ wide. Proxima's parallax is smaller still.
- 8.282J PS1 Problem 3: with 0.001″ errors, how far can distances be measured to about 10%? ([8.282J PS1](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/resources/ps1/)) Our solution: 10% accuracy needs p ≥ 0.01″. `python3 -I -c 'print(1/0.01)'` → 100.0 pc.

## 15.6 How stars die

**Concept sequence**
1. When a Sun-like star runs out of fuel, its core contracts until the electrons can be squeezed no closer. The Pauli exclusion principle sets up "degeneracy pressure", which holds the star up as a white dwarf ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).
2. The more massive the white dwarf, the smaller it is. The models give zero radius at about 1.4 M☉, meaning that "even the force of degenerate electrons cannot stop the collapse of a star with more mass than this" ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).
3. "The maximum mass that a star can end its life with and still become a white dwarf—1.4 MSun—is called the Chandrasekhar limit" ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).
4. Many stars born far heavier than 1.4 M☉ shed mass and still end below the limit ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).
5. Chandrasekhar: born 1910 in Lahore; nephew of C. V. Raman; finished college at 19; did the first white-dwarf calculations "during the long boat voyage" to England. Eddington "publicly ridiculed" the work. Nobel Prize in 1983 ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).

**Definitions**
- Chandrasekhar limit, as quoted above ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).
- Degeneracy pressure: "no two electrons can be in the same place at the same time doing the same thing" ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Limit ≈ 1.4 M☉ ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)). `python3 -I -c 'print("%.3e"%(1.4*1.9884e30))'` → 2.784e+30 kg (Sun's mass from [NASA SF](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html)).
- 8.282J teaches this in Lec 26, "Fermi Pressure, White Dwarf Stars, and the Chandrasekhar Limit", with optional "White Dwarf Models" and a PS10 "Collapsing White Dwarf" problem ([8.282J syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/pages/syllabus/); [assignments](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/pages/assignments/)). No notes are posted, so the sequence comes from AS.

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- None needed beyond the limit. The Sun at 1 M☉ is below it, so it will end as a white dwarf ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).

**Demonstrations**
- None physical. A crowded-seats analogy (no two in the same seat) is a common teaching image and is not in AS. AS uses its own personified electrons ("You can press inward all you want…"). If we use a local-train analogy, flag it as ours.

**Concept questions**
- `:::predict`: "Add more mass to a white dwarf. Does it get bigger or smaller?" Smaller ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)). This is counter-intuitive and a strong prediction.
- "Will the Sun become a white dwarf?" Yes, because it is below 1.4 M☉.

**Warnings the sources give**
- Electron degeneracy pressure "is different from, and much stronger than, the normal electrical repulsion between charges" ([AS 23.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/23-1-the-death-of-low-mass-stars)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**

**Gaps**

## 15.7 The stretching universe

**Concept sequence**
1. Doppler shift for light: a source moving away shows lines shifted to longer wavelengths (redshift), and one moving closer shows a blueshift ([AS 5.6](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/5-6-the-doppler-effect)).
2. Slipher (from 1912) found most spiral "nebulae" racing away, at up to 1800 km/s ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
3. Hubble and Humason (1929, 1931): the farther the galaxy, the faster it recedes, v = H × d ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
4. **Ants on a stretching ruler, raisins in rising bread:** every ant or raisin sees every other one move away at a speed proportional to distance, with no special centre. "None of the ants were actually moving of their own accord, it was the stretching of the ruler that moved them apart" ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
5. Galaxies "are passive participants in the expansion of space". Galaxies themselves don't expand, because gravity holds them together ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
6. The better description of the redshift: "the light waves are stretched by the stretching of the space they travel through", with z = Δλ/λ₀ ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)).
7. "There is no location you can visit where the stretching of space began or where we can say that the Big Bang happened" ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)).
8. The age is roughly 1/H, and the best estimate is 13.8 billion years ([AS 29.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-1-the-age-of-the-universe)).

**Definitions**
- Redshift: "a stretching of waves—the wavelength of each wave increases" ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)).
- **IGCSE 0625 6.2.3**: redshift is "an increase in the observed wavelength of electromagnetic radiation emitted from receding stars and galaxies". It is "evidence that the Universe is expanding and supports the Big Bang Theory". The syllabus also covers H₀ = v/d and "all the matter in the Universe was present at a single point" ([school-wording.md](../research/school-wording.md)).
  - Bridge sentence (same file): "Your syllabus says galaxies are 'receding'. A careful way to picture it is space stretching between them."

**Laws, equations, standard values**
- Hubble's law v = H × d. AS uses H ≈ 22 km/s per million light-years, "converging on a value near 22", with older estimates of 15–30 ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
- Universe age 13.8 billion years, ± about 100 million ([AS 29.1](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-1-the-age-of-the-universe)).

**Worked numbers we can reuse**
- AS example: a galaxy 100 million ly away recedes at 2200 km/s ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)). `python3 -I -c 'print(22*100)'` → 2200.
- IGCSE units: `python3 -I -c 'print(round(22*3.2616,1))'` → 71.8 km/s per Mpc. In SI and as a Hubble time: `python3 -I -c 'H=22e3/(1e6*2.998e8*3.15576e7);print("%.3e"%H, round(1/H/3.15576e7/1e9,2))'` → 2.325e-18 s⁻¹; 1/H = 13.63 billion years. That is close to AS's 13.8. AS 29.1 explains why 1/H ≈ age is a "happy coincidence" of deceleration and acceleration.
- Ruler analogy numbers: the observer ant sits on the 2 cm mark, other ants sit at 4, 7 and 12 cm, and the ruler doubles in length in 1 minute ([AS 26.5 Fig 26.16](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)). Speeds seen from the 2 cm ant: `python3 -I -c 'print([2*(x-2)-(x-2) for x in (4,7,12)])'` → [2, 5, 10] cm/min. Each speed equals the original distance, so speed is proportional to distance. AS gives 2 and 5 cm/min and the same pattern. Redo from any other ant to show there is no centre.

**Demonstrations**
- Balloon with dots, or a rubber band with ink marks, stretched. 8.282J PS13 Problem 6 is titled "Expanding Balloon Analogy" ([8.282J PS13](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-282j-introduction-to-astronomy-spring-2006/resources/ps13/)), and AS uses a ruler and raisin bread. Feasible and safe in the flat.
- Draw a wave on the balloon before inflating it. It stretches as the balloon grows, as in AS 29.2's "As an elastic surface expands, a wave on its surface stretches" ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)).

**Concept questions**
- `:::predict`: "Every galaxy is moving away from us. Does that mean we're at the centre?" No ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
- "Do the raisins grow as the bread rises?" No, and neither do galaxies ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
- "Where did the Big Bang happen?" Nowhere in particular, or everywhere ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)).

**Warnings the sources give**
- "Our two analogies are useful… but you must not take them literally". The ruler and loaf have edges and a middle, but "the universe has no boundaries, no edges, and no center" ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).
- Hubble distances work only if H was constant. For far galaxies, its change over time must be included ([AS 26.5](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-5-the-expanding-universe)).

**Fidelity traps for a 12-year-old version**
- **"The Big Bang was an explosion in space, and galaxies fly out from a centre."** False ([AS 29.2](https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/29-2-a-model-of-the-universe)). Minimal true version: "space between galaxies is stretching, everywhere at once."
- **The IGCSE phrase "all the matter… at a single point"** (school-wording.md) reads like "a point in space". AS says there is no location where it began. The bridge should say: "everything we can see was once squeezed far closer together, and that happened everywhere."
- **"Cosmic redshift is just the Doppler effect."** AS 29.2 calls the space-stretching description "more accurate". Allow Doppler as the first picture, then upgrade it (AS does exactly this: "When you were young and naïve—a few chapters ago…").
- **H₀ units.** AS uses km/s per million ly. IGCSE expects H₀ = v/d in its own units, which school-wording.md doesn't specify. Show the conversion once.

**Gaps**
- CMBR (in IGCSE 6.2.3) is AS 29.4, not fetched.
- 8.282J PS13 problems reference Zeilik & Gregory (copyrighted). Use the titles only.
