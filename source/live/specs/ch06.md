# Live figure specs, Chapter 6 (Pressure, floating and the ocean of air)

Written by the chapter writer for the widget builders. No `:::live` lines are in `src/ch06.md`; add them under the
named `:::fig` lines when the widgets exist. Both widgets are Canvas 2D (`api.canvas`), no sound, no sensors.

## 6.4 `c6_tank` → `w_pressuretank` (label: "Fill the tank")

**What it shows.** A tank of liquid seen from the side, with pressure arrows on the left wall that grow in step with
depth, and three small holes in the right wall whose jets get faster with depth. It is the still figure brought to
life: *p* = *ρgh*, and pressure pushing sideways as hard as down.

**Controls (3).**
1. Slider **Depth of liquid**: 0 to 2.0 m (step 0.1), default 1.5 m. Changes the water level; jets from holes above
   the surface stop.
2. Tabs **Liquid**: Fresh water (1,000 kg/m³) · Sea water (1,025 kg/m³) · Mercury (13,600 kg/m³). Densities are
   OpenStax College Physics 2e Table 11.1 (grounding/ch06.md 6.2, 6.4).
3. Toggle **Show the air's 101 kPa**: adds the atmosphere's pressure to every readout (absolute pressure) or hides it
   (pressure from the liquid alone, the default, which is what the still figure shows).

**Readout (one or two sentences, updated live).** "At 1.0 m the water pushes with 9.8 kPa on every square metre of
wall, in every direction. Twice as deep, twice the push." With mercury selected: "Mercury is 13.6 times as dense, so
1.0 m of it pushes with 133 kPa: more than the whole atmosphere." When the depth slider is moved: "The jet from the
deepest hole is fastest: its speed goes with the square root of the depth below the surface." Tapping a hole shows its
depth and pressure.

**Physics it must honour.**
- Pressure from the liquid at depth *h*: *p* = *ρgh* with *g* = 9.8 N/kg, computed every frame from the slider, not
  tabulated. Arrow lengths exactly proportional to *p* (same scale across the three liquids; clip mercury's arrows at the
  frame edge and say so in the readout).
- Jet speed from a hole at depth *h* below the surface: *v* = √(2*gh*) (Torricelli's law; it follows from the energy
  bookkeeping of Chapter 7 and is physically exact for an ideal liquid). Draw the jet as a parabola with that horizontal
  speed, falling with *g*. A hole above the surface produces no jet.
- The liquid level falls as the jets run only if a "Drain" mode is added later; by default the level is held (an
  unlimited supply at the top), and the readout says so: "the tank is kept topped up".
- Equal depth means equal pressure whatever the tank's width: if the builder adds a width control, the readout must show
  the pressure unchanged.

**Slowed or exaggerated.** Nothing is exaggerated. The jets are drawn at true relative speeds but the whole animation
may run at half speed for legibility; if so, the readout says "slowed to half speed".

**No sensor or audio.**

## 6.5 `c6_buoyancy` → `w_buoyancy` (label: "Drop it in")

**What it shows.** A block in a tank. Pressure arrows on all its faces (bottom longer than top, sideways equal and
opposite), the red net buoyant force, and the block's weight. Change the block's density or the liquid's and the block
rises, sinks or hangs, settling where the forces balance. Below the tank, two bars: "weight" and "buoyant force", with
"water displaced" shown as a shaded volume in the tank.

**Controls (3).**
1. Slider **Block density**: 100 to 10,000 kg/m³ (logarithmic feel: steps of 100 up to 2,000, then 500), default 920
   (ice). Marks on the slider track: wood 700 · ice 920 · water 1,000 · aluminium 2,700 · steel 7,800 (OpenStax College
   Physics 2e Table 11.1 and 8.01SC notes Table 27.1).
2. Tabs **Liquid**: Fresh water 1,000 · Sea water 1,025 · Mercury 13,600 kg/m³.
3. Button **Hollow it out**: swaps the solid block for a hull of the same mass (same steel, thin walls round a large
   air space, 10× the volume), the chapter's ship argument. A second press swaps back.

**Readout.** Floating: "Ice, 920 kg/m³, floats with 92% of its volume under the water: it displaces exactly its own
weight of water." Sinking: "Steel is 7.8 times as dense as water. The water pushes up with only 1/7.8 of the block's
weight, so it sinks, but the push is still there: resting on the bottom it presses down with only 1 − 1/7.8 ≈ 87% of
its weight in air."
Hanging: "Density equal to the water's: the block hangs wherever you leave it." Hollowed: "The same steel now displaces
10 times as much water when pushed under, so it rises until a tenth of the hull is below the waterline." Sea water:
"Sea water is 2.5% denser, so the block floats a little higher: 89.8% submerged instead of 92%."

**Physics it must honour.**
- Buoyant force = *ρ*_fluid × *V*_submerged × *g*, computed every frame. Weight = *ρ*_block × *V* × *g*.
- Fraction submerged when floating = *ρ*_block / *ρ*_fluid exactly; when *ρ*_block ≥ *ρ*_fluid the block rests on the
  bottom and the buoyant force bar shows *ρ*_fluid × *V* × *g* (the anchor still feels it; never zero).
- Pressure arrows: top and bottom arrow lengths proportional to *p* = *ρgh* at those depths (plus the atmosphere, which
  cancels and may be omitted if the readout says so); sideways arrows equal and opposite at each depth. Net arrow =
  bottom push − top push = buoyant force, to the pixel.
- Motion toward the equilibrium position is damped (the block should settle, not oscillate forever); a floating block
  bobs once or twice and stops. Honesty note in the readout when it moves: "the water's drag is exaggerated so the block
  settles quickly".
- Conservation check: the shaded "water displaced" volume must equal the submerged part of the block at every frame.

**Slowed or exaggerated.** Drag/damping exaggerated (said in the readout). Densities, fractions and force ratios are
real. The hull's wall thickness is drawn thicker than true for visibility; the readout gives the true volume ratio.

**No sensor or audio.**
