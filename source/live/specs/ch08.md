## 8.3 · figure `c8_conduction` · widget `w_conduction`

**What it teaches.** A warm hand and a room-temperature surface are at different temperatures; what the hand *feels* is the
rate at which energy crosses the boundary. Metal carries it away fast (feels cold), wood slowly (feels neutral). Both
surfaces start at the same temperature.

**Scene.** Canvas 640 × 320, the same layout as the still: a warm "hand" region above a boundary line, the surface below.
Particles are drawn as discs with the still's colours (hand `c.proton`, surface `c.electron` for metal, `c.neutron` for
wood). Each particle jiggles about a fixed home position with a random-phase amplitude that grows with its local
temperature; nothing drifts (conduction moves energy, never material). A column of "temperature" is kept per depth layer
(hand: 1 layer; surface: 6 layers).

**Controls (three).**
1. Tabs: **Steel · Wood · Aluminium**. Sets the surface's conductivity k = 14, 0.12 and 220 W/(m·K) (CP Table 14.3, the
   stainless value for the thali, wood's mid-range).
2. Slider **Hand temperature**: 32–38 °C, default 35 °C (the room is fixed at 30 °C; label it).
3. Button **Touch** (starts the contact; a second press lifts the hand and lets the surface relax back to 30 °C).

**Physics it must honour.**
- One-dimensional conduction between layers: each step moves energy between neighbouring layers in proportion to
  k × (temperature difference). The hand layer is held near its set temperature but *loses* energy at the boundary rate,
  and the widget shows that loss as the "what you feel" bar.
- Energy conservation per frame: the energy leaving the hand equals the energy gained by the surface layers (the room
  sink only drains the deepest layer, slowly). Print the running totals in the readout and assert they match.
- Ratios are real: with aluminium the boundary flow is 220/14 ≈ 16× steel's and about 1800× wood's at the same
  temperature difference; the wood surface layer visibly warms toward hand temperature within seconds, the metal
  surface stays near 30 °C.
- Particle jiggle amplitude ∝ local temperature above a baseline, so "warming" is visible as livelier dots, never as
  colour alone (keep the fill colours fixed per material; add a thin ring that brightens with temperature as the
  secondary cue).

**What is slowed or exaggerated (say so in the readout).** Real conduction into a slab is far faster for metals than any
animation can show at this frame rate; the simulation runs the wood case at real-ish pace and *slows the metal cases*
by a stated factor (e.g. "metal shown 20× slower than life"). Jiggle amplitudes are exaggerated about 1000×.

**Readout.** Two sentences, updated live: "Both surfaces are at 30 °C. Energy is leaving your hand at [rate] (steel), about
[n]× faster than through wood. The surface under your hand reads [T] °C." After a few seconds on wood: "The wood surface
has warmed to [T] °C, so the flow has dropped to [rate]: this is why wood stops feeling cold." Reduced motion: one still
frame per tab with the same numbers.

**No sensors, no audio.**
