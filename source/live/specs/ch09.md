# Live-figure specs, Chapter 9 (Vibrations, waves and sound)

BOOKPLAN §5 marks four live figures here (9.3 ripple tank, 9.5 microphone oscilloscope, 9.6 harmonic builder, 9.7
Doppler with audio). No `:::live` line has been added to the chapter; the widget builder adds them under the figures
named below when the widgets exist. All numbers below are from grounding/ch09.md and provenance/ch09.yml.

## 9.3 · figure `c9_wave_carries` · widget `w_ripple` (WebGL shader via `api.gl`, 2D fallback)

**What it teaches.** The disturbance travels; the water does not. Energy spreads, so the rings fade; two sets of rings
pass through each other and add.

**Scene.** A top-view ripple tank, 640 × 360. Tapping (or clicking) the surface drops a "finger" at that point and
starts rings spreading from it; up to three sources at once (older ones fade out). A floating paper scrap (a small
light rectangle) sits where the reader last placed it with a long press, or at a default spot; it rides the surface.

**Controls (three).**
1. Tap the surface: new source (the still's "finger dipped here").
2. Slider **Rhythm**: continuous dipping at 0.5–3 Hz at the last tapped point, or off (default: single dips only).
3. Tabs **One finger · Two fingers**: the second tab places two fixed sources 120 px apart dipping in step, to show the
   rings passing through one another and the still lines between them.

**Physics it must honour.**
- Rings spread at one fixed speed (the shader's time scale), so wavelength = speed ÷ rhythm: raising the rhythm packs
  the rings closer; dipping "harder" (not a control) would change height only. State the speed in the readout.
- Amplitude falls as the ring grows: the height of a ring must scale as 1/√r (energy ∝ amplitude² shared around a
  circumference ∝ r), and the readout says so when a ring has grown.
- Superposition: with two sources the surface height is the plain sum of the two ring patterns; nothing else.
- The paper scrap must move in a small closed loop as a ring passes (forward on the crest, back in the trough) and
  return to its starting point to within a pixel; its drift over one dip must be zero. Draw its loop faintly.
- Fallback (`fallback(ctx2d, …)`): the same rings drawn as circles with the same amplitude law.

**What is slowed or exaggerated (readout).** Heights are exaggerated for visibility; the ring speed is slowed to about
a tenth of real ripple speed; say both.

**Readout.** "Rings spread at [v] cm/s. The paper scrap has moved [d] mm from where it started (it goes round a loop and
comes back). Dipping faster packs the rings closer: wavelength [λ]." With two fingers: "Where the rings cross, their
heights add; along the still lines they cancel."

**No sensors, no audio.**

## 9.5 · figure `c9_loud_high` · widget `w_scope` (sensor lab: `api.mic`)

**What it teaches.** Loudness is amplitude; pitch is frequency; they are separate.

**Scene.** The still's four-panel grid is replaced by one live oscilloscope trace (pressure at the microphone against
time, 640 × 240) above a small spectrum strip (`freq()` bins, 640 × 80) that shows the strongest frequency. Nothing is
recorded or sent; the page says so next to the button (core.js does this).

**Controls (three).**
1. **Use my microphone** (from `api.mic`; permission on press; "Microphone not available here" fallback).
2. Tabs **Waveform · Both**: the waveform alone, or waveform plus the spectrum strip.
3. Button **Freeze**: holds the current trace so the reader can compare two hums (the previous frozen trace stays
   drawn in the muted colour under the live one).

**Physics it must honour.**
- The trace is the raw `time()` buffer, drawn with a fixed vertical scale so a louder sound is a taller trace with
  unchanged spacing; the time axis is labelled in milliseconds from `sampleRate`.
- The readout's loudness number is RMS in decibels relative to the quietest level the device returns, with the
  caption "relative dB: every +10 dB is 10× the sound energy" (CP 17.3), never an absolute dB claim.
- The pitch readout is the strongest bin from `freq()` converted with `binHz`, shown only when a clear peak exists
  (peak at least 12 dB above the median bin), in hertz; otherwise "no clear note".
- Show the audible range marker: the spectrum strip runs 20 Hz to the Nyquist limit on a log axis with 100, 1000 and
  10,000 Hz labelled, so the reader sees a hum near the left and a whistle far to the right.

**What is slowed or exaggerated.** Nothing; the trace is the real signal. Say "this is the real pressure at your
microphone, drawn 1000 times slower than it happens".

**Readout.** "Taller trace, same spacing: louder, same note. Closer crests: a higher note, [f] Hz. Loudness [n] dB
(relative)." Reduced motion: a frozen single frame on each update.

**Sensor use.** Microphone only, opt-in, on-device; stops on close and when scrolled off screen.

## 9.6 · figure `c9_harmonics` · widget `w_harmonics` (Web Audio via `api.audio`)

**What it teaches.** A note is a fundamental plus overtones; whole-number overtones sound like a note (string, tabla),
out-of-tune ones like a thud (plain drum). The mix is timbre.

**Scene.** The still's ladders become six vertical sliders for the amplitudes of six overtones, drawn as a ladder;
beside them, the resulting waveform (one period) and a "mode strip" showing the first three string patterns lighting up
in proportion to their amplitude.

**Controls (three).**
1. Tabs **String · Plain drum · Tabla**: set the overtone *frequencies* to the string ladder (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 × f₀),
   the ideal flat-drumhead ladder (1, 1.593, 2.136, 2.295, 2.653, 2.917 × f₀; computed from Bessel zeros, flagged in
   the ledger as a standard-physics model outside the grounding sources) or the tabla ladder (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 × f₀ with
   a short decay on the upper ones).
2. Six amplitude sliders (count as one control group): 0–1 each, defaults 1, 0.5, 0.33, 0.25, 0.2, 0.17 (the 1/n
   series of a plucked string's shape).
3. Button **Play** (f₀ = 220 Hz, 1.5 s, via `harmonics([a1…a6], f0, …)`; the drum and tabla tabs pass their ratios, so
   `harmonics` must accept an optional ratio list, or the widget builds the voices with `tone` itself).

**Physics it must honour.**
- The waveform drawn is the exact sum of the sine components at the ratios and amplitudes set; the string tab's sum
  must be periodic at f₀ and the drum tab's must visibly fail to repeat.
- Energy: the master gain is normalised so the loudness does not jump between tabs (sum of squares of amplitudes).
- Keep the ratios honest: the drum ladder's numbers come from the circular-membrane modes; the tabla's harmonic ladder
  is what Raman & Kumar 1920 reported (whole-number multiples), not a measured tabla spectrum.
- Bottle check (optional fourth tab is NOT allowed; put it in the readout instead): with "String" selected and only odd
  overtones raised, say "a pipe closed at one end has only these: 1, 3, 5".

**What is slowed or exaggerated.** The waveform is drawn at one period of f₀ stretched across the canvas (say "one
220th of a second, stretched"). Nothing else.

**Readout.** "String: overtones at 2, 3, 4… times the fundamental: a note you can hum." / "Plain drum: overtones at 1.59,
2.14, 2.30… times the lowest: they clash, so you hear a thud with no clear note." / "Tabla: the syahi pulls the overtones
onto whole numbers: it sings." Plus "timbre: same note, different mix" when the reader changes sliders without changing
tabs.

**Audio use.** Web Audio, from the Play button only; stops on close.

## 9.7 · figure `c9_doppler` · widget `w_doppler` (Canvas 2D + `api.audio`)

**What it teaches.** A moving source's compressions bunch ahead and spread behind; the pitch heard ahead is higher and
behind lower; the driver hears the true note; the drop happens quickly as the source passes.

**Scene.** A horn moves left to right across a 640 × 300 canvas past a fixed listener drawn at the bottom centre
(the reader "on the platform"), emitting a ring every period; rings spread at the sound speed, each centred where the
horn was when it made it. A small gauge shows the frequency arriving at the listener, and the sound plays it.

**Controls (three).**
1. Slider **Train speed**: 0–120 km/h, default 80 km/h (the chapter's ASSUMED example).
2. Slider **Horn note**: 200–500 Hz, default 300 Hz.
3. Button **Sound the horn** (starts the pass, with audio; a second press stops it).

**Physics it must honour.**
- Sound speed fixed at 348.7 m/s (30 °C, CP 17.2); rings spread at that speed in the drawing's scale; the horn moves at
  the slider speed in the same scale, so the bunching ratio is real: (c + v)/(c − v).
- The frequency at the listener is computed from the arrival times of successive rings at the listener's position
  (never from the formula directly), so the gauge and the audio show the real geometry: nearly steady while the train is
  far off, a quick drop as it passes, steady after. The formula values must agree with the ring-arrival count: at
  80 km/h and 300 Hz, 320 Hz approaching and 282 Hz receding, within 1 Hz.
- The audio voice (`tone(f, {dur: Infinity})`) is driven with `setFreq` from the same computed arrival rate, and a
  constant gain (no loudness ramp), so the reader hears pitch change without loudness change (the 9.5 point).
- A "ride with the driver" readout line shows the on-board frequency, always the horn's true note.

**What is slowed or exaggerated (readout).** The pass is slowed about 10× so the rings are visible; the drawing's ring
spacing is real for the chosen speed (unlike the still, which is drawn at 0.3 of the speed of sound and says so). The
audio pitch is real-time, not slowed, so the audible drop lasts a few seconds; say "sound in real time, drawing slowed
10×".

**Readout.** "Train at [v] km/h, horn [f] Hz. You hear [f_obs] Hz: [higher/lower] by [n] Hz. The driver hears [f] Hz.
Rings ahead are [ratio]× closer than rings behind." Reduced motion: a still of the bunched rings for the chosen speed
with the two numbers.

**Audio use.** Web Audio from the button; stops on close or when scrolled off screen.
