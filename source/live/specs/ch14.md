# Live figure specs, Chapter 14 (Earth, Moon and Sun)

No `:::live` lines are in `src/ch14.md`; these specs are for the widget builders. All three may later become tabs of
The Sky explorer (`m_sky`, section `#explore-sky`), which already carries the Sun-height, Moon-phase and schematic tide
views; if so, the figure buttons should open the explorer at the matching tab with the chapter's defaults. Still SVGs
stay as the first view. Book colours and theme classes only; nothing moves until the reader taps; pause off screen;
reduced motion opens paused; at most three controls each.

## Figure c14_sun_height → `w_seasons`

- **Idea taught (14.2):** the two things the tilt does. A slanted beam spreads the same light over more ground, and the
  day is shorter when the north leans away. Distance to the Sun is shown to be irrelevant.
- **Controls (3):** a *date* slider over one year (default: today); a *latitude* menu with Mumbai 19.08° N (default),
  the equator 0°, the Tropic of Cancer 23.44° N and Paris 48.85° N; a *torch* toggle that switches the right-hand panel
  between "Mumbai noon Sun" and "torch on graph paper" (the torch panel has its own angle slider in place of the date).
- **Picture:** left, the orbit from above with the axis leaning the same way at every date and the Earth's position
  marked; the Earth–Sun distance shown as a number (147.1 to 152.1 million km, from NASA perihelion and aphelion,
  interpolated by day of year with perihelion ≈ 3 January). Right, the horizon panel of the still figure: the noon Sun
  at the computed altitude, a beam of fixed width, the lit patch of ground measured in metres, and the day length.
- **Readout:** "Noon Sun 85.6° high (leaning 4.4° north). The same beam lights 1.00 m of ground. Daylight about 13.2 h.
  Earth–Sun distance 152.1 million km: farthest of the year." On the torch panel: "Tilted 60°: the patch covers 2.0 times
  the area, so each square gets half the light."
- **Physics it must honour:** solar declination δ(date) from a standard approximation (good to about 1°), noon altitude
  = 90° − |φ − δ| with the north/south lean reported, patch width = beam width / sin(altitude), day length from
  cos H = −tan φ tan δ (geometric, with the −0.833° refraction allowance switchable in the readout), inverse-square
  sunlight ratio from the distance (7% between perihelion and aphelion, stated so the reader sees how small it is).
  Latitudes inside the tropics must show the Sun passing north of overhead between the two zero-shadow dates.
- **Exaggerated or slowed:** nothing. The orbit's eccentricity is too small to draw, so the readout carries the distance.
- **No sensors or audio.**

## Figure c14_phases → `w_phases` (three.js)

- **Idea taught (14.3):** half the Moon is always lit; the phase is how much of the lit half faces us; the dark part is
  the Moon's night side, never the Earth's shadow.
- **Controls (3):** a *day of the month* slider (0 to 29.5, default 3: a waxing crescent); a *view* toggle between "from
  above the orbit" and "from Mumbai"; a *scale* toggle between "drawn close (not to scale)" and "true distance" (the Moon
  shrinks to a dot about 30 Earth-diameters away, so the reader sees why no shadow reaches it except in an eclipse).
- **Picture:** a lamp-lit sphere (the Moon) orbiting a sphere (the Earth) with parallel sunlight from one side; drag to
  turn the camera; the "from Mumbai" view shows the disc with the correct lit fraction and the lit side on the correct
  (west) side for a northern observer. The Moon keeps the same face towards the Earth as it goes round (one turn per orbit).
- **Readout:** "Day 3: waxing crescent, about 10% lit. The lit half faces the Sun; we see a sliver of it. Rises about
  2½ hours after the Sun." Lit fraction (1 − cos θ)/2 and the approximate moonrise delay 49 min × day.
- **Physics it must honour:** sunlight parallel; the lit hemisphere always exactly half; synchronous rotation; 29.5-day
  cycle; the Earth's shadow (umbra) drawn as a faint cone only in the true-distance view, so the reader sees it miss the
  Moon except at full-Moon alignment.
- **Exaggerated:** the drawn-close view's distance (say so in the readout). Orbit tilt omitted here (14.4's business).
- **No sensors or audio.**

## Figure c14_tides → `w_tides`

- **Idea taught (14.5):** two bulges from the *difference* in the Moon's pull; two highs a day; the Sun adds about 46%;
  spring and neap tides follow the phase.
- **Controls (2):** a *time* slider over one lunar month (hours, default 0, play button steps the Earth's rotation and the
  Moon's orbit together); a *Sun* toggle that adds the Sun's bulges (so spring and neap tides appear and disappear).
- **Picture:** schematic, clearly labelled: the Earth turning, the Moon orbiting at 12.19° per day, two water bulges
  drawn as an ellipse aligned with the Moon (plus the Sun's smaller ellipse when toggled, the two added vectorially), a
  Mumbai marker on the Earth, and a strip chart underneath plotting the water height at Mumbai against time, with the
  Moon's phase icon along the top.
- **Readout:** "Mumbai is under the near bulge: high tide. Next high in about 12 h 25 min; tomorrow's about 50 min later.
  Full Moon: Sun and Moon bulges add, a spring tide." At the quarters: "Sun's bulges sit in the Moon's troughs: a neap tide."
- **Physics it must honour:** bulge heights in the ratio Moon 1 : Sun 0.46 (from M/d³ with NASA values); lunar day
  24 h 50 min (the Earth turns 360° in 24 h while the Moon advances 12.19° per day); the second high on the far side;
  spring tides at new and full (both alignments equally); the strip chart's two daily highs with the 14.8-day
  spring–neap envelope. State in the readout that the Earth's two bulges are drawn thousands of times too tall, that
  real tides lag the Moon and vary by coast, and that Mumbai's real times come from published tide tables.
- **Exaggerated or slowed:** bulge height (hugely; the real all-water figure is under 1 m); the Moon's distance; the
  tidal lag and coastal effects are omitted (said in the readout). Time runs at the reader's speed.
- **No sensors or audio.**
