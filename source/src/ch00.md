Nobody standing on the ground has ever seen the whole Earth. From Marine Drive the sea looks flat to the horizon. From a plane the horizon is a gentle curve, and that is all you get. Yet about 2,200 years ago, a Greek scholar living in Alexandria worked out how far it is all the way round the Earth, and his answer was good. He never left Egypt. His equipment was an upright stick, the shadow it cast at noon, and a story a traveller had brought from a town far to the south.

This chapter is about how he could do that, and it contains no law of physics at all. Its subject is a way of looking at the world that every later chapter depends on: keeping what you *see* separate from what you *think* it means; measuring carefully, and saying how sure you are; being at ease with enormous and tiny numbers; and knowing how to make a good guess before you make a careful measurement. *The Invisible World* used this way of seeing to find atoms. Here we use it to size up a planet, and then to start on the real subject of this book: things that move.

## 0.1 What we see and what we think

:::scene Alexandria, around 200 BCE

Eratosthenes was told a curious thing. At Syene, a town far up the Nile to the south, there was a deep vertical well, and at noon on the longest day of the year the sunlight reached all the way to the bottom of it. That could only happen if the Sun stood exactly overhead. In Alexandria, on that same day at that same noon, an upright column cast a short but definite shadow. The Sun was not quite overhead there.

Two observations, from two cities. On their own they are just facts. What made Eratosthenes a physicist was what he did next: he asked what *picture of the world* would make both facts true at once, and then he asked what else that picture would let him work out. Hold on to his two facts. The chapter ends with his answer.

Everything in science starts with an ==observation==: something a careful person can see, hear or measure, and that another careful person could check. "The well is lit to the bottom at noon on the longest day" is an observation. "The Sun is directly over Syene" is already a small step beyond it, and "the Earth is round" is a much bigger step. Those bigger steps are a ==model==: a picture of how things are, built to explain the observations. The observation is what anyone would see. The model is our best explanation so far.

:::note Observation
Something seen or measured that another careful person could check. Observations are where every investigation starts.
:::

:::note Model
A picture or description of how something works, built from observations, used to explain them and to predict new ones. Models are always simpler than the real thing and usually right only within limits.
:::

Take the roundness of the Earth. Educated Greeks already knew it two thousand years before Columbus, and they knew it for good reasons. Aristotle collected them. When the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon during a lunar eclipse, the edge of that shadow is always a circle. Travellers heading south see new stars appear above the southern horizon, while the North Star sinks lower behind them. And a ship sailing away does not simply shrink to a dot. Its hull disappears first, while the mast and sail are still in view. Each of these is an observation. The model that explains all three is a ball. A flat disc can cast a round shadow only when it faces the light squarely; turned edge-on, it casts a line. Only a sphere casts a round shadow from every direction.

:::predict
On a clear day you watch a ship sail straight away from Marine Drive, through binoculars. As it gets further away, what do you expect to see?
- The whole ship shrinks evenly until it is a dot
- The bottom of the ship disappears first; the mast is the last thing you see
- The top of the mast disappears first; the hull is the last thing you see
???
The hull goes first and the mast last, as sailors have always reported. On a flat sea the whole ship would simply shrink. The bulge of water between you and the ship hides the lowest part first, which is exactly what a round Earth predicts.
:::

:::fig c0_hull_down() | The same observation, two models. On the left, a flat sea: a ship sailing away would shrink but stay whole. On the right, a curved sea: the water bulges up between the watcher and the ship and hides the hull before the mast. Sailors see the right-hand picture. The curve of the sea is drawn thousands of times too strong.

Here is the part that matters most. Pythagoras, two centuries before Aristotle, also said the Earth was a sphere. His reason was that spheres were the most perfect shape, so the Earth ought to be one. He reached the right answer with no evidence at all. Aristotle reached the same answer by asking what shape would explain the shadow, the stars and the ships. Only the second way is science. A model earns its place by explaining what we see, and by predicting something we have not looked at yet.

Even a good model can be defended too long. The Greeks knew that if the Earth travelled round the Sun, the nearer stars should seem to shift against the farther ones during the year, the way a lamp post seems to shift against the buildings behind it as you walk past. They looked for that shift and could not find one. Most of them concluded that the Earth stays still. The other possibility, that the stars are so unimaginably far away that the shift is too small to see, seemed absurd. It was true. The shift was finally measured, with telescopes, about two thousand years later, and we will meet it again when we measure the distance to the stars.

So a physicist keeps two columns in her head. In one column go the observations, which stay true whatever anyone thinks. In the other go the models, which are our best explanations so far and can always be improved. A ==law== is a short statement of a pattern that has held in every case anyone has tested. A ==theory== is a well-tested explanation of why the pattern holds. Neither is ever proved beyond all doubt, because nobody can test every possible case. What we can say is: every test so far agrees, and one good test that disagreed would sink it.

:::note Law
A short statement of a pattern that has held in every case tested, often a single equation.
:::

:::note Theory
A well-tested explanation, checked many times by many people. To a scientist, "it's only a theory" makes no sense: a theory is as good as explanations get.
:::

:::careful
> "The Greeks thought the Earth was flat, and Columbus proved it was round."

Educated people around the Mediterranean knew the Earth was round at least two thousand years before Columbus sailed. Eratosthenes did not set out to prove it was round. He assumed it, because Aristotle's evidence was already convincing, and used that model to do something new: measure how big the round Earth is.
:::

:::school
IB MYP science names *models* and *evidence* among the ideas every science shares. Cambridge IGCSE physics does not have a topic on this at all; its first topic is measuring. The habit is taken for granted, which is why we practise it here.
:::

:::check
A friend says: "The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west, so the Sun goes round the Earth." Which part of that sentence is an observation, and which is a model? Can you think of a second model that fits the same observation?
???
The observation is that the Sun rises in the east and sets in the west; anyone can check it. "The Sun goes round the Earth" is a model built to explain it. A second model that fits just as well is Aryabhata's from the letter at the front of this book: the Earth turns towards the east, so everything in the sky seems to drift west. One observation cannot choose between the two. You need more observations, and later chapters collect them.
:::

## 0.2 Measuring anything

:::scene Sèvres, near Paris, 1879

In 1879 a metalworker named George Matthey made a small cylinder: 39 millimetres tall and 39 millimetres across, nine parts platinum to one part iridium. It went to the International Bureau of Weights and Measures at Sèvres, just outside Paris, and sat under three glass bell jars, one inside the other, with six official copies kept alongside it. For the next 130 years, when anyone anywhere said "one kilogram", that is what they meant: exactly as much stuff as that cylinder, no more and no less. Every kitchen scale in Mumbai traced back, through a chain of copies, to a lump of metal in a French vault.

It had a flaw. Over the decades, tiny amounts of whatever floated in the air stuck to its surface, and its mass slowly changed. The changes were far too small to feel. They were not too small to measure, and a kilogram that drifts is a kilogram nobody can fully trust. So on 20 May 2019 the cylinder was retired. The kilogram is now defined by fixing a constant of nature called the Planck constant at exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds, together with the already-fixed metre and second. The sentence is a mouthful, but the idea is simple. We stopped trusting a lump of metal and tied the kilogram to a number of nature that never changes, anywhere, ever.

:::story kilogram

Why go to that trouble? Because a measurement is a number *and* a unit, and the unit has to mean the same thing to everyone. "The pitch is 20" tells you nothing. "The pitch is 20 metres" does. A ==unit== is an agreed amount that everyone measures against, and a measurement is how many of those amounts you have. The world has agreed on a set called SI units. There are seven basic ones; for this book you need three: the metre for length, the kilogram for mass and the second for time. Everything else is built from them. Speed is metres per second, m/s. Later you will meet the newton and the joule, which are also just combinations of metres, kilograms and seconds with a short name.

:::note Unit
An agreed amount of something, such as one metre or one second, that everyone measures against. A measurement is a number of units: 20 metres, 3.5 seconds.
:::

The three basic units have each made the same journey, from an object to a number of nature. The metre began in 1791 as one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole, became a platinum–iridium bar in 1889, then a certain number of wavelengths of light from krypton in 1960, and since 1983 it has been the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The second was one 86,400th of an average day until 1967; now it is a count of 9,192,631,770 vibrations of a caesium atom. The kilogram was the last to leave its lump of metal behind. Since 20 May 2019 all seven base units rest on seven fixed constants, and a laboratory anywhere can build a kilogram, a metre or a second from scratch without asking Paris.

Now the part that trips people up. Suppose you measure a stick with a ruler marked in millimetres, and it comes to 36.5 mm. That last digit, the 5, is a guess. The end of the stick fell somewhere between the 36 and the 37 marks, and you estimated how far between. With a ruler marked only in centimetres you would have written 3.6 cm, or perhaps 3.7, and the 6 or 7 would be the guess. The finer the marks, the more digits you can honestly write down, and the last one is always uncertain.

:::fig c0_rulers() | The same stick measured with three rulers. With marks every 10 cm you can only say "about 0.4 dm". With marks every centimetre, 3.6 or 3.7 cm. With marks every millimetre, 36.5 mm. In every case the last digit is an estimate between two marks.

Every measurement carries an ==uncertainty==: the range within which the true value probably lies. That range is honesty about what the instrument can tell you, and a careful measurement always states it. Physicists often write it as a percentage. If a kitchen scale reads 150 g for a katori of rice, and repeated weighings scatter by about 2 g either way, the uncertainty is 2 in 150, about 1.3%. (Those numbers are invented to show the method; your scale and your katori will give you real ones.)

:::note Uncertainty
The range within which a measurement's true value probably lies. Every measurement has one. As a percentage: uncertainty ÷ value × 100.
:::

:::predict
You weigh the same katori of rice five times on a kitchen scale, lifting it off and putting it back between weighings. What do you expect the five readings to look like?
- Exactly the same number every time
- Numbers that differ by a gram or two
- Numbers all over the place
???
Usually the second. The scale rounds to the nearest gram, the katori lands in slightly different spots, the air moves. The spread of your five readings is a direct measure of your uncertainty, and it is worth doing: it turns a vague "about 150 g" into "150 g, give or take 2".
:::

Two more words, which people mix up. A measurement is *accurate* if it is close to the true value. It is *precise* if repeating it gives nearly the same answer each time. They are not the same. A scale that reads 20 g with nothing on it will give you five very similar readings, all 20 g too high: precise and wrong. The cure is to check your instrument against something you trust, which is exactly why the world keeps standards like the old kilogram and its replacement.

:::careful
> "My scale gave the same reading five times, so it must be right."

Agreeing with itself is precision. Being right is accuracy. A scale can be beautifully precise and still read 20 g too high every single time. Check it: weigh something whose mass you know, such as a sealed 1 kg packet, and see what it says.
:::

:::school
Cambridge IGCSE defines mass as "a measure of the quantity of matter in an object". When the kitchen scale says 1 kg, that is the mass it means. Weight is something different, a force, and it arrives in Chapter 3.
:::

:::check
A friend measures a pencil with an ordinary school ruler and writes down 17.3482 cm. What has gone wrong?
???
A ruler marked in millimetres lets you read to the nearest millimetre and estimate one more digit: 17.35 cm at best. The 8 and the 2 are invented; the ruler cannot see them. Writing extra digits does not make a measurement better. It makes it dishonest about its uncertainty.
:::

## 0.3 Powers of ten

In the store cupboard there is a 25 kg sack of rice. How many grains are in it? Nobody is going to count them. And yet, with a teaspoon and a kitchen scale, you can know the answer well enough to be sure of it.

:::predict
How many grains of rice are in a 25 kg sack?
- About ten thousand
- About a million
- About a hundred million
- About ten billion
???
About a million, and the next section of text shows how you can find that out for yourself in ten minutes. Most people guess far too high or far too low, because big numbers all feel the same. The point of this section is to make them feel different.
:::

Here is the chain. Count the grains in one level teaspoon. Suppose you get about 200 (your own count will replace that number). Weigh ten level teaspoons together, because a kitchen scale cannot weigh one grain or even one teaspoon honestly; suppose ten teaspoons come to 50 g, so one teaspoon is 5 g. Then 200 grains weigh 5 g, so there are 40 grains in a gram, 40,000 in a kilogram, and 40,000 × 25 = 1,000,000 in the sack. A million. If your teaspoon count is 20% different from mine, your answer will be 20% different too, and that is fine. The question was "how many, roughly?", and for that kind of question an answer within a factor of ten either way counts as close enough.

:::home A teaspoon of rice
Tip a level teaspoon of rice onto a plate and push the grains into groups of ten with a finger; count the groups. Do it three times with three fresh teaspoons: the counts will not agree exactly, and the spread between them is your uncertainty from Section 0.2. Then weigh ten level teaspoons on the kitchen scale, divide by ten for one teaspoon, and work out grains per gram, grains per kilogram packet, and grains per 25 kg sack. Write the sack's number in the form "something × 10 to a power". Everything goes back in the rice tin afterwards.
:::

A million is written 1,000,000, and physicists prefer to write it 10⁶: a one followed by six zeros, or ten multiplied by itself six times. A thousand is 10³. A thousandth, 0.001, is 10⁻³. The little number is the ==power of ten==, and it is the single most useful number about any quantity, because it tells you the *scale* you are on. Numbers with the same power of ten are said to have the same ==order of magnitude==: 800 and 450 are both around 10², both "a few hundred", and for a rough question they count as the same size.

:::note Power of ten
The small raised number in 10⁶ or 10⁻³. It counts how many times ten has been multiplied (or, when negative, divided). 10⁶ is a million; 10⁻³ is a thousandth.
:::

:::note Order of magnitude
The power of ten of a quantity when it is written as a number times a power of ten. Two things of the same order of magnitude are within about a factor of ten of each other.
:::

:::figwide c0_ladder() | A ladder of sizes. Every step along it multiplies the length by ten. A proton, a hydrogen atom, a wave of light, a grain of sand, you, the Earth, the Sun, the distance from the Earth to the Sun, one light year and the Milky Way are 36 steps apart from end to end.

Look at where things sit on the ladder in Figure 0.3. A grain of sand is three steps below you: a thousand times smaller. A hydrogen atom is ten steps below you, and the Sun is nine steps above you, so from an atom to the Sun is nineteen steps of ten, which is a one followed by nineteen zeros. Each step *multiplies*. Ten times bigger, and then ten times bigger again, is not twenty times bigger; it is a hundred times bigger. People's intuition treats the steps as if they were additions, and that is why a million and a billion feel like neighbours when one is a thousand times the other. This is also why "too small to see" is not one size. A bacterium and an electron are both invisible, and in mass they are fifteen steps of ten apart, far more steps than separate you from the Sun.

The prefixes you already use are steps on this ladder: kilo means ×1,000 (10³), centi means ÷100 (10⁻²), milli means ÷1,000 (10⁻³), micro ÷1,000,000 (10⁻⁶) and nano ÷1,000,000,000 (10⁻⁹). Converting between them is just moving the decimal point. Figure 0.3 itself is drawn with each step the same width, which is called a logarithmic scale. Such a scale never reaches zero, because there is always another step down: a tenth, a hundredth, a thousandth, for ever.

The [Powers of Ten explorer](#explore-scale) at the front of this book lets you climb this ladder with your thumb, from a proton out to the Milky Way, with a readout of how many times bigger each thing is than the last. Spend a few minutes there now. The feel for scale you get from it will be used in every chapter after this one.

:::try Investigation 0.3
In the Powers of Ten explorer, start at yourself and zoom out until the Earth fills the view, counting the ×10 steps. Then zoom in from yourself to a hydrogen atom and count again. Which journey has more steps? Check your counts against Figure 0.3.
:::

:::careful
> "Ten times bigger, and then ten times bigger again, is twenty times bigger."

It is a hundred times bigger. Each step on the ladder multiplies by ten; it does not add ten. Three steps is a thousand times, six steps a million times. The steps on Figure 0.3 are drawn equal in width, but each one is ten times the jump of the one before.
:::

:::check
How many seconds are there in a day? Write the answer in the form "a number times a power of ten", and say how many steps up the ladder a year is from a day.
???
24 hours × 60 minutes × 60 seconds = 86,400 s, which is 8.64 × 10⁴ s. A year is about 365 days, so about 3.16 × 10⁷ s. That is between two and three steps up the ladder, since 365 lies between 100 and 1,000.
:::

## 0.4 Guess like a physicist

It is a monsoon afternoon in July, the lanes are rivers, and the news says 100 mm of rain has fallen on the city since morning. A hundred millimetres is ten centimetres: it would barely cover your ankle. How much water is that, over the whole of Mumbai?

:::predict
How much rain falls on the whole of Mumbai on a day with 100 mm of rainfall?
- A few swimming pools' worth
- A few thousand tonnes
- Tens of millions of tonnes
- More than the whole Arabian Sea
???
Tens of millions of tonnes. Hardly anyone guesses this right first time, because it needs two steps of reasoning rather than a feeling. By the end of this section you will be able to work it out on the back of a bus ticket.
:::

Physicists make rough estimates like this all the time, and they have a name for them: Fermi problems, after Enrico Fermi, who was famous for working out surprising numbers on the back of an envelope. The method has two steps. First, find quantities you can estimate or look up. Second, find the relationship that joins them to the thing you want. Then check that the units come out right.

:::story estimation

For the rain, the relationship is simple: the volume of water is the area it fell on multiplied by how deep it lies. Look at a map. Mumbai is a long, thin strip running north–south. Call it about 40 km long and about 15 km wide. That gives an area of about 600 km², which is 6 × 10⁸ m². The depth is 100 mm, or 0.1 m. So the volume is 6 × 10⁸ m² × 0.1 m = 6 × 10⁷ m³. A cubic metre of water has a mass of 1,000 kg, a tonne, so the rain weighs about 6 × 10¹⁰ kg: sixty million tonnes, in one day, on one city. Written as bottles, it is 6 × 10¹⁰ one-litre bottles: sixty billion of them.

:::fig c0_rain() | The whole city treated as one flat slab, 40 km by 15 km, with 0.1 m of rain lying on it. The arithmetic underneath turns the slab into a volume and then into a mass. The layer of rain is drawn thousands of times too thick; drawn to scale it would be thinner than this line.

Check the units before you believe it. Kilometres had to become metres before they met the depth in metres, and then m² × m = m³, a volume; volume × kg/m³ = kg, a mass. The units agreeing does not make an estimate right, but units that disagree always mean a mistake somewhere.

Now the honest question: how good is this number? Only as good as its weakest input. The city's area you can read off a map to within perhaps 20%. The rainfall is much shakier. The 100 mm was one gauge's reading, and rain over a city is patchy; Colaba and Borivali can have quite different days. If the real average depth was 50 mm, the answer halves. If it was 200 mm, it doubles. So the depth is the input worth improving, and you can improve it yourself.

:::home A rain gauge on the balcony
Ask an adult before you set anything up on a balcony or windowsill. Find a tall glass or jar with straight vertical sides: a bottle with a narrow neck will not do, because then the depth of water inside no longer equals the depth of rain. Stand it in the open, away from the overhang of the roof, inside a bucket or weighed down so the wind cannot take it. After a day of rain, measure the depth of water with a ruler, in millimetres. That is your rainfall, in the same units the weather office uses. Compare it with the figure in the news for your part of the city, and then redo the whole-city sum with your own number.
:::

The classic example is Fermi's own: how many piano tuners are there in Chicago? Nobody knows the inputs exactly, and it does not matter. Chicago had about 3 million people, perhaps 5 to a home, perhaps one home in ten with a piano, so about 60,000 pianos. If a tuner can tune about 750 pianos a year, the city needs about 80 tuners. Fermi rounded that to "about a hundred", and about a hundred is right. A blind guess might have been 10 or 100,000. An estimate like this is a chain of reasonable links, and anyone can check each link and tighten it; a wild guess offers nothing to check.

Notice how the answers are written: "about 100", "about 60 million tonnes". Never "60,300,000 tonnes". An estimate whose inputs are good to 20% cannot have an answer good to the nearest tonne, and writing extra digits pretends otherwise. One significant figure, sometimes two, is all an estimate has earned. This habit, of guessing first and only then measuring, runs through every chapter of this book. Before any experiment, ask what you expect, roughly. If the measurement comes out a thousand times off your estimate, one of them is wrong, and you will want to know which.

:::careful
> "An estimate is just a wild guess with a number on the end."

A guess is a feeling. An estimate is a chain: a few inputs you can defend, a relationship you can state, and units that check out. Anyone can look at the chain, point at the weakest link and replace it with a better number. That is why "about a hundred piano tuners" is worth far more than "lots".
:::

:::check
A new tower near your school has 40 storeys. Estimate its height, and say which input you trust least.
???
A storey is about 3 m (a ceiling a little above your reach, plus the floor between), so 40 × 3 m = 120 m, roughly. The storey height is the weak link: real storeys run from about 2.5 m to 4 m, so the answer could be 100 m or 160 m. Counting the storeys exactly does not help much; measuring one storey would.
:::

## The shadow at Syene

:::fig c0_eratosthenes() | Eratosthenes' idea. Sunlight reaches the Earth in parallel rays. At Syene a ray runs straight down the stick and into the well: no shadow. At Alexandria, further round the curve of the Earth, the same parallel sunlight meets an upright stick at an angle and casts a shadow. That shadow angle is exactly the angle between the two sticks at the centre of the Earth. The real angle is 7.2°; here it is drawn at 30° so that you can see it.

:::case The shadow at Syene
Back to Alexandria. Here is everything Eratosthenes had.

1. At noon on the longest day of the year, sunlight reached the bottom of a vertical well at Syene, far to the south. So at that moment the Sun stood exactly overhead at Syene.
2. In Alexandria, to the north, at that same noon, an upright column cast a shadow. From the lengths of the column and its shadow, the sunlight was arriving at an angle of 1/50 of a full circle away from straight down: about 7°, or exactly 7.2°.
3. The distance from Alexandria to Syene was known: 5,000 stadia, a stadion being the length of a Greek running track.
4. The Sun is enormously far away, about 150 million kilometres, so its rays reach the whole Earth travelling almost exactly parallel to one another. If everyone on Earth pointed at the Sun at the same instant, all their arms would be parallel.

Use Figure 0.5. What two things did Eratosthenes have to *assume*, beyond his observations? How far is it all the way round the Earth, in stadia? And why, more than two thousand years later, can nobody say exactly how close he came?
???
He assumed two things. The Earth is a sphere, which Aristotle's evidence had made reasonable. And sunlight arrives in parallel rays, which is true because the Sun is so far away.

Then the reasoning runs like this. "Straight up" at Syene points at the Sun. "Straight up" at Alexandria points 7.2° away from the Sun. Since the sunlight is parallel at both places, "straight up" at the two cities must differ by 7.2°, and on a sphere that can only mean the two cities are 7.2° apart around its curve, which is 1/50 of the way round. So the whole way round is 50 times the distance between them: 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia.

How good was that? The modern figure is 40,000 km. For Eratosthenes' number to equal it, a stadion would have to be 160 m. The trouble is that nobody knows which stadion he used; several lengths were in use. With the common Olympic stadion his answer comes out about 20% too big, roughly 48,000 km. With another stadion of about a sixth of a kilometre it comes out at about 41,700 km, about 4% too big. (Some books say "within 1%" for that stadion; redo the multiplication and you will find they are wrong.) So the honest statement is: the method was brilliant, the answer was somewhere between a few percent and about a fifth too large, and we cannot say exactly, because a measurement is a number *and* a unit, and the unit was never written down precisely enough.

There is a lovely echo. In 1791 the metre itself was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the pole. That made the circumference of the Earth 40,000 km *by definition*: Eratosthenes' question, answered by turning it into a unit.

One more thing. Mumbai lies south of the Tropic of Cancer, so twice a year, once in May and once in July, the noon Sun stands straight overhead and an upright stick casts no shadow at all. On those days Mumbai is Syene. A friend with a stick in a city far to the north could be Alexandria, and the two of you could measure the Earth with a phone call and a ruler.
:::

:::next
You can now separate what you see from what you think, measure something and say how sure you are, place any quantity on the ladder of tens, and make a guess that is worth something. What you cannot yet do is describe the simplest event in the world: a ball tossed into the air. Where is it, how fast is it going, which way, and how is that changing from one instant to the next? Those words, position, velocity and acceleration, took people two thousand years to get right. Chapter 1 gets them right in a train and a bathroom.
:::
