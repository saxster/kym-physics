Eratosthenes measured the Earth by keeping two things apart: what he saw, and what he thought it meant. This chapter turns that same habit on the most ordinary thing in the world, something moving. Before anyone could ask *why* a ball flies, falls and stops, they had to learn to say exactly *how* it moves: where it is, when, how fast, in which direction, and how fast that is changing. Getting those words right took about two thousand years. Physicists call this part of the subject kinematics, the describing of motion, and it is the grammar of everything that follows.

Here is the puzzle to carry through the chapter. Toss a ball straight up. It rises, slows, and for one instant at the very top it is not moving at all. Then it falls. Is anything happening to the ball at that instant? Most people say no: it has stopped, so nothing is going on. In one study, 44% of students said the ball's acceleration at the top is zero. By the end of this chapter you will film your own toss in slow motion and read the answer off the bathroom tiles.

## 1.1 Where and when

:::scene Churchgate station, Mumbai, a weekday morning

The local pulls out so gently that for a moment it is the platform that seems to slide backwards, with its pillars and its tea stall and the people on it. Then your body catches up with what your eyes are saying and the world rights itself: the train is moving, the platform is still. But stop and ask how you know. If the windows were painted over and the ride were perfectly smooth, could anything inside the carriage tell you whether it was moving or standing at the platform? You could drop a coin, pour water, toss a ball, bounce it. Every one of those would happen exactly as it does in your kitchen.

Galileo made this point with a ship. Shut yourself in a windowless cabin on a ship moving steadily over calm water, and nothing you can do in the cabin will tell you whether the ship is moving or tied up at the quay. Steady motion cannot be felt. The word *steady* is doing all the work in that sentence; a braking train is felt by everybody, and that is the subject of Section 1.3 and of Chapter 2. But as long as the motion is steady, "is it moving?" turns out to be a question about what you are comparing the train with, and about nothing else.

That comparison is called a ==reference frame==: the thing you choose to measure positions from, together with the directions you measure along. Standing on the platform you use the station as your frame, and the train moves. Sitting in the train you use the carriage as your frame, and the platform moves. Neither frame is the true one. The frame is the viewpoint you have chosen, not a backdrop that comes with the world, and once you have chosen it, every ==position== is a location measured within it: three metres down the carriage, or two hundred metres north of the station.

:::note Reference frame
The thing you choose to measure positions from, with a set of directions: the ground, a train, a boat. All steadily moving frames are equally good; motion only means something relative to a stated frame.
:::

:::note Position
Where something is, measured from a chosen point in a chosen frame: "3 m from the back of the carriage", "200 m north of the station".
:::

:::predict
Your friend walks steadily along a corridor, bouncing a ball as she goes. To her the ball goes straight up and down beside her hand. You stand still at the end of the corridor and watch. What path do you see the ball trace?
- Straight up and down, the same as she sees
- A zigzag: up and forward, down and forward, over and over
- It depends how fast she walks: a zigzag if she hurries, straight up and down if she strolls
???
A zigzag, however slowly she walks, because between one bounce and the next the ball has moved along the corridor with her. For her the ball goes straight up and down. Neither of you is wrong. You are describing the same motion in two different frames, and each description is exactly right in its own frame.
:::

:::fig c1_two_frames() | One bouncing ball, two frames. Seen by a friend walking alongside, it goes straight up and down. Seen by someone standing still, it zigzags down the corridor. Both pictures are true; each belongs to the frame it was drawn in.

Try it in a corridor at home or at school, taking turns to be the walker and the watcher. Then try it in your head on the train. You walk towards the front of a carriage at an easy 5 km/h while the train does 80 km/h along the track. Relative to the carriage you are moving at 5 km/h. Relative to the track you are moving at 85 km/h, and if you turn round and walk towards the back, at 75 km/h. All three numbers are true at the same time; each belongs to its frame. To change frames you simply add the frame's own velocity, which is the whole secret of this chapter's closing case. (The train's 80 km/h here is a round number for the sum, not a timetable figure.)

Two more words have to be separated, because everyday speech blurs them. A cyclist rides 3 km west, turns round and rides 2 km east. The ==distance== she has travelled is 5 km: the length of the whole path, the number her bicycle's odometer would show. Her ==displacement== is 1 km west: where she ended up compared with where she began, with the direction. If she had ridden back to her starting point her distance would be 6 km and her displacement zero. Displacement is a ==vector==, a quantity with a size and a direction. Distance is a ==scalar==, a size alone. The difference seems like hair-splitting until Section 1.2, where it makes two quantities that sound alike come out completely different.

:::note Distance and displacement
Distance is the length of the path travelled, a scalar. Displacement is the change in position, from start to finish, with its direction: a vector.
:::

:::note Vector and scalar
A vector has a size and a direction (displacement, velocity, force). A scalar has a size only (distance, speed, mass, time).
:::

:::careful
> "The platform is really still and the train is really moving. The ground is the true frame; the train's view is an illusion."

For steady motion there is no experiment, inside or outside the train, that can crown one frame the true one. The ground is the handy frame, because we live on it, and that is all. Keep the word *steady*, though. The moment the train brakes you can tell, with your eyes shut, that something has changed. Steady velocity cannot be felt; a change of velocity can. That difference is the hinge of Chapter 2.
:::

:::school
Cambridge IGCSE sorts quantities into *scalars*, which have size only, like mass, and *vectors*, which have size and direction, like weight. Distance is a scalar and displacement a vector in exactly that sense.
:::

:::check
You walk from your front door to the shop at the corner, 300 m away, and come back. What distance did you cover, and what was your displacement?
???
Distance 600 m: the whole path, there and back. Displacement zero: you ended where you started, so your position did not change at all.
:::

## 1.2 How fast, and which way

Take the corridor again, with a phone stopwatch and a chalk mark every metre. Walk six metres in three seconds. Your ==speed== was 6 m ÷ 3 s = 2 m/s, two metres every second. That is the first and simplest number about motion: speed is distance divided by the time it took. A car that covers 150 km in 3.2 hours averaged 47 km/h. A student who walks 205 m at a steady 1.4 m/s takes 205 ÷ 1.4 = 146 s, about two and a half minutes. A cricket ball, a train, a planet: speed is always distance over time, and its unit in physics is metres per second.

:::note Speed
How fast something covers distance: distance travelled divided by the time taken, in metres per second (m/s). A scalar.
:::

But "the car averaged 47 km/h" hides everything that happened on the way: traffic, a stop for chai, a fast stretch on the Sea Link. The speedometer shows something different, the speed *right now*. To get at that, physicists take the distance covered in a shorter and shorter time: the last minute, the last second, the last hundredth of a second. As the interval shrinks, the average over it settles down to one value, the ==instantaneous speed==, the speed at that instant. Every speedometer is an instrument for doing that sum.

:::note Instantaneous speed
The speed at one instant, found by taking the average speed over a smaller and smaller time around that instant. It is what a speedometer shows.
:::

Now bring back the vector from Section 1.1. An auto-rickshaw takes you 3 km to the market and 3 km back in half an hour. Its average speed is 6 km ÷ 0.5 h = 12 km/h. Its average ==velocity== is zero. Velocity is displacement divided by time, and the auto's displacement was zero: it ended where it began. Velocity is the vector version of speed, with a direction attached: 1.7 m/s north; 2 m/s towards the front of the carriage; 12 km/h, but which way? The distinction matters because the laws of motion in the next chapters are about velocity, direction included. A thing that turns a corner at a steady speed has changed its velocity, and Section 1.3 takes that further.

:::note Velocity
Displacement divided by the time taken: speed together with a direction. A vector. A round trip has an average velocity of zero whatever its average speed.
:::

The best tool for seeing all of this at once is a graph with time along the bottom and position up the side.

:::fig c1_graph_walk() | A walk along a corridor as a position–time graph: forward 6 m in 3 s, standing still for 3 s, back 3 m in 3 s, then still again. The steeper the line, the faster the walk. A flat line means standing still, and a line sloping downwards means walking back towards the start. The dashed line is a friend who starts at the far end and strolls back at a steady 0.5 m/s; the circles mark the two moments when the two of you are at the same place.
:::live graphwalk

Read Figure 1.2 slowly. For the first three seconds the line climbs steadily: two metres of position for every second of time, which is a velocity of 2 m/s. Then the line goes flat: time passes but position does not change, so you were standing still. Then it slopes downwards: position is decreasing, so you were walking back, and it drops 3 m in 3 s, a velocity of 1 m/s in the backward direction, which we would write as −1 m/s if forward is positive. The *slope* of a position–time graph is the velocity. Steep means fast; flat means still; downhill means going the other way. If the line were curved, with its slope changing from moment to moment, the slope at any one point would be the instantaneous velocity there.

:::story motion-graphs

:::predict
In Figure 1.2 your line and your friend's dashed line cross at 4 seconds. At that moment, which of these is true?
- You are at the same place
- You are moving at the same speed
- Both
???
Only the first. Crossing lines mean the same position at the same time, nothing more. Your speed is the slope of your line, which is flat: zero. Your friend's slope is 0.5 m/s. You are standing still as she walks past you. The same thing happens at 8 seconds, when you overtake her on your way back, walking twice as fast as she does. Being in the same place at the same time says nothing about how fast either of you is going.
:::

:::careful
> "The graph shows the path the walker took."

The horizontal axis is *time*, not a direction in space. The walk in Figure 1.2 happened along one straight corridor; the graph's rising and falling shows position changing as the seconds pass. A curved position–time graph does not mean a curved path. When you meet the graph of a thrown ball in Section 1.5 this will matter.
:::

:::school
Cambridge Lower Secondary calls this a *distance–time graph*, with speed as its gradient; IGCSE topic 1.2, Motion, uses the same graphs. Ours is a *position*–time graph, which is why it can slope downwards when you walk back. A graph of distance travelled can only ever climb.
:::

:::five Velocity
? What does it measure?
How fast an object's position is changing and in which direction: displacement divided by time taken, measured in metres per second (m/s).
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *velocitas*, swiftness, from *velox*, swift. Everyday English uses *velocity* as a grand word for speed, which is misleading: in physics, velocity always includes the direction of travel.
? What problem did it solve?
Speed alone cannot tell you where a moving thing will end up: walking at 2 m/s north and walking at 2 m/s south have the same speed and take you to opposite ends of the street.
? Why does it work?
Because treating motion as an arrow with both a size and a direction lets you add motions in different frames of reference and read velocity straight off the slope of a position–time graph.
? How does it help?
It lets you work out where two trains on the Western line will meet, how a boat crosses a flowing river, and why turning a corner at steady speed still counts as a change of motion.
?? How do we know?
Every frame-transformation experiment, from walking down a moving train carriage to tracking aircraft in a crosswind, shows that positions and velocities add as directed arrows (vectors), not as plain numbers.
?? What it isn't
It is not average speed along a winding path: an auto-rickshaw that drives 3 km to the market and 3 km home in half an hour has an average speed of 12 km/h and an average velocity of zero.
?? Where does it stop working?
Adding velocities by simple addition works for walks, trains and rockets, and breaks down only when speeds approach the speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s), where Einstein's relativity takes over.
:::

:::check
Does a car's odometer measure distance or displacement? Does its speedometer show speed or velocity?
???
The odometer adds up distance: every kilometre driven counts, whichever way you went, so a round trip still adds to it. The speedometer shows speed, the size of the velocity at that instant, with no direction. If it showed velocity it would need a compass as well.
:::

## 1.3 Speeding up, slowing down and turning

Back in the local. At a steady 60 km/h you feel nothing; you could be sitting in your kitchen. What you do feel is the pull-away from the platform and the brake coming into the next station: the moments when the velocity is *changing*. The rate of that change is what physicists call ==acceleration==, and getting it right needs more care than the everyday word suggests.

Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time the change took. Say a train starts from rest and reaches 30 km/h, which is 8.3 m/s, in 20 s. Its velocity changed by 8.3 m/s in 20 s, so its acceleration was 8.3 ÷ 20 = 0.42 metres per second *per second*: every second, it gained another 0.42 m/s. The unit is written m/s², "metres per second squared", which just means (metres per second) per second. Now say the same train, running at 30 km/h, brakes to a stop in 8 s. Its velocity changed by 8.3 m/s again, this time downwards, in 8 s: an acceleration of about 1.0 m/s², pointing backwards, against the direction of motion. If forward is the positive direction we write it as −1.0 m/s².

:::note Acceleration
The change in velocity divided by the time taken for the change, in m/s². It is a vector: it points in the direction the velocity is *changing*, which is not always the direction the object is moving.
:::

:::work
Pulling away: 0 → 8.3 m/s in 20 s
acceleration = 8.3 m/s ÷ 20 s = 0.42 m/s²
Braking: 8.3 → 0 m/s in 8 s
acceleration = −8.3 m/s ÷ 8 s = −1.0 m/s²
:::

So slowing down is an acceleration too. Everyday English keeps the word for speeding up and uses "deceleration" for slowing, and you may use that word if you like; in physics it just means an acceleration that points against the velocity. What you must not do is read the minus sign as "slowing down". The sign only tells you which way the acceleration points along the axis *you* chose. Figure 1.3 shows the four cases.

:::fig c1_four_cars() | Four cars, with the positive direction to the right. The v arrow shows which way the car moves; the a arrow shows which way its velocity is changing. Cars (a) and (c) are speeding up, though (c) has a negative acceleration. Cars (b) and (d) are slowing down, though (d) has a positive acceleration. The rule that never fails: arrows the same way, speeding up; arrows opposite, slowing down.

Now the part that surprises almost everyone. Velocity is a vector, so it changes when its size changes *or when its direction changes*. An auto taking a roundabout at a steady 20 km/h is accelerating the whole way round, because its velocity is swinging from north to west to south even though the speedometer never moves. The acceleration points towards the inside of the bend, which is where the velocity is being pulled. You know this in your body: on the roundabout you lean, and when the auto straightens out you stop leaning. The same thing that lets you feel braking lets you feel turning, and in Chapter 3 it will be the same thing that lets a force do its work.

:::predict
An auto-rickshaw goes all the way round a roundabout at a perfectly steady 20 km/h. Is it accelerating?
- No: its speed is constant, so its acceleration is zero
- Yes: its direction keeps changing, so its velocity keeps changing
- Only on the parts where the driver speeds up
???
Yes, the whole way round. Its velocity is changing because its direction is changing, even though its size is not. You can feel it: your body leans towards the outside the entire time you are on the curve.
:::

:::careful
> "A negative acceleration means slowing down."

Look at car (c) in Figure 1.3: it moves left and its velocity arrow is getting longer, so it is speeding up, yet its acceleration is negative because it points left and we called right positive. The sign depends on a choice you made. The arrows do not. Compare the directions of v and a: the same way means speeding up, opposite ways means slowing down, every time.
:::

:::school
The IGCSE syllabus says a resultant force "may change the velocity of an object by changing its direction of motion or its speed". Both of those are changes of velocity, so both are accelerations. That is why physicists count turning a corner as accelerating.
:::

:::five Acceleration
? What does it measure?
How quickly velocity is changing, whether in speed, in direction, or in both, measured in metres per second every second (m/s²).
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *accelerare*, to hasten or quicken (*ad-* towards + *celer* swift). The everyday name misleads you into thinking it means only speeding up; in physics, slowing down and turning a corner are accelerations too.
? What problem did it solve?
Before Galileo, thinkers tried to link falling speed to distance fallen and got into tangles. Defining acceleration as the change in velocity per unit of time made the law of falling simple: every falling body gains the same 9.8 m/s of downward velocity each second.
? Why does it work?
Because forces in nature do not set an object's velocity directly; they set the rate at which its velocity changes from moment to moment.
? How does it help?
It explains why a ball at the very top of its throw still has a downward acceleration of 9.8 m/s² even at the instant its velocity passes through zero, and predicts the 1, 4, 9, 16 square-law distances on Galileo's ramp.
?? How do we know?
Stroboscopic photographs, slow-motion phone video against bathroom tiles, and Galileo's inclined-plane timings all show consecutive position gaps changing by a constant amount in every equal time step.
?? What it isn't
It is not velocity: a ball at the top of a toss has zero velocity and full acceleration, while a train cruising at 80 km/h on a straight track has high velocity and zero acceleration.
?? Where does it stop working?
The constant-acceleration formula *d* = ½*at*² holds only while the acceleration stays steady; when air drag grows on a fast-falling raindrop, acceleration shrinks toward zero and you must step through the motion bit by bit.
:::

:::check
A train is moving to the left and comes smoothly to a stop. Taking right as the positive direction, what is the direction of its acceleration, and what is its sign?
???
The train is slowing, so its acceleration points opposite to its velocity: to the right. With right as positive, that is a positive acceleration, even though the train is slowing down.
:::

## 1.4 Galileo's ramps

:::scene Padua, about 1604

Galileo Galilei, working in Padua, wanted to know exactly how a falling thing speeds up. Does its speed grow steadily with time? Does the distance it has fallen grow steadily? Something else? A dropped ball reaches the floor in well under a second, far too fast for anything he had to time it. So he did something that looks like cheating and is actually one of the great ideas in physics: he slowed the fall down. A ball rolling down a gentle slope gathers speed in the same *way* as a falling ball, only more lazily. Make the slope shallow enough and you can watch the whole thing happen, and time it with a steady beat.

:::story p-galileo

:::predict
A ball starts from rest at the top of a long ramp. In the first second it rolls 10 cm. How far from the start is it after two seconds?
- 20 cm: twice the time, twice the distance
- 30 cm
- 40 cm: twice the time, four times the distance
???
40 cm. After three seconds it is 90 cm from the start, and after four seconds 160 cm. Doubling the time multiplies the distance by four; trebling it, by nine. Almost everyone's first instinct is 20 cm, and the next few paragraphs explain why that instinct is wrong.
:::

Here is what Galileo found, and what you can find on your own floor. Mark where the ball is after one beat, two beats, three beats and four. The marks are not evenly spaced. If the first mark is one unit from the start, the next are at 4, 9 and 16 units: the square of the number of beats. Look instead at the gaps between the marks: 1, then 3, then 5, then 7. The odd numbers, one after another. Every extra beat adds the next odd number to the distance.

:::fig c1_ramp() | A ball rolling down a ramp, drawn at five equally spaced moments. From the start the distances are 0, 1, 4, 9 and 16 units, the squares of the beat numbers, and the gaps between the moments are 1, 3, 5 and 7, the odd numbers in order. The ramp is drawn much steeper than Galileo would have used it; a real one is nearly flat, so that the whole run takes several seconds.

Why squares? Because what grows steadily is the *velocity*, not the distance. With a constant acceleration the ball gains the same extra speed in every beat: say 20 cm/s per beat. During the first beat its speed rises from 0 to 20 cm/s, averaging 10 cm/s, so it covers 10 cm. During the second beat it rises from 20 to 40, averaging 30, and covers 30 cm; that is the gap of 3. During the third beat, 40 to 60, average 50, gap 5. The distances from the start are 10, 40, 90, 160 cm: the squares. A graph of velocity against time is a straight line climbing from zero, and the distance covered is the area under that line, a triangle, which is why it comes out as half of the acceleration times the time squared.

:::eq
distance from rest = ½ × acceleration × time²
:::

:::work
Speed gained each second: 20 cm/s
After 1 s: speed 20 cm/s; average over the second 10 cm/s; distance 10 cm
After 2 s: speed 40 cm/s; distance = ½ × 20 × 2² = 40 cm
After 3 s: speed 60 cm/s; distance = ½ × 20 × 3² = 90 cm
After 4 s: distance = ½ × 20 × 4² = 160 cm
:::

The slope of the ramp changes only how big the acceleration is. Gentler slope, smaller acceleration, longer beats; but the marks still fall at 1, 4, 9, 16, and the gaps still go 1, 3, 5, 7. That is what let Galileo claim his ramp was telling him about falling: tilt it steeper and steeper and nothing in the pattern changes, so the pattern must hold for the steepest "ramp" of all, straight down. The numbers 1, 3, 5, 7 are ours to check, by the way: neither of this book's main sources prints them, so we computed them from distance ∝ time², and you can too.

:::evidence Galileo's ramps | Padua, about 1604
Galileo's inclined-plane experiments are well documented; it was with ramps, not with drops from towers, that he worked out how falling things speed up. How he kept time is less certain. The historian Stillman Drake concluded that he sang a steady beat, with small gut frets fixed across the ramp so that the ball clicked as it passed each one, and that he moved the frets until the clicks fell exactly on the beats. The frets then sat at 1, 4, 9, 16. The story goes that way; it is one historian's reconstruction, and the ramps themselves are the part that is certain.
:::

:::home Galileo's ramp with coins for bells
You need a straight board about 2 m long, such as a plank or a long shelf, a marble or a smooth small ball, and masking tape. Prop one end of the board on a single book, so that the slope is as gentle as it can be while the marble still rolls all the way down; a gentle slope makes the beats long and easy to hear. Put tape marks at 10, 40, 90 and 160 cm from the top, measuring with a tape. At each mark, tape a small folded tab of stiff paper or kitchen foil so its tip hangs down and gives a crisp *tick* as the marble brushes past, or set a coin just to the side of the marble's line so the marble grazes its rim (if the marble has to climb *over* a coin at the 10 cm mark, it is still moving so slowly that the coin's edge will stop it). Release the marble from rest at the top, with no push, and listen: the four clicks should come at an even beat, like a slow clock. If the beats bunch up, the release was a push or the board is not straight. Filming it in slow motion and counting frames between clicks is a stricter test, and the markers are optional: with video you can mark where the marble is at equal time steps instead. Everything here is kitchen-safe; keep the marble away from small children.
:::

:::careful
> "The ball speeds up steadily, so the distance must grow steadily too."

The speed grows steadily: 20, 40, 60 cm/s. The distance does not, because a faster ball covers more ground in each beat than it did in the last. Distance grows with the *square* of the time: 1, 4, 9, 16. Mixing these two up is the commonest slip in all of kinematics, and Galileo's marks are the cure.
:::

:::check
A ball starts from rest and its speed grows by 4 m/s every second. After 2 seconds, is its speed 8 m/s or 16 m/s? How far has it travelled?
???
Its speed is 8 m/s: it gained 4 m/s in each of two seconds. The 16 belongs to the *distance* pattern, not the speed. Its distance is ½ × 4 × 2² = 8 m, which you can also get as average speed × time: it averaged 4 m/s over the two seconds, so 4 × 2 = 8 m.
:::

## 1.5 The top of a throw

Now the puzzle this chapter opened with. A physics lecture at MIT begins exactly this way: the lecturer tosses a marker into the air and asks, at the highest point, is its velocity zero? And is its acceleration zero? The first answer is easy. For one instant at the top the marker is going neither up nor down, so its velocity is zero. The second answer is where 44% of students go wrong, and where everything you have learnt in this chapter pays off.

:::predict
At the very top of a toss the ball's velocity is zero. What is its acceleration at that instant?
- Zero: it has stopped, so nothing is changing
- 9.8 m/s² downwards, exactly as it is on the way up and on the way down
- 9.8 m/s² upwards, because the ball was going up
- Something smaller than 9.8 m/s², because the ball is barely moving
???
9.8 m/s² downwards, the same as at every other instant of the flight. Acceleration is the rate at which velocity *changes*, and at the top the velocity is changing as fast as ever: from a little upward to a little downward. It passes through zero without pausing there. The ball's position stops changing for an instant. Its velocity never stops changing at all.
:::

Think about what acceleration means: how fast the velocity is changing, which is a different thing from speed. Use the numbers for a toss that rises about one metre. The ball leaves your hand at about 4.4 m/s. Every tenth of a second, gravity takes almost 1 m/s off its upward velocity: 4.4, then 3.5, 2.5, 1.5, 0.5, then −0.5, −1.5, −2.4 and so on, where the minus sign means "downwards". Read along that list and find the top. It is somewhere between 0.5 and −0.5, around 0.45 s, and nothing special happens there. The velocity does not slow its change, hesitate or pause. It goes through zero at the same steady rate, 9.8 m/s every second, that it had all the way up and keeps all the way down. Zero velocity, for an instant; full acceleration, the whole time.

:::fig c1_toss_strobe() | A one-metre toss in front of a tiled wall, drawn every tenth of a second: going up on the left, coming down on the right. The gaps between successive positions are 39, 30, 20 and 10 cm on the way up, a mere 0.2 cm across the top, then 10, 19, 29 and 39 cm on the way down. Every gap is about 9.8 cm shorter than the one before, straight through the top. Tiles 10 cm apart; the ball's size is not to scale.
:::live videotrack

You can see the same thing in the positions, which is what a camera records. Figure 1.5 marks the ball every tenth of a second. Going up, the gaps between marks are 39 cm, then 30, 20 and 10. At the top the ball barely moves: 0.2 cm between the 0.4 s and 0.5 s marks. Coming down, the gaps are 10, 19, 29, 39. Now subtract each gap from the one before it: the gaps shrink by 9.8 cm every tenth of a second, on the way up, over the top and on the way down, with no break in the pattern. That steady shrinking of the gaps *is* the acceleration, made visible. A ball that really stopped changing at the top would have two equal gaps there. It never does.

:::work
A toss that rises 1.0 m leaves the hand at 4.4 m/s and reaches the top after 0.45 s.
Height every 0.1 s: 0, 39, 69, 89, 99, 99, 89, 70, 41, 2 cm (the two 99s are 98.7 and 98.9 cm before rounding).
Gaps: 39, 30, 20, 10, 0.2, −10, −19, −29, −39 cm.
Change in each gap: about −9.8 cm every 0.1 s, the whole way.
:::

Why 9.8? Once the ball has left your hand, nothing is pushing it up any more. The only things acting on it are the Earth's pull and a little air. The Earth's pull does not switch off at the top of the flight, or weaken, or wait for the ball to stop; it is the same pull at the top as everywhere else, and it changes the ball's velocity at the same rate everywhere: 9.8 m/s every second, downwards. Physicists call this motion ==free fall==, and it includes the way up as well as the way down, because the ball is in the grip of gravity alone from the moment it leaves your hand. The number 9.8 m/s² is written *g*, the acceleration of free fall. It is 9.78 at some places on Earth and 9.83 at others, so 9.8 is a good average for anywhere, including Mumbai.

:::note Free fall
Motion under gravity alone, with nothing pushing, holding or throwing, and air resistance small enough to ignore. A tossed ball is in free fall from the moment it leaves the hand, going up as well as coming down.
:::

:::note g, the acceleration of free fall
About 9.8 m/s², downwards, everywhere near the Earth's surface (9.78 to 9.83 depending on where you are). Every object in free fall changes its velocity by 9.8 m/s every second.
:::

Velocity is one straight line sloping down from +4.4 m/s to −4.4 m/s; it passes through zero at 0.45 s and keeps right on going. Acceleration is one flat line at −9.8 m/s², top included. The height graph looks like the ball's path, but it is not: the ball went straight up and straight down, and the horizontal axis is time.

:::home Film your own toss
Find a wall with tiles, or stick strips of tape on a plain wall at measured 10 cm intervals, and measure a tile with a ruler so you know its real height. Set the phone camera to its slow-motion mode, which on many phones films at 120 or 240 frames a second; check what yours does. Prop the phone against a book a few metres back, in bright light, so that it does not move. Toss a ball about a metre straight up, catch it, and stop filming. Now step through the clip. Do not compare one frame with the next: at 240 frames a second the gap changes by less than a fifth of a millimetre per frame, which no camera will show you against a tile. Instead, mark the ball's height every tenth of a second, which is every 24th frame at 240 frames a second, every 12th at 120, every 3rd at 30. Write down the heights in tiles and work out the gaps. Going up they should shrink by about one tile each time, pass through almost nothing at the top, and grow by about one tile each time coming down. The live version of Figure 1.5 does the marking for you, frame by frame, and plots the graph; the video stays on your phone.
:::

:::careful
> "At the top it has stopped, so nothing is happening to it. It just hangs there for a moment."

It does not hang. The heights at 0.4 s and 0.5 s differ by 0.2 cm, and that is the nearest it ever comes to a pause. Its velocity is changing at 9.8 m/s every second through that instant, exactly as it was a moment before and will be a moment after. Zero velocity and zero acceleration are different things: one says the ball is not moving right now, the other would say its motion is not changing, and the second is false. There is a related wrong idea, that the push of your hand "runs out" at the top. Once the ball has left your hand there is no upward push on it at all, from the first instant of its flight. What carries it upwards is a question for Chapter 2.
:::

:::school
IGCSE writes "the acceleration of free fall *g* is approximately 9.8 m/s²", which is exactly the number used here. You will also meet *g* written as 9.8 N/kg; that is the same number read from a different side, and Chapter 4 explains how.
:::

:::check
A ball thrown almost straight up passes a coconut on the way up, misses it, and passes it again on the way down. Ignoring air, how do its speeds compare at the coconut the two times?
???
They are the same size, in opposite directions. On the way up the ball loses 9.8 m/s of speed every second over the climb from the coconut to the top; on the way down it gains speed at the same rate over the same height, so it arrives back at the coconut with exactly the speed it had there before, now pointing down.
:::

## Two trains on the Western line

:::fig c1_trains() | Three trains on parallel tracks, with their velocities relative to the track. Relative to your window, the faster train on one side creeps past at 10 km/h, and the train coming the other way flashes past at 120 km/h. The speeds are round numbers chosen for the puzzle, not timetable data.

:::case Two trains on the Western line
You are on a local heading north at a steady 60 km/h, by the window, with a clear view out of both sides of the carriage.

1. On one side, a train on the next track appears beside you and slides slowly past, so slowly that you watch one passenger reading his newspaper for five or six seconds before his window leaves yours behind. Relative to your window it is moving at about 10 km/h, forwards.
2. On the other side, a train flashes past in the opposite direction. Relative to your window it passes at about 120 km/h.
3. The passenger with the newspaper looks up, sees your carriage drifting backwards past his window, and says to his neighbour: "Look, that train is going backwards."

What is each of the other two trains doing relative to the track: how fast, and which way? And is the man with the newspaper wrong?
???
To change frames, add the frame's own velocity, as in Section 1.1. The slow train moves at 10 km/h forwards relative to you, and you move at 60 km/h north relative to the track, so relative to the track it is doing 60 + 10 = 70 km/h north: the same direction as you, slightly faster, which is why it overtakes so gently. The flashing train moves at 120 km/h relative to you, the other way; relative to the track that is 120 − 60 = 60 km/h south. It is doing exactly your speed, in the opposite direction, and the two speeds add up to the 120 km/h you saw. In metres per second, 10 km/h is about 2.8 m/s, a brisk walk, and 120 km/h is about 33 m/s.

The man with the newspaper is not wrong. In his train's frame your carriage really does move backwards, at 10 km/h. In the track's frame his train does 70 km/h north and yours 60. In your frame his train creeps forward at 10. Every one of those statements is a complete and correct description of the same motion, each in its own frame. What would be wrong is to say that one of them is what is "really" happening and the others are illusions. For steady motion, no frame has that right.
:::

:::next
You can now say exactly how something moves: its position in a chosen frame, its velocity, and its acceleration, and you have seen that a ball at the top of its flight has zero velocity and full acceleration at the same instant. But a question has been left hanging since the top of the throw. After the ball leaves your hand, nothing pushes it upwards, yet it keeps rising for almost half a second. What carries it? Every twelve-year-old has an answer to that, and so did Aristotle, and they are the same answer, and it is wrong. Chapter 2 is about why things keep moving.
:::
