In Chapter 1 you learned to describe motion without asking where it came from: where a thing is, how fast it is going and in which direction, and how its velocity changes from one moment to the next. You filmed a ball at the top of its throw and found that even at the instant it hung still, its velocity was changing. Describing is one job. Explaining is another, and this chapter starts on it.

Here is the puzzle to carry with you. A local train is slowing into a station, and the driver brakes harder than usual. Everyone standing lurches towards the front of the compartment. A school bag slides along the floor. A man dozing against the pole wakes with a jolt, looking round for whoever shoved him. Nobody did. The train has no arms. Nothing in the carriage reached out and pushed those people forward, and yet they all moved. By the end of this chapter you will be able to say exactly what happened to them, and the answer turns out to be the most important single idea about force in the whole book: a force changes how something moves. Keeping it moving needs no force at all.

## 2.1 Aristotle's world

:::scene Mumbai, a weekday evening

Start with something you can do before you read another line. Put a book flat on the table and give it a shove. It slides a hand's width, slows, and stops. Shove it harder and it slides further, and stops. Flick a steel katori across the kitchen counter: the same. Everything you have ever pushed has behaved this way, and from all that experience you have built a rule without ever writing it down. It goes something like this: *things move while something pushes them. Take the push away and they slow down and stop. Moving needs a mover.*

That rule has a famous author. Aristotle, in ancient Greece, reasoned his way to the same conclusion from the same kind of evidence: a cart moves while the ox pulls it, and stops when the ox stops. Everything that moves, he said, is moved by something. For nearly two thousand years this was physics, and it deserves respect. Aristotle described what really happens on Earth, where every moving thing is rubbing against a floor or pushing through air. He was right about the facts. He was wrong about the cause, and the mistake is so natural that most people make it today. In 1985 two physicists, Halloun and Hestenes, gave a careful test to 478 university students in the United States before their first physics course. Sixty-five per cent agreed that every motion has a cause, something that keeps it going; only 17 per cent reasoned the way Newton did.

A ==force== is the physicist's word for a push or a pull. It has a size and a direction, and, as you will see in Chapter 3, it always comes from some other object: a hand, a rope, the floor, the Earth. The question this chapter asks is what forces actually *do*. Aristotle's answer was that they make things move. Hold that answer up against the sliding book and see whether it survives.

:::note Force
A push or a pull on an object, with a size and a direction. It is always exerted by some other object. Measured in newtons (N), a unit you meet in Chapter 3.
:::

:::predict
You shove a book across the table and let go. It slows down and stops. Why does it stop?
- The push you gave it has been used up.
- Nothing is pushing it any more, so it stops on its own.
- Something is pushing it backwards the whole time it slides.
???
Something is pushing it backwards: the table, through friction. You cannot see that push, and that is exactly why it took two thousand years to notice. Aristotle would have chosen the second answer, and most students today choose the first. The rest of this chapter earns the third one, and Section 2.5 puts friction on the scales to prove it is a real force.
:::

:::fig c2_book_slide() | Three moments in the life of a shoved book. While your hand pushes, it speeds up. After you let go, it slows and stops. The chapter's question is drawn as the dashed arrow: what, if anything, is pushing it backwards?

The hard thing about the sliding book is that the slowing-down is so ordinary that it hides in plain sight. Nobody asks why a book stops, any more than they ask why water is wet. To see the question at all you have to do what Chapter 0 taught: separate what you *observe* from the *model* you use to explain it. "The book slid thirty centimetres and stopped" is an observation. "It stopped because its push ran out" is a model, and so is "it stopped because the table pushed it backwards". Two models, one observation. The next three sections decide between them.

:::recall 0
In Chapter 0 you kept observations and models apart. Sort these three sentences about a katori flicked across the counter: "it slid about 40 cm and stopped"; "it stopped because the push you gave it ran out"; "it stopped because the counter pushed back on it".
???
The first is the observation: anyone watching would agree on it. The other two are models, stories about the cause. Both explain the same observation, so the observation alone cannot choose between them. You need a new observation that the two models predict differently, which is what Section 2.3 provides.
:::

:::check
Think of three things you pushed today that stopped after you let go. For each one, name what was touching it while it slowed down.
???
Whatever you chose (a door, a chair, a cup slid across the table, a ball rolled on the floor), something was touching it as it slowed: the floor, the table, its hinges, and always the air. Each of those is a candidate for the hidden backward push. Nothing you pushed today stopped while touching nothing at all.
:::

## 2.2 The arrow problem

:::scene Alexandria, 6th century

Aristotle's rule had one awkward case, and thinkers argued about it for a thousand years. An archer draws a bow and lets go. The string pushes the arrow for a fraction of a second, and then the arrow is on its own, flying across a field with nothing touching it. If moving needs a mover, what is moving the arrow? Aristotle's own answer was that the air behind it pushes it along. In Alexandria, in the sixth century, a scholar named John Philoponus found that hard to believe, and argued instead that the bow must give the arrow something that stays with it as it flies. Five hundred years later, in Bukhara, Ibn Sina discussed the idea in his *Book of Healing* and gave the something a name, *mayl*, an inclination. In the fourteenth century, in Paris, Jean Buridan called it *impetus*, and worked out that it grows with the arrow's speed and with the quantity of matter in it. (The Paris scholars almost certainly never read Philoponus directly. The idea reached them through Arabic.)

This is good thinking, and the ==impetus== idea is not simply wrong. Buridan was right that a moving thing carries something, and right that the something depends on both speed and amount of stuff; in Chapter 3 that quantity gets its modern name, momentum. The mistake was in what kind of thing it is. Buridan's impetus is a *force* stored inside the arrow, a push that the arrow carries and spends. That is the idea Halloun and Hestenes found in 65 per cent of their students before the course, and in 44 per cent of them after it. It feels right. It is the picture most people have of a thrown ball: the throw is "in" the ball, and the ball slows as the throw runs out.

:::note Impetus
A medieval idea: a kind of force that a mover puts into a moving object, which the object carries along and uses up. A forerunner of momentum, but a force is exactly what a moving object does not carry.
:::

Here is the test that modern physics applies to every force, and it is the most useful habit in this book. **For any force you name, you must be able to name the other object that exerts it.** A force is an interaction between two things. Your hand pushes the book: two things. The Earth pulls the arrow down: two things. So look at the arrow in mid-flight and ask what is touching it or pulling on it. The string has gone. The archer has gone. Only two things are left: the air it flies through, and the Earth beneath it. Those two can exert forces on the arrow. A "force of the throw" has nobody to exert it, so it is not a force at all.

:::predict
While the arrow flies, which way does the air push on it?
- Forward, from behind, carrying it along (Aristotle's answer).
- Backward, against its motion.
- Not at all; the air does nothing.
???
Backward. Newton put it plainly when he explained his first law: a flying projectile keeps moving except in so far as the air's resistance slows it and gravity pulls it down. Ride a bicycle fast and you feel on your face which way air pushes on something moving through it. The air is the arrow's brake, and the planets, which meet almost no air, keep moving for ages.
:::

:::fig c2_arrow_forces() | The forces on an arrow in flight, each one named with the object that exerts it: the Earth pulls it down and the air pushes it backwards. The blue arrow is its velocity, which is not a force. Aristotle's forward push from the air is drawn crossed out, because no object exerts it.

So nothing pushes the arrow forward. The arrow does not need anything to push it forward. What it got from the bowstring is not a stock of force to spend; it is motion, and motion simply continues until a force changes it. The air and the Earth are the only forces acting, and both are busy *changing* the motion: the air slowing it, the Earth bending its path downward into the familiar arc. Take those away (fire the arrow in empty space) and it would fly in a straight line for ever at the speed it left the string. That is a strange claim, and Section 2.3 shows how Galileo convinced himself of it.

:::evidence The students who thought like Buridan | 1985
Halloun and Hestenes gave a diagnostic test to 478 university students in the United States before and after their first physics course, then interviewed some of them. On the pretest, 65 per cent showed the impetus belief: that a moving object carries a force that keeps it going. Forty-seven per cent believed an object slows down when no force acts on it at all. Sorting each student by their overall pattern, the researchers found 65 per cent impetus-thinkers, 18 per cent Aristotelians and 17 per cent Newtonians. After a semester of lectures, 44 per cent still held the impetus view, and in the interviews students "held firm to mistaken beliefs even when confronted with phenomena that contradicted those beliefs". Showing someone a demonstration is not enough. That is why this book asks you to predict first.
:::

:::story impetus

:::careful
> "The ball carries the force of my throw inside it, and when that force is used up it stops."

Apply the two-object test. After the ball leaves your hand, who is exerting "the force of your throw"? Not your hand: it is no longer touching the ball. There is no second object, so there is no such force. What the ball carries is motion, and motion only ever gets *changed*, by the air, by the Earth's pull and, finally, by the floor. A ball thrown in empty space would carry the same motion for ever.
:::

:::check
A crumpled paper ball flies across the classroom. While it is in the air, list every force acting on it, and for each force name the object that exerts it.
???
Two forces. The Earth pulls it downward (gravity), and the air pushes on it, mostly backward against its motion. There is no forward force, because nothing is touching it from behind: your hand stopped acting the moment the ball left it. The ball keeps moving forward because nothing is stopping it, except, slowly, the air.
:::

## 2.3 The ball that never stops

:::scene Padua, about 1604

Galileo Galilei spent years rolling balls down ramps in Padua, because falling is too fast to time and a ramp slows it down to a speed he could measure. You met those ramps in Chapter 1. Later, in the books he wrote as an old man, he described an experiment that he could only do in his head, and it is one of the great arguments in science. Imagine two ramps facing each other, like a letter V, and let a ball roll down one of them. It rolls up the other and climbs to very nearly the height it started from, falling a little short because of friction. Now make the second ramp less steep. The ball must travel further along it to climb back to its starting height, and it does. Make the ramp shallower still and the ball rolls further still, always climbing to the same height. Now lay the second ramp perfectly flat. The ball can never climb back to its starting height, however far it rolls. So, Galileo reasoned, it never stops.

Notice what kind of argument that is. No ramp is perfectly smooth, so no real ball ever rolls for ever; Galileo did not claim to have watched one. He watched the *trend*. Every time he reduced the steepness, the ball went further, and the only thing stopping the pattern from continuing was friction. Reasoning about where a trend points when you remove the thing that spoils it is called a ==thought experiment==, and it works only when every step of the trend has first been checked against real ramps.

:::note Thought experiment
An experiment reasoned through in the head because it cannot be done for real, built on a trend that has been measured as far as it can be. Galileo's flat ramp is the classic one.
:::

:::predict
A steel katori is sliding across the kitchen floor. Imagine that at this instant someone switches friction off completely, like a light, so that the floor and the air no longer rub on it at all. What does the katori do?
- Slows down and stops, a little later than it would have.
- Slows down more gently, but still stops eventually.
- Keeps sliding at exactly the same speed, in a straight line, for ever.
???
It keeps the same speed in a straight line, for ever. Of the 478 students in Halloun and Hestenes' study, 47 per cent chose one of the first two kinds of answer. If you did too, you are in large company, and the reasoning below is aimed at you.
:::

You can walk Galileo's trend yourself, on a floor instead of a ramp. Slide a katori across a rough floor and it stops quickly. Dust the floor with talcum powder and it slides further. Smear a little oil on the floor and it slides further still. Each change does the same thing, which is to reduce the rubbing between the katori and the floor, and each time the stopping takes longer. Follow the trend to its end: take the rubbing away entirely and the stopping takes for ever. An air-hockey table does nearly this. With the fan off, the puck stops in a moment. With the fan on, a cushion of air lifts the puck off the surface, almost all the friction is gone, and the puck glides from one end to the other at very nearly constant speed, as if nothing had happened to it. Nothing did.

:::fig c2_two_ramps() | Galileo's thought experiment. Left: the ball climbs back to nearly its starting height. Middle: a shallower ramp, and the ball travels further to reach the same height. Right: a flat ramp. The ball can never get back to its starting height, so it never stops. The dashed line is the starting height in each case.
:::live ramp

Put numbers on the kitchen version to see how big the effect is. An ice cube is a wonderful low-friction object: skating on ice is possible because steel slides over ice with a friction coefficient of only about 0.02, measured in laboratories and listed in physics textbooks (Section 2.5 explains the coefficient). Suppose an ice cube leaves your fingers at 1 metre per second, which is a gentle flick. On a wet steel thali it would slide about 2.5 metres before stopping, if the thali were that long. On a cotton cloth, whose friction coefficient nobody has published but which is probably around 0.3, the same flick carries it about 17 centimetres. One flick, two surfaces, fifteen times the distance. The two speeds and the cloth's coefficient are guesses you can improve on; the ratio is the lesson.

:::home Ice on a thali, ice on a cloth
Wet a large steel thali and set it flat on the counter. Lay a folded cotton cloth beside it. Take an ice cube from the freezer, let it sit for half a minute so its surface starts to melt, and flick it gently across the cloth. Note where it stops. Now flick it the same way across the wet thali. Keep a hand ready at the far edge. **Predict first:** how many times further will it go on the steel? Then try a third surface (the tiled floor, a plastic tray) and arrange the three in order of friction. What you are watching is Galileo's trend. The less the surface rubs, the longer the cube keeps the motion you gave it. Wipe up the water afterwards: a wet tile is slippery for people too.
:::

Here, then, is the result that Aristotle could not have reached without a frictionless floor, and that Galileo reached by imagining one. A moving thing on which nothing pushes or pulls keeps moving in a straight line at a steady speed. It does not need a force to keep going. It only needs the absence of a force to stop it. Physicists call such a thing an *isolated* body: one that is cut off from every push and pull, as far as that can be arranged. An isolated body keeps a constant velocity, and that velocity may be zero. Rest is simply the special case of a steady speed that happens to be nought.

:::try Investigation 2.3
Use the ramp model with its friction slider. Set the second ramp at 30° and release the ball: note the height it reaches. Lower the ramp to 15°, then 5°, then flat, and write down how far along the ramp it travels each time. Then set friction to zero and repeat. Which setting lets the ball reach exactly its starting height? What happens on the flat ramp when friction is zero, and what happens when it is not?
:::

:::careful
> "The ball never stops" means balls never stop.

Every real ball stops, because every real ball meets friction and air. Galileo's claim is about what *would* happen with those removed: nothing would slow the ball, so nothing would stop it. The claim is tested not by watching a ball roll for ever, which is impossible, but by watching the stopping fade exactly as the friction fades. Talc, oil, air cushion, ice: each step confirms it.
:::

:::check
You push a box across the floor and let go. It slides on for a moment, slowing down. While it slides, which way does the total force on it point: forward, backward, or nowhere?
???
Backward. The only horizontal force left is friction from the floor, which pushes against the sliding. That backward force is what makes the velocity shrink. If the total force pointed nowhere, the velocity would not change, and the box would slide on at the same speed.
:::

## 2.4 Newton's first law

:::scene A local train, Mumbai

Now go back to the braking train, and watch it from two places at once. First stand on the platform. The train is coming in at a steady speed. Everyone inside is moving at that same speed, feet, body and bag together. Then the brakes grip the wheels and the train slows. What slows the passengers? Think about what is touching each one. The floor touches their feet, and the floor is part of the train, so it drags their feet to a slower speed by friction. Nothing is touching their heads, shoulders or hands. Those parts carry on at the speed the train had a moment ago, exactly as Galileo's ball would. So each passenger's feet slow with the train while the rest of them keeps going, and from the platform you watch a whole compartment of people tilt towards the front. The bag on the floor had less grip than a pair of shoes, so it slides further before friction stops it.

:::predict
The train brakes hard. Watching from the platform, what happens to a passenger standing in the aisle?
- A forward push throws her forward.
- Her feet are slowed by the floor, while the rest of her carries on at the speed the train had.
- Nothing happens to her until she hits something.
???
Her feet are slowed and the rest of her carries on. There is no forward push; there is a *backward* force, friction on her shoes, and it acts only on her feet. The top of her keeps the old velocity because nothing has yet acted to change it. That is why braking feels like a shove from behind, and why the dozing man looked round for the shover.
:::

Now step inside the train and feel it. From in here, as the brakes bite, it genuinely feels as if something pushes you forward, and if you shut your eyes you would swear to it. Physicists have a name for that feeling: a *fictitious force*. Inside anything that is speeding up, slowing down or turning, everything seems to be pushed the opposite way. But apply the two-object test. What object is doing the pushing? There is none. The feeling is real; the force is not. It is what the first law looks like from inside a carriage that is changing its velocity while you try not to change yours.

:::fig c2_train_brake() | The braking train from two places. From the platform (left): the floor pushes backward on the passenger's feet and slows them, while her upper body keeps the train's old velocity, so she tilts forward. From inside (right): it feels like a push from behind, but no object exerts that push, so it is drawn dashed.

Isaac Newton wrote the rule down in 1687, in the book usually called the *Principia*, as the first of his three laws of motion. In today's words: *every body keeps its state of rest, or of steady motion in a straight line, unless forces acting on it compel it to change that state.* Galileo had grasped the idea first, with his ramps, and Newton never acknowledged him; but Newton made it the foundation of everything that followed. Notice how exact the law is. A body left alone does not "tend" to keep moving, as if it might wear out eventually. It keeps its velocity *exactly*, for as long as the total force on it is zero. Speed and direction both stay fixed, and the only thing that can change either is a force.

:::note Newton's first law
A body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion keeps the same speed in the same straight line, unless a force acting on it changes that state. Also called the law of inertia.
:::

The tendency that the law describes, to stay at rest or to keep moving in a straight line at a steady speed, is called ==inertia==, and every object has it. Rest and motion are covered alike: a parked bus has inertia just as much as a moving one, which is why it is hard to get moving. How much inertia a thing has is measured by its ==mass==, the quantity of matter in it. Try to shove a basketball and it goes; try to shove a boulder the same way and it barely moves. A kilogram of puffed rice and a kilogram of steel bolts have the same mass and the same inertia, though one fills a bag and the other fits in your palm; what differs is how much space the matter takes up. Mass is the same on Earth, in orbit and on the Moon. It is a count of stuff, not a pull.

:::note Inertia
The tendency of an object to stay at rest, or to keep moving in a straight line at constant speed. It is a property, measured by mass. It is not a force.
:::

:::note Mass
How much matter an object contains, and so how much inertia it has. Measured in kilograms (kg). The same everywhere in the universe, unlike weight (Chapter 3).
:::

One more thing the law does *not* say. It does not say that moving at constant speed is impossible while a force acts. Push a heavy trunk across the floor at a steady walking pace and you are certainly pushing, perhaps with 50 newtons. But friction is pushing back with 50 newtons, and the total force on the trunk is zero. Your push cancels the friction that would otherwise stop it; the moving itself needs nothing from you. Take away both, and the trunk would slide on by itself.

:::home The coin, the card and the glass
Stand an empty glass on the table, lay a stiff card (a playing card or an old visiting card) flat across its rim, and put a coin on the card right above the middle of the glass. Flick the card sharply sideways with a finger, hitting its edge. Done well, the card shoots away and the coin drops straight into the glass. Now do it badly on purpose: pull the card away slowly. The coin rides along with it. The physics is the first law plus a very short time. While the card moves, its friction drags the coin sideways, but a sharp flick is over in a few hundredths of a second. If it lasts 0.05 seconds and the card's friction coefficient on the coin is about 0.3 (a guess), the coin picks up a sideways speed of only about 0.15 metres per second and has slipped about 4 millimetres, far less than the glass is wide, before there is no card under it and the Earth's pull takes over. Pull for half a second instead and the slip grows a hundredfold, to about 37 centimetres: the coin simply goes with the card. Short time, small change in motion. Keep that thought for the chapter's case file.
:::

:::recall 1.5
In Chapter 1 you threw a ball straight up and watched the very top of its flight, where for an instant it is still. Suppose you toss your keys up on the platform while you wait for the next train. At the top, is any force acting on them? And does Newton's first law say they should stay there?
???
The Earth is pulling the keys down the whole time, so a force is acting, and the first law says their velocity *must* be changing. It is: from upward to downward, through zero. Zero speed for an instant does not mean zero acceleration. If no force acted, the law says the keys would hang at the top for ever. They do not, which is proof enough that a force is there.
:::

Take two identical toy cars, and load one with ₹5 coins taped on top. Give each the same gentle push with a ruler laid flat and released from the same bend. Which is harder to get moving? Which rolls further once it is going, and why might that be a trickier question than it looks? (Think about friction on the wheels.)
:::

:::school
Your IGCSE syllabus words the first law as: an object either remains at rest or continues in a straight line at constant speed unless acted on by a resultant force. "Resultant force", "net force" and "unbalanced force" all mean the same thing, which is what is left over after the pushes and pulls cancel. The syllabus also defines mass as a measure of the quantity of matter in an object.
:::

:::careful
> "The book kept sliding because of its inertia."

Try the test from Chapter 0: swap the word for a made-up one. "The book kept sliding because of its movability." The sentence says exactly as much, which is nothing. Inertia is a name for the fact that motion continues until a force changes it. The explanation of why the book kept sliding is that *nothing stopped it yet*: the floor's friction was small, so it took a while to bring the book to rest. Name the forces, or the absence of them. Never let the word do the explaining.
:::

:::story inertia

:::five Inertia
? What is it?
The property of every piece of matter that keeps it at rest if it is at rest, or moving in a straight line at constant speed if it is already moving, unless a net force acts on it.
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *inertia*, idleness or sluggishness, from *iners*, unskilled or inactive. Kepler introduced the word into physics for a body's reluctance to start moving, and Newton widened it to cover keeping moving as well, so the "lazy" name is half misleading: a fast-moving planet has inertia too.
? What problem did it solve?
For two thousand years people followed Aristotle in assuming that every moving thing needs a continuing push to keep going, which left nobody able to explain why an arrow keeps flying after it leaves the bowstring.
? Why does it work?
Because steady motion in a straight line and rest are the same physical state seen from two different steadily moving reference frames; neither needs a cause, and only a *change* of velocity requires a force.
? How does it help?
It explains why passengers lurch toward the front of a braking Mumbai local train (their feet are slowed by the floor while their upper bodies keep the train's old velocity), and how spacecraft cruise across the solar system with their engines off.
?? How do we know?
Galileo's facing ramps, ice cubes sliding across a wet steel thali versus a cloth, air-hockey pucks, and spacecraft in deep space all show that as rubbing and air resistance are removed, stopping fades away toward never.
?? What it isn't
It is not a force stored inside the moving object (that was the medieval *impetus* mistake), and saying "it kept moving because of inertia" is a label, not a cause: it kept moving because nothing stopped it yet.
?? Where does it stop working?
Newton's first law holds in any non-accelerating (inertial) reference frame; inside a braking train or a spinning merry-go-round, objects appear to veer on their own unless you account for the frame's own acceleration.
:::

:::check
A friend says: "Newton's first law means that a moving object keeps a constant acceleration unless a force acts on it." Fix the sentence, and say why the fix matters.
???
Replace "acceleration" with "velocity". A body with no net force on it keeps a constant *velocity*: the same speed in the same direction. Its acceleration is zero. If a body kept a constant non-zero acceleration with no force acting, its velocity would be changing by itself, which is exactly what the law rules out.
:::

## 2.5 Friction is a force

:::scene Mumbai, the kitchen counter

Rest your palm flat on the table and press down. The table presses up into your hand; you can feel it. Now keep pressing and slide your hand forward. A new push appears, dragging backward along your skin. Push down and forward at once and you feel both together. What you are feeling is the table's contact force, and it has two parts: one straight out of the surface, holding your hand up, and one along the surface, resisting the slide. The first part is the subject of Chapter 3. The second is ==friction==, and this chapter has been leaning on it since the first page without ever checking that it deserves the name. It does. Friction is as real a force as a shove, and it obeys rules you can measure in a kitchen.

:::note Friction
The part of the contact force between two touching surfaces that acts along the surfaces and opposes their sliding over each other. It is a real force, with a size you can measure and a direction you can predict.
:::

The rules for sliding friction were first written down by Leonardo da Vinci in his notebooks, between about 1493 and 1515, then forgotten; Amontons found them again on his own and published them in 1699. First, friction grows in proportion to how hard the surfaces are pressed together: double the pressing force and you double the friction. Second, it hardly depends on the area of contact: a brick slides just as reluctantly on its narrow end as on its broad face. Third (Coulomb added this), for ordinary speeds it hardly depends on how fast the surfaces slide. The ratio of friction to pressing force is a number called the coefficient of friction, written μ, and it depends only on the pair of materials. Rubber on dry concrete has a sliding coefficient of about 0.7, wood on wood about 0.3, steel on ice about 0.02. Those numbers are rough by nature; friction is complicated, and physicists admit it is still not completely understood.

:::fig c2_friction_closeup() | Left: the contact force on a sliding block has a normal component out of the surface and a friction component along it. The marked contact is enlarged at right. Microscopic junctions deform and adhere, then break as the surfaces slide. Pressing harder generally increases the real contact area; visible roughness alone does not explain friction. The close-up is schematic.

The second rule puzzled people for centuries, and the answer is under the magnifier in the figure. Surfaces that look smooth are, up close, ranges of tiny hills, and two surfaces touch only where hill meets hill. Pressing harder squashes more hills into contact and raises the friction. Spreading the same weight over a bigger face lowers the pressure, so fewer hills touch per square centimetre, and the two effects cancel. Even surfaces polished until no hills remain are not friction-free, because the molecules of one surface cling to the molecules of the other.

Friction behaves differently before anything slides. Put a book on a level table and nobody pushes it sideways: the friction on it is zero. Push it gently and it does not move, because friction has grown to match your push exactly. Push harder and friction grows to match again, up to a limit. Past that limit the book breaks free and slides, and the friction drops to a slightly smaller, roughly steady value. You can feel the drop with a luggage scale hooked to a heavy bag on the floor: pull gently, watch the reading climb and climb while nothing moves, then feel the bag lurch as the reading falls back. That lurch is also why chalk squeals on a blackboard and why a violin bow makes a string sing: stick, slip, stick, slip, over and over, very fast.

:::fig c2_friction_graph() | How friction answers a growing push. With no push there is no friction. As you push harder, friction matches you exactly and nothing moves (the diagonal). At the limit the object slips, and sliding friction settles to a slightly lower, roughly steady value. The scale is schematic.

:::predict
You walk forward across the kitchen floor. Which way does the floor's friction push on the sole of your shoe?
- Backward, against the way you are walking.
- Forward, the way you are walking.
- Nowhere: there is no friction while you walk normally.
???
Forward. As you step, your foot tries to slide *backward* against the floor, and friction opposes that slide, which means it pushes your foot forward. Friction is what lets you walk at all; on wet ice it is nearly gone, and so is your ability to go anywhere. Friction opposes the *sliding of surfaces across each other*, which is not always the same as opposing the direction you happen to be moving.
:::

Friction makes things warm, as you know from rubbing your hands on a cold morning or touching a bicycle brake after a long downhill. School books say friction "produces heating", and that is right. What it does not do is destroy anything. The motion that friction takes away does not vanish; it spreads out into the warmth of the two surfaces, and Chapter 7 follows every bit of it.

:::school
The IGCSE syllabus describes solid friction as the force between two surfaces that may impede motion and produce heating. Notice the word "may". Friction impedes a sliding book, and it also lets you walk and a car accelerate; it opposes sliding between surfaces, whichever way that works out for the object.
:::

:::careful
> "Friction always acts against the direction I'm moving."

Friction acts against the *sliding of the two surfaces over each other*. When you walk, your sole tends to slide backward along the floor, so friction on it points forward, and that forward friction is what moves you. When a car's wheels spin on wet mud, friction is too small to push the car forward, and the car stays put. The direction of friction is set by the surfaces, not by the direction of travel.
:::

:::five Friction
? What is it?
The force along two touching surfaces that opposes their sliding, or tending to slide, across each other.
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *frictio*, a rubbing, from *fricare*, to rub.
? What problem did it solve?
On Earth, pushed books and rolling balls always slow down and stop, which fooled observers for centuries into thinking motion dies out on its own. Recognising friction as an invisible backward push from the floor rescued the law of inertia.
? Why does it work?
Even surfaces that look polished are microscopic mountain ranges that touch only at their tips; pressing the surfaces together squashes more tips into contact and lets surface molecules tug on one another, resisting any slide.
? How does it help?
It lets you calculate how far a braking car or a flicked coin will slide, explains why a magician must yank a tablecloth fast, and shows why your shoes can push you forward when you walk.
?? How do we know?
Leonardo da Vinci and Guillaume Amontons measured the pull needed to drag blocks across tables and found that friction grows in proportion to the pressing force and barely changes when a brick is turned from its broad face onto its narrow edge.
?? What it isn't
It does not always oppose an object's overall motion (forward friction from the ground is what accelerates a runner or a bicycle), and static friction before sliding is not fixed at its maximum value: it matches whatever sideways push is applied, up to the slip limit.
?? Where does it stop working?
The simple rule that friction equals a constant coefficient times the normal force (*f* = μ*N*) is a working approximation that breaks down when surfaces melt, peel, lubricate, or stick at the atomic scale.
:::

:::check
A book rests on a level table and nobody touches it. How much friction acts on the book?
???
None at all. Friction before sliding takes whatever value is needed to stop the surfaces slipping, up to a limit, and with no sideways push there is nothing to oppose. The old recipe "friction equals the coefficient times the pressing force" gives only the *maximum* friction can reach before the book slips, not the friction acting at any moment.
:::

## The tablecloth trick

:::case The tablecloth trick
A magician at a birthday party lays a cloth on a table, sets out plates, glasses and a vase of flowers, grips the edge of the cloth and yanks. The cloth is gone and the dishes stand where they were, barely rattled. Afterwards, Kym and two friends argue about how it works.

1. One says the plates have to be heavy: "the heavier the plate, the more it stays put".
2. One says the cloth has to be slippery and without a hem.
3. One says the pull has to be fast and straight down over the table's edge, and that a slow pull would drag everything off.

Kym notices that the plates did move, a centimetre or so, in the direction of the pull.

Which of the three is right, and why did the plates move a little but not a lot? Does a heavier plate help?
???
The second and third are right. The first is the popular explanation, and it is wrong.

While the cloth slides under a plate, the only sideways force on the plate is the cloth's friction, which drags the plate the way the cloth is going. The plate starts to move, exactly as the first law says it must when a force acts. How *far* it moves depends on two things: how big that friction is, and how long it acts. A slippery cloth makes the friction small. A fast pull makes the time short.

Put in numbers, with inputs that are guesses. A sliding coefficient of 0.3 between cloth and plate and a pull that takes 0.1 seconds give the plate a speed of about 0.3 metres per second and a slip of about 1.5 centimetres while the cloth is under it. Once the cloth is gone the plate slides on the bare table and stops within about another 1.5 centimetres. Halve the pulling time to 0.05 seconds and the slip drops to about 4 millimetres, a quarter as much. Stretch the pull to half a second and the plate slides about 37 centimetres: off the table. That is Kym's centimetre, explained, and the magician's speed, explained.

Now the heavy plate. Friction grows in proportion to how hard the surfaces press together, so a plate twice as heavy feels twice the friction from the cloth. But it also has twice the mass, so twice the force produces exactly the same change in motion. The acceleration of a plate dragged by a cloth is the coefficient times 9.8 metres per second squared, whatever the plate weighs. Heavy plates do not help. Smooth plates, a smooth hemless cloth, and a short, sharp pull do.

If you try it, use plastic plates and a tea towel, and ask an adult first.
:::
