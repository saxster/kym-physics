Chapter 2 settled what a force does. It does not keep things moving; a book sliding on a frictionless table would slide for ever with nobody pushing it. A force *changes* motion, and when nothing changes a thing's motion, nothing is acting on it. That leaves three questions hanging, and this chapter takes them in turn. How do you add up several forces acting at once? How much does a given force change the motion, and why is a loaded truck so much slower off the mark than a scooter? And who, exactly, is doing the pushing when a table holds up a book?

The last question leads somewhere strange. A mosquito meets the windscreen of a car on the Sea Link at 90 km/h. The mosquito is destroyed; the car does not notice. Ask anyone which one felt the bigger force and they will say the mosquito, obviously. In a test of about 900 university students, 73 per cent said the bigger object pushes harder in a collision. Every one of them was wrong, and by the end of this chapter you will be able to work out by how much.

## 3.1 Adding forces

:::scene A school sports ground, Mumbai

Sports day, and the tug of war is on. Two teams dig their heels into the dry ground, lean back and pull. For a long moment the rope does not move; then one team gives, and the other hauls them over the line. Think about the rope, or better, think about a loaded cart with a rope tied to each side and two teams pulling it in opposite directions, which is the same contest with a clearer middle. Two forces act on the cart at once. What does it do?

You already know the answer from experience. If the pulls are equal, the cart stays put. If one side pulls harder, the cart goes that way, and the harder the difference, the more briskly it goes. A force has a size and a direction, and when several forces act on one object at the same time, forces in the same direction add up and forces in opposite directions cancel. What is left over after the cancelling is called the ==net force==, and it is the net force, never any single push on its own, that decides how the motion changes.

:::note Net force
The total of all the forces acting on an object, after opposing forces have cancelled. Your school syllabus calls it the resultant force. If it is zero, the object's velocity does not change.
:::

Put made-up numbers on the cart. Four children pull it to the left with 120, 100, 90 and 80 newtons: 390 newtons altogether. Three bigger children pull it to the right with 150, 130 and 110 newtons: also 390. Net force: 390 − 390 = 0, and the cart's velocity does not change. If it was at rest it stays at rest, and Chapter 2 reminds you that if it was already rolling it would keep rolling at the same speed. Now a fourth child joins the right-hand side with 100 newtons. Net force: 100 newtons to the right, and the cart begins to move that way. (A newton, written N, is the unit of force; Section 3.2 says exactly how big it is. A 100 gram packet of biscuits resting on your palm pushes down with about 1 N.)

:::predict
You push a loaded trolley along a smooth level corridor at a steady walking pace. While it moves at that steady speed, the forces on it are:
- Unbalanced, with more force forward than backward; that is why it keeps moving.
- Balanced: the net force is zero.
- Unbalanced, with more force backward; the trolley is always fighting friction.
???
Balanced. Steady speed in a straight line means the velocity is not changing, and Chapter 2 says that happens only when the net force is zero. Your forward push is being cancelled by friction and air pushing back. Balanced forces do not mean "not moving". They mean "not changing its motion".
:::

:::fig c3_tug_cart() | A cart pulled both ways. Left: 390 N to the left and 390 N to the right cancel; the net force is zero and the velocity does not change. Right: one more child joins, the pulls no longer cancel, and the net force of 100 N to the right sets the cart moving that way. The numbers are made up; the arrows are drawn to the same scale.

It is worth asking how anyone knows that forces add like this. It is not a definition; it is a measured fact. Apply one force to a cart on a smooth track and measure how its velocity changes. Apply a second force on its own and measure again. Then apply both at once. The change in velocity you get is exactly what you would get by laying the two force-arrows end to end and reading off the single arrow from start to finish. Physicists check this kind of thing before they trust it, and arrows that add end to end are called vectors; velocity is one too, as you saw in Chapter 1.

Two habits make force problems easy, and both come from the tug of war. First, decide *which object* you are talking about, and draw only the forces acting *on* that object, never the forces it exerts on other things. For the cart, that means the pull of each rope, the Earth's pull downward, the ground's push upward, and friction. Second, draw the object as a dot and each force as an arrow from the dot, with its length showing its size. That sketch is called a free-body diagram, and it turns a crowded scene into a sum.

:::try Investigation 3.1
Make your own force meter. Hang a small plastic bag from a rubber band and put one ₹5 coin in it; measure how far the band stretches. Then two coins, then four. Is the stretch for four coins twice the stretch for two? Now loop two identical rubber bands side by side and hang four coins from the pair: predict the stretch before you look. This is how a spring balance turns a stretch into a force reading.
:::

:::school
IGCSE writes "resultant force" where this book says "net force"; some school sheets say "unbalanced force". All three mean the same thing: what is left after the pushes and pulls have cancelled. The syllabus also says a resultant force may change the velocity of an object by changing its direction of motion or its speed.
:::

:::careful
> "If the forces on it are balanced, it must be standing still."

Balanced forces mean the velocity is not changing. A parked car and a car cruising at a steady 60 km/h on a straight road both have zero net force on them. The second one is moving; it is just not speeding up, slowing down or turning. "Balanced" describes the forces, not the speed.
:::

:::check
Two friends push a stalled scooter in the same direction with 200 N and 150 N. Friction and the rough road resist with 350 N. What is the net force on the scooter, and what does it do?
???
200 + 150 − 350 = 0 N. The net force is zero, so the scooter's velocity does not change. If it was at rest, it stays at rest, which is why the friends feel they are pushing hard and getting nowhere. One more push of any size would tip the balance and start it moving.
:::

## 3.2 Why heavy things are hard to start

:::scene A traffic signal, Mumbai

The light goes green. A scooter is away before you have finished looking at it; a loaded truck beside it rolls forward so slowly that you could keep pace on foot for the first few metres. Both engines are working hard. The difference is in what the force has to move. Here is your probable explanation: *heavy things are hard to get going because they are heavy.* It sounds like it cannot be wrong, and it mixes up two different things, which this section separates.

Start with a cleaner experiment than a traffic signal. Put a cart on a smooth track where friction barely matters, and push it with a steady force, the same every second. What does the cart do?

:::predict
You push a cart along a very smooth track with a steady force, never bigger, never smaller. What does the cart do?
- Moves at a steady speed set by how hard you push.
- Speeds up, and keeps speeding up for as long as you push.
- Speeds up at first, then settles at a steady speed.
???
It keeps speeding up. A steady force gives a steady *change* in velocity, every second the same extra speed. Two-thirds of Halloun and Hestenes' 478 students chose the first answer, and so did 44 per cent of more than 2,000 American school students on a national test. If the cart settled at a steady speed, the net force on it would have to be zero, which means something else, friction or air, had grown to cancel your push. On a very smooth track, nothing does.
:::

Newton's second law puts a number on it. Push a cart with a certain net force and it gains speed at a certain rate; double the net force and it gains speed twice as fast. Keep the force the same and double the mass by loading the cart, and it gains speed only half as fast. In symbols, with *a* for the rate of gaining speed (the acceleration from Chapter 1), *F* for the net force and *m* for the mass:

:::eq
**net force = mass × acceleration**, or *F* = *ma*
:::

The ==newton== is defined by this rule: 1 N is the net force that makes a 1 kg mass gain 1 m/s of speed every second. The law is the reason a push on a basketball sends it flying while the same push on a stalled car barely moves it: the force is the same, and the free-body diagrams look identical, but the car has a thousand times the mass and gets a thousandth of the acceleration. It is also the reason the loaded truck crawls away from the signal. Suppose, with made-up round numbers, that a scooter with its rider has a mass of 200 kg and a loaded truck 10,000 kg, and that each engine manages a net forward force of 2,000 N. The scooter accelerates at 2,000 ÷ 200 = 10 m/s², and the truck at 2,000 ÷ 10,000 = 0.2 m/s², fifty times less, for the same force. A real example from a textbook: a 51 N net force on a 240 kg lawn mower gives 51 ÷ 240 = 0.21 m/s².

:::note Newton (N)
The unit of force. One newton is the net force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². On Earth, a 100 g mass weighs about 1 N.
:::

:::fig c3_f_ma() | Newton's second law in three rows. Top: a push on a cart gives it some acceleration. Middle: double the push on the same cart, double the acceleration. Bottom: the original push on a cart of double the mass, half the acceleration. Force arrows and acceleration arrows are each drawn to scale within the figure.

Now the two things that "heavy" mixes up. The truck is hard to get going because of its *mass*, the quantity of matter in it, which is what the *m* in the law measures. That is the inertia from Chapter 2. The truck is also *heavy*, which means the Earth pulls down on it hard. The pull of the Earth on an object is called its ==weight==, and weight is a force, measured in newtons like any other. The two are linked but different. If the Earth's pull is the only force on something, the second law gives its acceleration as weight ÷ mass, and that acceleration, for everything near the Earth's surface, comes out at 9.8 m/s². Turn it round and you get the rule for weight:

:::eq
**weight = mass × 9.8 N/kg**
:::

:::note Weight
The force with which the Earth (or whatever planet you are on) pulls an object down. It equals the mass times the local strength of gravity: on Earth about 9.8 newtons for every kilogram.
:::

A 1 kg bag of sugar has a weight of 9.8 N on Earth. On the Moon, where gravity is weaker, the same bag weighs about 1.7 N, yet its mass is still 1 kg and it is exactly as hard to start moving sideways. Take the truck into orbit, where it floats weightless, and it is still 10,000 kg and still needs 2,000 N to accelerate at 0.2 m/s². Mass is the thing that resists being accelerated. Weight is a particular force that happens to act on it. "Heavy things are hard to start" is true on Earth only because things with lots of mass also have lots of weight.

:::work
A cricket ball of mass 0.16 kg (a round number; real ones are close to this):
weight = 0.16 kg × 9.8 N/kg
weight = 1.57 N, about one and a half newtons
:::

One small puzzle for the bathroom. A weighing scale cannot measure mass directly. What it feels is the force pressing on it, your weight, through springs or sensors inside, and it divides by 9.8 before it shows you a number in kilograms. Stand on it, reach out and press down on the edge of the basin, and the number drops, though your mass has not changed by a gram. Section 3.3 explains why.

:::try Investigation 3.2
Stand on a bathroom scale next to a sturdy table. Read the scale. Predict what the reading will do when you press down on the table with one hand, then do it. Now lift gently on the underside of the table instead (do not pull it over). What happens to the reading, and what does that tell you about which force the scale is measuring?
:::

:::school
Your syllabus says weight is a gravitational force on an object that has mass, and gives *g* = 9.8 two ways: as 9.8 N/kg (the pull on each kilogram) and as the acceleration of free fall, 9.8 m/s². They are the same number seen from two sides. When someone says they "weigh 40 kg" they mean their mass; in a physics answer, write mass in kg and weight in N.
:::

:::careful
> "A steady push gives a steady speed. My bicycle goes at a steady speed when I pedal steadily."

Your bicycle does, and the reason is the forces you cannot see. Pedalling steadily on level ground, you are pushed forward by the road and pushed back by air and rolling friction, and the two cancel: net force zero, steady speed. Stop pedalling and the backward forces win, so you slow. Pedal harder and you win, so you speed up. A steady *net* force always gives a steady change of speed. The bicycle feels like a counter-example only because the net force on it is zero.
:::

:::check
A trolley and its load have a mass of 60 kg. You push with 80 N, and friction pushes back with 50 N. What is the trolley's acceleration?
???
Net force = 80 − 50 = 30 N. Acceleration = 30 N ÷ 60 kg = 0.5 m/s². Only the net force counts; using the 80 N on its own would give the wrong answer.
:::

## 3.3 Can a table push?

:::scene Kym's desk, Mumbai

A book lies on the desk. The Earth pulls it down with its weight, about 10 newtons for a big textbook. It does not move. By everything in this chapter so far, the net force on it must be zero, so something must be pushing it *up* with 10 newtons, and the only thing touching it is the desk. Ask people whether a desk can push and about half say no. "The desk is just in the way. It stops the book falling, but it doesn't *push*; pushing is something living things do." A researcher named Minstrell, who studied high-school students' ideas about forces, found that only around half of them would say a table pushes up on a book. The other half are not foolish. A desk looks utterly passive. Where would a push come from?

:::predict
A book lies still on the desk. Which is true?
- The desk pushes up on the book, exactly as hard as the Earth pulls it down.
- The desk does not push; it just blocks the book from falling.
- The desk pushes up, but less than the book's weight, which is why the book presses into it.
???
The first. Zero net force needs an upward push equal to the weight, and the only candidate is the desk. The rest of this section shows where the push comes from, because "it must" is a poor reason to believe anything.
:::

Here is a chain of four steps, each one a small move from the last, and it was designed by a researcher named Clement to walk people from what they already believe to what is true.

:::home The spring, the foam, the ruler and the table
**Step 1.** Press your palm down on a spring, or a rubber ball, or a folded sponge. It squashes, and you can feel it pushing back; the harder you press, the harder it pushes. Nobody doubts that a spring pushes. **Step 2.** Lay a book on a thick sponge or a folded towel. The sponge dents under it and stays dented. The dent is the sponge's way of pushing up with exactly the book's weight: it squashed until its push matched the load, and there it stopped. **Step 3.** Lay a 30 cm steel ruler across the gap between two stacks of books, and put a ₹5 coin in the middle. Add more coins, one at a time, with your eye level with the ruler. It sags, visibly, a little more with every coin, and for every coin it holds. The ruler is doing exactly what the sponge did: bending until its push upward matches the weight on it. **Step 4.** Now lay the book on the table. The table is a ruler that is very, very stiff. It sags under the book by an amount far too small to see, and that tiny sag is its push. Make the load bigger (sit on the table) and it sags a little more and pushes a little harder, just as the ruler did. Keep the ruler on the desk for a day: it is the table's argument in a form you can watch.
:::

:::fig c3_bridging_chain() | Four steps from a spring to a table. The spring and the sponge visibly squash and push back. The steel ruler bridging two book stacks sags under coins, more with each coin. The table sags too, by far too little to see; the sag in the drawing is exaggerated. In every case the upward push grows until it matches the weight.

What is bending, down at the bottom? The atoms of the table top. Press on them and they are squeezed a tiny bit closer together than they like to sit, and the electrons around each atom push back against the electrons of its neighbours. That push is the same thing that stops your hand passing through the desk. Squeeze harder, and the atoms are squeezed closer and push harder. A table is a spring made of atoms, so stiff that its squash is invisible, and no more passive than a spring.

The push that any surface gives straight out of itself, at right angles to the surface, is called the ==normal force==. "Normal" here is the old mathematical word for *at right angles*; it does not mean ordinary. The normal force is a strange kind of force, because no formula tells you its size in advance. It is whatever it needs to be to stop the object sinking in: 10 N for the book, more if you press on the book, less on a slope. A skier of mass 60 kg standing on a 25° slope presses on the snow with only part of her 588 N weight, and the snow pushes back with about 533 N at right angles to the slope. A book lying on a level desk with nothing else touching it gets a normal force equal to its weight, and that is the only case where the two are equal; the moment anything else pushes or pulls on the book, or the desk accelerates, the normal force changes to match.

:::note Normal force
The push of a surface on anything resting on it, directed straight out of the surface (at right angles to it). It takes whatever size is needed to stop the object passing into the surface. It equals the weight only for an object at rest on a level surface with nothing else pushing on it.
:::

This is also what your hand feels when you hold a cricket ball. You do not feel the Earth's pull on the ball; you feel the ball pressing on your palm, and your palm pressing back with 1.57 N, which is the normal force from your hand. Lift the ball a little with your other hand and the first palm feels less, because it is now providing less of the support. Toss the ball up and, while it is in the air, nothing supports it at all: the normal force is gone, and the ball is weightless in exactly the sense that an astronaut is. Gravity has not switched off for either of them. Chapter 5 comes back to that.

:::school
At school you may see the table's push called the normal force, the normal contact force or the normal reaction. "Normal" means at right angles to the surface. Whatever the name, it is the surface pushing back because it has been squashed, never a force the object carries with it.
:::

:::careful
> "The table doesn't push. It just gets in the way."

Getting in the way *is* pushing. The table bends under the book, by far too little to see, and the bent table pushes up like a bent ruler. If it did not, the book would keep moving downward through the wood, because a force (the Earth's pull) would be acting with nothing to balance it. Put a ruler across two book stacks and load it with coins, and you can watch the push being made.
:::

:::check
When you jump off a low wall, for the moment you are in the air you feel weightless. Has gravity switched off? What has changed?
???
Gravity has not switched off; it is what brings you down. What has gone is the normal force. On the ground, the floor pushed up on your feet and your body felt that push as "weight". In the air, nothing supports you, so there is no push to feel, and you are falling freely. Astronauts in orbit feel the same, for the same reason (Chapter 5).
:::

## 3.4 Every push has a partner

:::scene London, 1687

In the *Principia*, after the first two laws, Newton wrote a third, and it is the one that people find hardest to believe. Press a stone with your finger, he said, and the stone presses your finger. Whenever one object exerts a force on a second, the second exerts a force of the same size, in the opposite direction, on the first. Forces never come singly. Every push has a partner, and the two partners act on different objects.

Your finger on the stone is easy to accept, because you can feel the stone pressing back. The law bites when the two objects are very different. A marble hits a moving train. Which feels the bigger force at the instant they meet, the marble or the train? Almost everyone says the marble, because the marble is the one that gets smashed. Newton says the forces are exactly equal. The marble is smashed because it is small and fragile and the same force gives it an enormous acceleration, while the same force on a train of vastly greater mass changes its motion by an amount nobody could measure. Equal forces; wildly unequal effects. That is the rule you need for the mosquito.

:::predict
Kym and her father hook two luggage scales together and pull against each other. Dad is stronger and pulls harder. Which scale shows the bigger reading?
- Dad's scale.
- Kym's scale.
- Both read the same.
???
Both read the same, whoever "pulls harder". The pull of each scale on the other is one pair of forces, equal and opposite. Of about 900 university students asked the truck-and-car version of this question before their physics course, 73 per cent said the bigger object pushes harder. After the course, 39 per cent still did. Do the experiment.
:::

:::home Two luggage scales
Borrow the two hanging luggage scales (the kind for weighing suitcases at the airport) that most homes have at least one of; a neighbour will have the other. Hook them together and pull against a partner, gently at first, then as hard as you both can. Read both dials at the same moment. They agree, to about 0.1 kg. Now hook one scale to a door handle and pull the other as hard as you like: it still reads the same as the one on the handle, and the handle is not pulling at all in any way you would recognise. Finally, pull "harder than your partner" on purpose. You cannot; the readings move together. The dial says kg, which is a weight reading divided by 9.8: a reading of 10 kg means the hook is being pulled with about 98 N.
:::

:::fig c3_luggage_scales() | Two luggage scales hooked together and pulled in opposite directions. Both dials show the same reading, however hard either person pulls, because the pull of each on the other is a single pair of equal and opposite forces. A reading of 10 kg on the dial is a pull of about 98 N.

Why can the scales never disagree? Follow the forces along a rope in a tug of war. Each short piece of rope pulls on the next piece, and the next piece pulls back on it with the same force; every link in the chain is a pair. The size of that pull is the rope's tension, and it is the same all along a light rope, which is why both teams feel the same pull however the contest is going. The luggage scales are two links in such a chain, and each reads the tension in the chain at its own position. There is no way to make your link pull harder than the link next to it.

Now the subtle part, which textbooks still get wrong. Look at yourself standing on the floor. Two forces act on you: the Earth pulls you down (your weight, about 392 N if your mass is 40 kg), and the floor pushes you up with the normal force, also 392 N. They are equal and opposite, and they cancel, which is why you stay put. They are *not* a Newton pair. Both act on the same object, you, and a pair always acts on two different objects. The partner of the Earth's pull on you is *your* pull on the Earth: you attract the whole planet upward with 392 N, and it responds with an acceleration of about 7 × 10⁻²³ m/s², which is why nobody has noticed. The partner of the floor's push on you is your push on the floor. Four forces, two pairs, and the two forces that happen to cancel belong to different pairs.

:::fig c3_pairs_person() | The four forces when you stand still, sorted into pairs. Pair 1: the Earth pulls you down, and you pull the Earth up. Pair 2: the floor pushes you up, and you push the floor down. The two forces acting *on you* (one from each pair) cancel, which is why you stay still. A pair never cancels, because its two forces act on different objects.

Once you see pairs everywhere, moving about makes a new kind of sense. You walk by pushing the floor backward with your foot, and the floor pushes you forward: that forward friction from Chapter 2 is the partner of your backward push. A swimmer shoves the wall of the pool and the wall shoves her off. A car's tyres push the road backward and the road pushes the car forward. A helicopter pushes air down; the air pushes the helicopter up. An octopus squirts water backward and the water pushes the octopus forward. In every case the thing that moves off is the one that is free to move, and the force that moves it is the partner of the push it gave.

:::story third-law

:::school
This is Newton's third law. School books often say "every action has an equal and opposite reaction". Two words make that safe: the two forces act on *different objects*, and they act *at the same instant*. There is no first push followed by a reply. "Reaction" is a name, not a delay.
:::

:::careful
> "The truck pushes the car harder than the car pushes the truck. It's bigger and it's going faster."

At the moment they touch, the force of the truck on the car and the force of the car on the truck are equal and opposite, always. What differs is what the equal forces *do*: the car, with less mass, gets the much bigger acceleration and the much bigger damage. "Going faster" cannot matter either, because speed depends on who is watching: from the truck driver's seat, it is the car that is rushing at the truck.
:::

:::check
If every pull your tug-of-war team gives the rope is matched by an equal pull from the rope on your team, how can your team ever win?
???
Because the pair acts on different objects: the rope's pull on you is matched by your pull on the rope, and neither of those is the whole story of what happens to *you*. To see which way your team moves, add up the forces *on your team*: the rope's pull one way and the ground's friction on your feet the other. The team with the better grip gets more friction from the ground than the rope can match, and goes backward, dragging the rope and the other team with it. A tug of war is won through the shoes.
:::

## 3.5 Momentum and rockets

:::scene Thumba, Kerala, 21 November 1963

Before any formula, feel it. A cricket ball comes at you at 20 km/h and you catch it: easy. The same ball at 100 km/h: your hands sting, and the keeper wears gloves for a reason. Now a tennis ball at 100 km/h: the same speed, far less sting. There is a quantity here that is bigger for a faster ball *and* bigger for a heavier one, and the simplest way to think of it is *how hard the thing is to stop*. Newton called it the quantity of motion. Buridan, back in Chapter 2, had almost found it: his impetus grew with speed and with the amount of stuff, and so does this. We call it ==momentum==, and it is simply mass times velocity. A 0.16 kg ball at 30 m/s carries 0.16 × 30 = 4.8 kilogram-metres per second of it. Momentum has a direction, the direction of the velocity, and that matters in a moment.

:::note Momentum
How much motion an object has: its mass multiplied by its velocity, in kg·m/s. It points the way the object is moving. Hard to stop means a lot of momentum.
:::

What changes momentum? A force, acting for a time. Catch the fast ball with stiff arms and your hands stop it almost instantly, with a large force; let your hands ride back with the ball and the same momentum is removed over a longer time, with a smaller force, which is the whole of the keeper's art. The product of force and time is called ==impulse==, and it equals the change in momentum. You met it without the name at the end of Chapter 2: the tablecloth's friction acted on the plate for so short a time that it gave the plate almost no momentum. Bending your knees when you land from a jump is the same trick in the other direction.

:::note Impulse
A force multiplied by the time it acts. It equals the change in momentum it produces. The same change can come from a big force for a short time or a small force for a long time.
:::

Now put the third law next to impulse and something remarkable drops out. When two things push on each other, the forces are equal and opposite, and they act for the same time (they start and stop touching together). So the two impulses are equal and opposite, and the two changes in momentum cancel exactly. Whatever momentum one object gains, the other loses. Add up the momentum of both objects before they touch and after, and the total is the same. This is the conservation of momentum, and it holds for any set of objects on which nothing from outside is pushing: in a collision, however violent, the total momentum of the colliding things does not change. A textbook example: a 0.350 kg cart moving at 2 m/s meets a 0.500 kg cart coming the other way at 0.5 m/s. Before, the total is 0.350 × 2 − 0.500 × 0.5 = 0.45 kg·m/s. After the collision the first cart is measured bouncing back at 0.94 m/s, and the second must therefore be moving forward at 1.56 m/s, so that the total is still 0.45. Measure it, and it is.

:::fig c3_momentum_carts() | The two carts before and after they collide. Arrows show velocity, drawn to scale. Before: 0.350 kg at 2 m/s to the right and 0.500 kg at 0.5 m/s to the left, total momentum 0.45 kg·m/s to the right. After: the light cart bounces back at 0.94 m/s and the heavy one moves off at 1.56 m/s. The total is still 0.45 kg·m/s.

:::recall 2.2
A spacecraft far from any planet switches off its engines. It keeps going at the same speed. A friend says: "The push from the engines is stored inside it, and it will slow down when that push runs out." What is wrong with that, and what does the spacecraft actually carry?
???
Apply Chapter 2's test: who exerts "the push from the engines" after they are off? Nobody. There is no stored force, and nothing to run out. What the spacecraft carries is momentum, mass times velocity, and momentum changes only when a force acts. With no force, it keeps the same velocity for ever, just as the arrow would in empty space.
:::

Momentum is where the rocket comes from. The question people have always asked is: in empty space, with nothing to push against, how does a rocket move at all? The answer is that it carries the thing it pushes against. The engine throws hot gas backward out of the nozzle at enormous speed; by the third law, the gas pushes the rocket forward with an equal force, and that forward push is the thrust. By momentum, the gas going backward and the rocket going forward keep the total exactly what it was. A rocket does not push on the air behind it, and it does not push on the launch pad. It works *better* in a vacuum, where no air gets in the way of the exhaust. A balloon is the same engine in rubber: the stretched balloon squeezes its air out of the neck, and the air pushes the balloon the other way.

:::home The balloon rocket
Thread a drinking straw onto a long piece of string and tie the string tight between two chairs, or a door handle and a chair, a few metres apart. Blow up a balloon, hold the neck pinched, and tape the balloon to the straw with the neck pointing backward along the string. Let go. The balloon runs the length of the string with its air jetting the other way. Try a bigger balloon, and a balloon with the neck pinched half shut so the air leaves more slowly: which goes further, and which goes faster? The balloon is not pushed by its air hitting the room. It is pushed by the air it is throwing out, at the moment of throwing.

:::careful
> "The rocket pushes against the air behind it, so it can't work in space."

Rockets work better in space. The thing a rocket pushes against is its own exhaust, which it carries with it as fuel. Push gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward: a Newton pair, with the air playing no part. In the atmosphere the air only gets in the way of the exhaust and adds drag.
:::

:::check
An astronaut is floating in the middle of a space-station module, out of reach of every wall, holding a spanner. She wants to drift to the window. What should she do?
???
Throw the spanner directly away from the window. Her push on the spanner is matched by the spanner's push on her, and the total momentum of astronaut plus spanner stays zero: the spanner goes one way with some momentum, and she goes the other way with the same amount. Because her mass is much bigger, she drifts slowly, but she drifts. There is nothing else to push on, so this is the only way.
:::

## The mosquito and the windscreen

:::case The mosquito and the windscreen
A car on the Bandra–Worli Sea Link is doing 90 km/h, which is 25 metres every second. A mosquito drifting the other way meets the windscreen. In about a thousandth of a second, the mosquito goes from drifting to travelling with the car. The driver notices nothing.

1. A mosquito's mass is about 10⁻⁵ kg, a hundredth of a gram.
2. The car's mass is about 1,200 kg.
3. Newton's third law says the force of the windscreen on the mosquito and the force of the mosquito on the windscreen are equal and opposite.

Which feels the bigger force? And if the forces are equal, why is only the mosquito destroyed?
???
They feel exactly the same force. The third law allows no exception for size or speed.

Estimate it. The mosquito's momentum changes by about 10⁻⁵ kg × 25 m/s = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m/s, in about 0.001 s. Force × time = change in momentum, so the force is about 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.001 = 0.25 N: a quarter of a newton, about the weight of a 25 g biscuit, on each of them.

Now the second law, separately for each. The mosquito gets an acceleration of 0.25 N ÷ 10⁻⁵ kg = 25,000 m/s², about two and a half thousand times the acceleration of a falling stone. The car gets 0.25 N ÷ 1,200 kg, about 0.0002 m/s², a change of speed too small for any instrument in the car to register. The ratio of the two accelerations is the ratio of the two masses, about a hundred million to one. The mosquito is destroyed not because it felt a bigger force but because its body is tiny and fragile and the same force wrenched it to the car's speed in a millisecond.

Notice, too, that "the car was going faster" explains nothing. From the driver's seat the car is still and the mosquito is the one travelling at 90 km/h. Speed belongs to a point of view; the pair of forces does not.

(The mosquito's mass is an order-of-magnitude figure from a physics textbook; the car's mass and the thousandth of a second are our guesses. Change them and the conclusion does not move.)
