Chapter 3 ended with a mosquito on a windscreen and a rule that surprises almost everyone: the two of them push on each other equally hard. Forces come in pairs, and a force changes motion rather than keeping it going. This chapter takes those two rules to the most familiar force there is, and the one most people have wrong since they were five years old: the pull of the Earth on everything near it.

Here is the puzzle to carry with you. Hold a cricket ball in one hand and a ₹1 coin in the other, at the same height, and let go of both at the same instant. Which hits the floor first? Nearly everyone says the ball, because it is heavier. The chapter will give you the answer, and then something better than the answer: the reason, which turns out to be the same reason the Moon stays up and a spacecraft can coast to it with its engine off.

## 4.1 Heavier falls faster?

:::scene Delft, 1586

Picture a church tower in a Dutch town, and two men at the top with lead balls in their hands. Simon Stevin was an engineer and mathematician who thought the way to settle an argument was to try it. His friend Jan Cornets de Groot had climbed the tower of the Nieuwe Kerk with him. One ball weighed ten times as much as the other. About 30 feet below them, roughly 9 m, lay a wooden board. They let go of both balls at the same moment, and listened.

The argument they were settling was nearly two thousand years old. Aristotle had taught that heavier things fall faster, and faster in proportion to their weight. If that were right, the heavy ball should hit the board when the light one was still only a tenth of the way down, about 8 m above it, and the listeners would hear two thuds well apart. A few people had doubted this. In Alexandria in the sixth century, John Philoponus wrote that when you drop two very different weights, the difference in their fall times is tiny. But almost nobody had done the experiment carefully and written it down.

They heard one thud.

:::fig c4_two_predictions() | What Aristotle's rule predicts for two lead balls, one ten times heavier, dropped about 9 m, beside what Stevin and de Groot heard at Delft in 1586. On Aristotle's rule the light ball should still be a tenth of the way down when the heavy one lands. It landed with it.

Stevin printed the result in 1586, three years before Galileo reached Pisa. You may have heard that Galileo dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa. The only account of that comes from Vincenzo Viviani, his assistant late in life. Galileo himself never describes doing it, and most historians think it was a thought experiment, or at most a private test. So the famous drop is probably a legend, and the real drop happened in Delft. Galileo's reasoning about falling, though, was real and brilliant, and we will meet it again in a moment.

Before the explanation, take your own idea seriously, because it is Aristotle's idea and it is held by very clever people. In one well-known survey, 82% of university students starting a physics course believed that an object's own properties change how fast it falls, even with no air at all. The idea is not foolish. A feather does drift down slowly; a sheet of paper does lag behind a stone. What the idea gets wrong is which thing is doing that. So separate the two questions: what does the air do, and what does the Earth do? This section is about the Earth. The next one is about the air.

:::predict
You drop a cricket ball and a ₹1 coin from the same height at the same moment. Which lands first?
- The cricket ball, because it is heavier
- The coin, because it is smaller and slips through the air
- They land together, or so nearly that you cannot tell
???
They land together, or so nearly that you cannot tell. Both are small and heavy for their size, so from a table's height the air hardly touches either of them. Try it over a steel thali: you hear one clink. The feeling that the ball should win comes from watching light, wide things like paper and feathers, which are a different story.
:::

With the air out of the way, everything near the ground speeds up at the same rate as it falls: about 9.8 metres per second, every second. Let go of a stone. After one second it is moving at 9.8 m/s and has fallen 4.9 m. After two seconds it is moving at 19.6 m/s and has fallen 19.6 m. After three, 29.4 m/s and 44 m. Those numbers do not care what the stone is made of or how heavy it is. Falling with nothing but Earth's pull acting is called ==free fall==, and the rate of speeding up, 9.8 m/s², is the ==acceleration of free fall==, written *g*. It is not quite the same everywhere. Around the world it runs from about 9.78 to 9.83 m/s², depending on where you stand, so we round it to 9.8.

:::note Free fall
Motion with nothing acting but the pull of the Earth: no air resistance, no hand, no string. A ball thrown upward is in free fall too, on the way up, at the top, and on the way down.
:::

:::note Acceleration of free fall (*g*)
How fast a freely falling object speeds up: about 9.8 m/s² near the ground, which means its speed grows by 9.8 m/s every second. It points towards Earth's centre, and that direction is what we call "down".
:::

Why should a heavy ball and a light one speed up at the same rate? This needs Chapter 3, and it is the simplest true explanation there is. Earth pulls a heavier object harder: its weight is bigger. But Chapter 3 also said that a heavier object is harder to get moving. The same push gives a truck far less acceleration than a scooter. Double the mass of a falling ball and you double the pull on it, and you also double its stubbornness. The two doublings cancel exactly, and the acceleration comes out the same. The cricket ball is pulled many times harder than the coin, and it is exactly that many times harder to speed up. Stevin's two balls told the same story in lead.

:::eq
*m* *a* = *m* *g*  →  *a* = *g* (mass cancels out of free fall)
:::

:::five Equivalence of mass
? What is it?
The exact equality between an object's inertial mass (its stubbornness against any accelerating force in *F* = *ma*) and its gravitational mass (how strongly gravity pulls on it in *W* = *mg*).
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *aequivalere*, to have equal value or power. Physicists distinguish the two roles mass plays—resisting acceleration versus feeling gravity—and call their identity the *equivalence principle*.
? What problem did it solve?
There is no obvious reason why the mass that makes a loaded truck sluggish on a level road should be the exact same number that sets how hard the Earth pulls it downward; in electricity, by contrast, a particle's charge and its mass do not have a fixed ratio.
? Why does it work?
Because gravity couples to the very same property of matter that gives it inertia, so in *m*_inertial × *a* = *m*_gravitational × *g*, the two *m*'s cancel identically for lead, wood, water and feathers alike.
? How does it help?
It guarantees that every object in free fall accelerates at the exact same rate *g* at a given place, and it gave Albert Einstein the starting clue for general relativity: inside a freely falling lift, gravity disappears for everything at once.
?? How do we know?
Newton tested pendulums filled with gold, silver, lead, glass, sand, salt, wood, water and wheat; Loránd Eötvös tested materials with a torsion balance to one part in 10⁸; and the MICROSCOPE satellite tested titanium and platinum in orbit in 2022 to one part in 10¹⁵, finding zero difference.
?? What it isn't
It does not mean all objects feel the same gravitational *force*: a 10 kg lead ball feels ten times the downward pull of a 1 kg ball, and needs ten times the force to achieve the same acceleration.
?? Where does it stop working?
No experiment has ever found a breakdown of the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass, down to a quadrillionth of a part.
:::

:::evidence Timing a fall | a laboratory measurement
To measure *g*, let a dense steel ball fall exactly 1.0000 m and time it with an electronic timer: it takes 0.4517 s. In free fall the distance fallen is half of *g* times the time squared, so *g* is twice the distance divided by the time squared: 2 × 1.0000 ÷ 0.4517² = 9.80 m/s². Repeat it with a heavier ball and the time does not change. The ball has to be dense so that the air does not matter, and the timer has to be electronic, because a thumb on a stopwatch is late by your reaction time, which can be a large part of such a short fall. You can measure that reaction time yourself: a friend holds a 30 cm ruler hanging between your open finger and thumb and lets go without warning. If you pinch it after 20 cm have passed, it fell for 0.20 s; 10 cm means 0.14 s, and the full 30 cm means 0.25 s.
:::

:::school
At school you will see *g* written two ways: 9.8 m/s², the acceleration of free fall, and 9.8 N/kg, the gravitational field strength, the pull on each kilogram. They are the same number seen from two sides. IGCSE calls the pull on an object its weight, "a gravitational force on an object that has mass", measured in newtons.
:::

:::careful
> "Heavier things fall faster."

With the air out of the way, they do not: everything speeds up at 9.8 m/s per second, whatever it weighs, because a heavier thing is pulled harder and is harder to get moving by exactly the same factor. In air a light, wide object can lag, but that is the air's doing, not the weight's. A coin and a cricket ball from a table's height land together, and you can hear it.
:::

:::five Free fall
? What is it?
Motion under the pull of gravity alone, with no air resistance, hand, floor or string pushing on the object.
? Why is it called this?
*Free* here means free of every other force besides gravity. The word *fall* misleads people into thinking it applies only on the way down: a ball thrown straight up is in free fall on the way up, at the very top, and on the way down, from the instant it leaves your hand.
? What problem did it solve?
Everyday falls in air mix two things together, Earth's downward pull and the air's upward drag, so a feather lags behind a stone and people concluded for two millennia that heavier things naturally fall faster.
? Why does it work?
Earth pulls a body of ten times the mass with ten times the gravitational force, and by Newton's second law (*a* = *F*/*m*) ten times the mass also takes ten times the force to achieve the same acceleration; the two factors of ten cancel out completely.
? How does it help?
It tells you that every dense object near Earth's surface changes its vertical velocity by 9.8 m/s every second downward, letting you time the depth of a well or predict the arc of any thrown ball.
?? How do we know?
Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot dropped two lead balls of 10-to-1 weight 30 feet onto a board at Delft in 1586 and heard a single thud, and David Scott dropped a 1.32 kg geological hammer and a falcon feather in the Moon's vacuum on 2 August 1971 and watched them hit the lunar soil side by side.
?? What it isn't
It is not motion at terminal speed: once air drag grows to balance a raindrop's weight, the drop is no longer in free fall and falls at a steady speed instead of accelerating.
?? Where does it stop working?
Near Earth's surface *g* ≈ 9.8 m/s² stays nearly constant over everyday heights, and weakens noticeably only when you climb hundreds of kilometres toward orbit.
:::

:::check
A friend drops a glass marble and a cricket ball together from a first-floor balcony about 4 m up, and says the ball will land first because it is heavier. What do you say, and how long does the fall take?
???
They land together, or so close that you cannot tell. Both are dense, so air barely matters over 4 m, and with air out of the way everything accelerates at 9.8 m/s². The time comes from 4 = ½ × 9.8 × *t*², so *t* = √(8 ÷ 9.8) ≈ 0.9 s.
:::

## 4.2 Air gets in the way

:::scene Mumbai, a monsoon afternoon

Stand under a balcony in July and watch the rain. Those drops have fallen from clouds that can be a few kilometres up. If a drop kept speeding up all the way down at 9.8 m/s every second, from 4000 m it would arrive at about 280 m/s, over 1000 km/h. The monsoon would be a hail of bullets. A big drop does sting a little, but it arrives at the speed of a bicycle, not a rifle shot. Something stops the speeding up. That something is the air, and this section is about what it does and when it matters.

Start with a feeling. Fill a bucket with water, hold your hand flat and sweep it through, then turn your hand edge-on and sweep again. Flat, the water fights you; edge-on, it hardly does. Your hand has to shove the water out of its way, and the faster you move, the more water you must shove each second and the harder you must shove it. Air does the same thing to anything moving through it, more gently, because air is far thinner than water. The force is called ==drag==. Drag always points against the motion, and it grows with speed: for most everyday objects, roughly with the speed squared, so doubling the speed makes the drag about four times as big. It also grows with the area facing the motion, which is why your edge-on hand slipped through.

:::note Drag (air resistance)
The force a fluid, such as air or water, puts on an object moving through it, pointing against the motion. It grows with speed and with the area facing the motion. Unlike the weight, it is zero when the object is still.
:::

:::predict
Take two identical sheets of paper. Crumple one into a tight ball and leave the other flat. Hold them at the same height and let go together. Which lands first?
- The flat sheet, because it is spread out
- The crumpled ball, by a lot
- Together: they weigh the same, so the Earth pulls them the same
???
The crumpled ball, by a lot. The Earth does pull them the same: same paper, same weight. The difference is the air. The flat sheet pushes a wide area of air out of its way and the drag soon matches its tiny weight; the ball slips through. Same weight, different fall. So weight was never the whole story.
:::

Now follow a raindrop from the moment it forms. At first it is barely moving, so there is almost no drag, and the only force is its weight: it speeds up at the full 9.8 m/s². As it gets faster, the drag grows. The weight does not change, so the net force downward shrinks, and the drop speeds up more and more slowly. Eventually the drag grows until it exactly matches the weight. Now the two forces cancel. Remember Chapter 2: balanced forces do not mean the drop stops; they mean its velocity stops *changing*. From here down it falls at a steady speed, its ==terminal speed==.

:::note Terminal speed
The steady speed a falling object reaches when the drag on it has grown to equal its weight, so the net force is zero. Both forces are still there; they cancel.
:::

:::fig c4_drag_balance() | A 2 mm raindrop at three moments: just released, drag is zero and it speeds up at the full rate; speeding up, drag has grown but is still smaller than the weight; at its steady speed the two arrows match. The graph shows its speed climbing and flattening at about 6.5 m/s, computed from the drag model in the MIT course notes.
:::live airfall

:::eq
**air drag**: *F*_drag = ½ *ρ* *C*_d *A* *v*²;  at terminal speed *v*_t:  ½ *ρ* *C*_d *A* *v*_t² = *mg*
:::

:::work
Suppose a raindrop doubles its radius (2× wider in every direction):
Volume and mass grow as 2³ = 8× → weight *mg* is 8 times larger
Cross-sectional area *A* grows as 2² = 4×
At terminal speed, *v*_t² ∝ weight ÷ area = 8 ÷ 4 = 2 → *v*_t increases by √2 ≈ 1.41 (about 41% faster)
:::

For a drop about 2 mm across, that steady speed is about 6.5 m/s, 23 km/h, which is bicycle speed. Bigger drops fall faster, because a bigger drop has more weight for each bit of area the air can push on. But there is a ceiling. Above about 4.5 mm a drop flattens into a parachute shape and breaks up, so the largest drops reach only about 10 m/s, 36 km/h. Even those take at least 400 s, nearly seven minutes, to fall from 4000 m. And while we are on raindrops: they are not teardrop-shaped. Small ones are nearly round, and drops of 2 to 3 mm are flattened underneath, like a bun.

:::evidence The speed of a raindrop | course notes and NASA
Physics courses work out a raindrop's terminal speed by setting drag equal to weight. The MIT course notes take a drop of mass 4 milligrams with a cross-section of 3 square millimetres, which is a drop about 2 mm across, and get 6.5 m/s. NASA, which measures rain from satellites, reports that a drop's speed in still air rises with its size up to a maximum of about 10 m/s for the largest drops. The two agree where they overlap. The same sum gives about 20 m/s for a small hailstone, and for people it gives the speeds skydivers report: roughly 350 km/h falling head-down and about 200 km/h spread-eagled, because spreading out puts more area in the way of the air.
:::

The same rule explains a lot. Two sheets of paper crumpled together into one ball weigh twice as much as one crumpled sheet but push on about the same air, so the double ball reaches a higher steady speed and lands first. A small animal has little weight for the area it presents to the air, so its terminal speed is low, and it can survive a fall that would kill a large one. A feather has almost no weight for its area, so its terminal speed is tiny, and it drifts.

:::home Four sheets of paper
Take four identical sheets of paper, A4 from the printer is ideal, and clear a space on the floor. **Predict first, every time.** First: one sheet flat against one sheet crumpled into a tight ball; drop them together from shoulder height. Second: one crumpled sheet against two sheets crumpled together into one ball, so the second ball has twice the weight but about the same size and shape. Drop them together several times and watch which lands first. The weights are the same in the first race and different in the second, yet the air decides both. Write down what you saw.
:::

:::careful
> "At terminal speed the drop has stopped speeding up, so gravity has stopped pulling on it."

Gravity is pulling exactly as hard as ever. The drag has simply grown until it pulls back equally hard, so the two forces cancel and the speed stays constant. Take the air away, as on the Moon, and the drop would go on speeding up all the way down.
:::

:::check
Two skydivers jump in the same spread-eagled position, one much heavier than the other. The heavier one ends up falling faster. Why, if both are pulled towards Earth with the same acceleration at the start?
???
Both start at 9.8 m/s², but the heavier skydiver has more weight to balance. Drag grows with speed, so she must reach a higher speed before the drag on her matches her larger weight. Her terminal speed is higher.
:::

:::school
IGCSE calls drag through air "air resistance". The rule you will need is the one from Chapter 2: an object keeps a constant speed in a straight line unless a resultant force acts. At terminal speed the resultant force is zero, so the speed is constant.
:::

In the live figure for this section, set the air to zero and drop a feather and a hammer: they fall together. Now slide the air up a little at a time. Predict which separates from the other first, and whether the heavier object's final speed is higher or lower than the lighter one's, before you look.

## 4.3 On the Moon

:::scene Hadley Rille, the Moon, 2 August 1971

The last moonwalk of Apollo 15 was nearly over. David Scott, the mission's commander, stood in front of the television camera with a geological hammer in one hand and a falcon's feather in the other. The hammer had a mass of 1.32 kg. He held them out at about the same height, roughly 1.6 m up, in front of the camera, and let go of both at the same moment.

:::predict
On the Moon there is no air. A hammer and a feather are let go together from the same height. Which lands first?
- The hammer: it is far heavier
- The feather: it is lighter and the Moon's pull is weak
- They land together
???
They land together. With no air to hold the feather back, nothing separates them: the Moon pulls the hammer much harder, and the hammer is exactly that much harder to get moving. NASA's transcription of the moment has Scott saying that "Mr Galileo was correct in his findings".
:::

Three things happened in that drop, and it is worth taking them apart. First, the hammer fell. Many people are sure that there is no gravity where there is no air. In one survey, 81% of 13-year-olds did not think there is gravity in space. But the hammer fell, and so did the feather: the Moon pulls. Second, they fell *together*, which is Stevin's single thud with the Moon supplying the vacuum. Third, they fell slowly. From 1.6 m the pair took about 1.4 s to land, where on Earth a hammer takes 0.57 s. The Moon is a smaller, lighter world, and its pull at its surface gives an acceleration of about 1.6 to 1.7 m/s², roughly one sixth of Earth's.

:::fig c4_moon_strobe() | A drop from 1.6 m photographed with a flash every 0.2 s, on Earth and on the Moon. On Earth the hammer lands after 0.57 s, before the third flash; a feather dropped beside it would lag far behind in the air. On the Moon the hammer and the feather fall side by side, slowly, and land together after 1.38 s. Positions are computed from ½*gt*² with *g* = 9.8 and 1.67 m/s².

Notice which fact explains which. The feather kept up with the hammer because there is no air. The two of them fell slowly because the Moon's pull is weaker. People often tangle these: "things fall in slow motion on the Moon because there is no air to push them." But air can only slow a fall. Take it away and a fall gets faster, not slower. The slow motion is all the Moon's gentle pull.

Scott gave the credit to Galileo. As we saw, the Leaning Tower drop is probably a legend, but the idea he was testing really was Galileo's, and Stevin's and Philoponus's before him: with the air out of the way, everything falls together. It took three and a half centuries and a journey to another world to see it done with a feather.

You can make a small vacuum of your own with a coin. Cut a paper disc a little smaller than a ₹5 coin and lay it flat on top of the coin. Drop the pair. The coin shields the paper from the air, so the paper rides down with it and the two land together, where the paper alone would flutter.

:::careful
> "There's no gravity on the Moon, because there's no air."

The hammer fell. The Moon pulls everything near it, at about a sixth of Earth's rate. Air and gravity have nothing to do with each other: air is the stuff that gets in the way of falling, and taking it away lets things fall freely, which is why the feather kept up.
:::

:::check
Suppose Stevin and de Groot had done their experiment on the Moon, dropping their two lead balls about 9 m onto a board. What would they have heard, and when?
???
One thud, as at Delft: the balls fall together whatever they weigh, and with no air there is nothing to separate them. They would have waited longer for it, about 3.3 s instead of 1.4 s, because the Moon's pull is weaker (9 = ½ × 1.67 × *t*² gives *t* ≈ 3.3 s).
:::

## 4.4 Thrown sideways

:::scene At home, at the kitchen table

Put two coins at the edge of a table. Nudge one so that it just drops off the edge. At the same instant, flick the other hard so that it flies out sideways. The flicked coin travels much farther before it lands. Does it stay in the air longer? Most people feel that it must: it was moving faster, it had more "force" behind it, so surely it had more to carry it. That feeling has a name, and you met it in Chapter 2.

:::recall 2
"The flicked coin keeps going forward because it carries the push of your finger with it, and when that runs out it falls." What is wrong with this picture?
???
This is the impetus idea from Chapter 2. Once the coin has left your finger, nothing pushes it forward. Its sideways speed simply stays, because a force is needed to *change* motion, not to keep it going. And it starts falling at once, not when something runs out: the Earth's pull acts from the first instant.
:::

:::predict
Two coins leave the edge of a table at the same instant. One is nudged so that it just drops; the other is flicked so that it flies out sideways. Which hits the floor first?
- The dropped coin: the flicked one is going faster and stays up longer
- The flicked coin: it hits the floor harder and sooner
- Both at the same moment
???
Both at the same moment. Listen: one clink. From a table about 75 cm high, both are in the air for 0.39 s, however hard you flick. Flicking changes how far the coin goes, not how long it takes to fall.
:::

Here is the idea that unlocks this, and it is one of the most useful ideas in the whole book. A flying coin has two motions at once: a sideways one and a downward one. Treat them separately. Sideways, after the coin leaves your finger, nothing pushes it or pulls it (the air, over a short flight, is too little to notice). So its sideways speed stays whatever the flick gave it. Downward, the Earth pulls, and the coin is in free fall: it speeds up downward at 9.8 m/s², exactly like the dropped coin beside it. The two motions do not interfere. The sideways motion does not hold the coin up, and the fall does not slow the sideways motion. All they share is the clock.

:::fig c4_two_coins() | Two coins photographed every 0.1 s after leaving a 75 cm table at the same instant, one dropped and one flicked at 2 m/s. At each flash they are at the same height: the dashed lines are level. The flicked coin moves an equal step sideways each 0.1 s, because nothing changes its sideways speed, while its downward steps grow as it falls. Both land after 0.39 s; the flicked one lands 78 cm out.
:::live projectile

So the dropped coin and the flicked coin fall in step, and they land together. The flicked coin simply covers ground while it does so. Flick it at 2 m/s and it lands 78 cm from the table; flick it at 4 m/s and it lands 1.56 m out, but still after 0.39 s. Put the two motions together and the path is a curve, the smooth arc that any thrown ball follows. An object moving under gravity alone like this is called a ==projectile==, and its path is its trajectory.

:::note Projectile
Anything thrown or launched that then moves under the pull of the Earth alone. Its sideways speed stays constant and it falls at *g*, and the two motions are independent, so its path is a curve.
:::

:::work
Table height *h* = 0.75 m, horizontal flick speed *v*_x = 2.0 m/s:
Vertical fall: *h* = ½ *g t*² → *t* = √(2 × 0.75 m ÷ 9.8 m/s²) = 0.39 s (same for dropped and flicked coins)
Horizontal range: *x* = *v*_x × *t* = 2.0 m/s × 0.39 s = 0.78 m (78 cm)
:::

:::five Independence of perpendicular motions
? What is it?
The rule that a projectile's horizontal motion and vertical motion happen simultaneously without affecting each other: horizontal velocity stays constant (when air drag is negligible) while vertical velocity changes downward at *g*.
? Why is it called this?
*Perpendicular* means at right angles (horizontal and vertical), and *independent* means neither motion speeds up, slows down, or alters the clock of the other.
? What problem did it solve?
Before Galileo, gunners and philosophers thought a cannonball flew in a straight line until its "impetus" was spent and only then dropped to the ground, making it impossible to calculate trajectories or explain why a flicked coin and a dropped coin land at the same instant.
? Why does it work?
Because Newton's second law (*F* = *ma*) is a vector equation: a vertical force (gravity) has zero component along the horizontal axis, so it produces zero horizontal acceleration while producing the full downward acceleration *g* vertically.
? How does it help?
It turns any two-dimensional throw or jump into two simple one-dimensional sums linked by the same flight time *t*: use the vertical motion to find how long the object is in the air, and multiply by the horizontal speed to find where it lands.
?? How do we know?
Stroboscopic photographs of two balls released together—one dropped from rest, one projected horizontally—show both balls on the exact same horizontal line in every flash from release to floor.
?? What it isn't
It does not mean the path is straight, and it does not mean the total speed stays constant: the horizontal speed *v*_x stays constant while the downward speed *v*_y grows, so the combined speed √(*v*_x² + *v*_y²) increases as the projectile falls.
?? Where does it stop working?
When air resistance is large (as for a fast cricket ball, a badminton shuttlecock, or a spinning ball), quadratic drag couples the horizontal and vertical speeds so that a fast sideways throw slightly increases the drag opposing the vertical fall.
:::

The physics course at MIT shows this with a trick that looks impossible. A gun is aimed straight at an apple hanging across the room, and the moment the gun fires, the apple is released. The bullet does not fly straight; it falls as it flies. But the apple falls too, at exactly the same rate, so the bullet meets it in mid-air, every time, however fast or slow the shot. Both fall together; the only thing that matters is pointing straight at the apple.

Now, the curved paths. In 1980 three researchers asked university students to draw the path of a ball coming out of a curved tube lying flat on a table. A third drew the ball still curving after it left a C-shaped tube, and half did so for a spiral tube. But with nothing to push it round any more, the ball goes straight. A path curves only while something is bending it: the tube's wall, or the Earth's pull. There is no "curviness" stored in the ball, just as there is no push stored in the coin.

One honesty note. All this ignores the air, and for a flicked coin or a tossed ball across a room that is fine. For a long, fast throw it is not. In the MIT course notes a ball thrown at 30 m/s lands at only about 72% of the distance the no-air curve predicts, and it comes down more steeply than it went up. A spinning ball can also curve sideways in the air, which is the drift a spin bowler gets before the ball even reaches the pitch. The clean two-motion picture is the skeleton; air adds the flesh.

:::careful
> "The flicked coin stays up longer because it is going faster."

Going faster sideways has no effect on falling. Both coins start with no downward speed and both are pulled down at 9.8 m/s², so they reach the floor together. Speed sideways only decides where the coin lands, not when.
:::

:::check
A ball is thrown horizontally from a balcony and takes 1.2 s to reach the ground. The same ball is then thrown horizontally from the same spot at twice the speed. How long does it take to reach the ground now, and what changes?
???
Still 1.2 s. The time to fall depends only on the height and on *g*; the sideways speed does not touch it. What changes is where the ball lands: twice the sideways speed for the same time means it lands twice as far out.
:::

Then double the sideways speed and predict again. Finally set the launch angle upward and watch the sideways steps: do they change size while the ball rises and falls?

## 4.5 Going round

:::scene Mumbai, on a local train

The train takes a curve and everyone standing sways the same way, outward from the bend, grabbing the handles. It feels exactly like being pushed. Ask anyone what pushed them and they will say "centrifugal force", the force that flings you outward when you go round a corner. The feeling is real. The force, as we will see, is not what it seems, and the test is simple enough to do on the kitchen floor.

Cut a gap about 5 cm wide in the rim of a paper plate. Put a marble inside and roll it round and round against the rim, fast. It circles until it reaches the gap.

:::predict
A marble rolls round the inside of a paper plate with a gap cut in its rim. When the marble reaches the gap, which way does it go?
- Outward, away from the centre of the plate, flung by the spinning
- Straight on, along the direction it was moving at that instant
- It keeps curving round for a while, then straightens out
???
Straight on, along the line it was moving on at that instant: a tangent to the circle. It does not fly outward, and it does not keep curving. In the 1980 study, 30% of students drew a curved path for a ball let go from a string; the marble disagrees with them every time.
:::

Think about what the rim was doing before the gap. A marble rolling in a straight line at a steady speed needs no force at all; Chapter 2 settled that. But the marble was not going straight. Its direction was changing every instant, and a change of direction is a change of velocity, which only a force can cause. Which force? The rim, pushing the marble inward, towards the centre, all the way round. Take the rim away and there is no inward push, so there is no more turning. The marble does what everything does when nothing acts on it: it carries straight on.

:::fig c4_whirl() | A marble rolling round the inside of a paper plate. At every moment its velocity points along the rim and the rim pushes it inward, towards the centre: that push is what keeps turning it. Where the rim is cut away the push stops, and the marble carries on along the line it was moving on. Nothing pushes it outward.
:::live whirl

That is the whole secret of going round. Anything moving in a circle is being pulled or pushed towards the centre, and that inward force is doing the turning. For a stone on a string it is the string's pull. For a car on a bend it is the friction of the road on the tyres. For the ping-pong balls in a bucket swung fast in a vertical circle, an MIT lecture demonstration, it is the bucket's bottom pressing on them, which is why they stay in even when the bucket is upside down at the top. For the Moon going round the Earth, it is gravity, which is where the next chapter begins. The inward force needed grows quickly with speed: on the same bend, a car at 100 km/h needs four times the sideways grip of a car at 50 km/h, because the needed acceleration goes as the speed squared. That is why fast cornering is dangerous. Physicists call any force doing this job ==centripetal==, which is Latin for "centre-seeking". It is a role, not a new kind of force: the string's tension, the road's friction or Earth's gravity plays it.

:::note Centripetal force
The inward force, towards the centre, that keeps an object moving in a circle by continually changing its direction. It is always some ordinary force doing the job: tension, friction, gravity, the push of a wall.
:::

:::eq
**centripetal acceleration**: *a*_c = *v*² ÷ *r*;  **centripetal force**: *F*_c = *m v*² ÷ *r*
:::

:::work
A 0.50 kg stone is whirled in a horizontal circle of radius *r* = 1.0 m at a steady speed *v* = 4.0 m/s:
Inward acceleration: *a*_c = *v*² ÷ *r* = (4.0 m/s)² ÷ 1.0 m = 16 m/s²
Inward pull needed from the string: *F*_c = *m a*_c = 0.50 kg × 16 m/s² = 8.0 N
(At twice the speed, 8.0 m/s, the string would have to supply 4 × 8.0 N = 32 N.)
:::

So what pushed you on the train? Nothing pushed you outward. When the train turned, your body did what the marble did at the gap: it carried straight on, while the train swung round under you. The handle, or the side of the carriage, then pushed you *inward* to bring you round with it. The outward "force" is the feeling of your own straight-line motion meeting a vehicle that is turning. A washing machine's spin cycle works on the same fact. The drum wall pushes the clothes inward, round and round. Water at a hole in the drum has nothing pushing it inward, so it carries straight on, through the hole and out.

:::school
Your syllabus never uses the word "centrifugal". IGCSE describes circular motion as "motion in a circular path due to a force perpendicular to the motion". In an exam, name the real inward force: the tension in the string, the friction of the road, or the gravitational pull of the Sun.
:::

Now the honest part about "centrifugal force", because you will keep hearing the words. Someone who insists on describing the world from inside the turning train, treating the carriage as if it were standing still, finds that Newton's rules do not quite work there: things seem to slide outward with nothing pushing them. To make the rules work in that spinning view, they have to add an outward force by hand. Physicists call that a fictitious force. It has no source; nothing is doing the pushing. Seen from the platform, there is no such force, just a passenger carrying straight on and a train turning. Both descriptions can be made to agree, but the one from the platform is the simpler and truer one, because in it every force has something causing it.

:::home A marble, a paper plate and a gap
Cut a gap about 5 cm wide in the rim of a paper plate and put the plate on a smooth floor, a tiled one is best. Roll a marble round the inside, fast enough that it hugs the rim. **Predict first:** which way will it leave at the gap? Mark your guess on the floor with a coin. Then watch, several times, from different starting points. It always leaves straight along the rim's direction at the gap, never outward. If you have a bucket with a sturdy handle, ask an adult to do the swing: a little water in the bucket, swung fast in a vertical circle outdoors, stays in even at the top, because the bucket's bottom keeps pushing the water inward, towards the centre of the swing.
:::

:::careful
> "A force throws me outward when the train goes round a bend."

Nothing pushes you outward. Your body carries straight on while the train turns, and then the handle or the carriage wall pushes you inward to bring you round. The only real force in the turning is the inward one. The outward feeling is your own straight-line motion meeting a turning vehicle.
:::

:::five Centripetal force
? What is it?
Any net force that points inward toward the centre of a curve and keeps an object moving along a circular path by continually turning its velocity vector.
? Why is it called this?
Isaac Newton coined *vis centripeta* in 1687 from Latin *centrum* (centre) and *petere* (to seek), to contrast with Christiaan Huygens' earlier *vis centrifuga* (centre-fleeing).
? What problem did it solve?
People felt tugged outward on a bend or when whirling a sling and assumed circular motion was driven by an outward push, which left them unable to explain why a stone released from a sling flies along the tangent rather than straight out from the centre.
? Why does it work?
By Newton's first law, a body with no force on it goes straight at constant speed; to bend that straight path into a circle at speed *v* and radius *r* requires an inward acceleration of *v*²/*r* toward the centre at every instant.
? How does it help?
It lets you calculate the road grip a car needs on a bend (four times as much grip at twice the speed), why water stays in an overhead swinging bucket, and how gravity holds the Moon and satellites in orbit.
?? How do we know?
Roll a marble round the inside of a paper plate with a gap cut in its rim: the instant the rim's inward push ends at the gap, the marble travels straight along the tangent, never outward.
?? What it isn't
It is not a new fifth force of nature to add alongside tension, friction or gravity on a free-body diagram; it is simply the job description for whichever real force (string tension, road friction, or gravity) is pointing toward the centre.
?? Where does it stop working?
If the required inward force *mv*²/*r* exceeds what the string or road friction can supply, the string snaps or the tyres skid and the object leaves the circle along a straighter path.
:::

:::check
A racing car goes round a bend at a perfectly steady 120 km/h. Is it accelerating? If so, which way?
???
Yes. Its speed is steady but its direction is changing, and velocity includes direction, so its velocity is changing: that is an acceleration. It points towards the centre of the bend, where the friction of the road on the tyres is pulling the car.
:::

## The ball off the Sea Link

:::scene Bandra–Worli Sea Link, Mumbai

:::case The ball off the Sea Link
The Bandra–Worli Sea Link is a 5.6 km cable-stayed bridge with a main span of 250 m. Imagine standing at its railing, high above the water, with three identical tennis balls and a friend who is sure of herself.

1. You drop the first ball straight down.
2. At the same instant your friend throws the second ball out horizontally, gently.
3. Also at the same instant, a third ball is thrown out horizontally, as hard as possible.
4. Your friend says: "The hard-thrown ball has the most force, so it stays up longest. The dropped one hits first."
5. A car is driving along the bridge beside you at a steady speed. Its passenger holds a fourth ball out of the window and simply lets go of it.

Ignore the air. Which of the first three balls hits the water first? Where do they land, compared with one another? And where does the fourth ball land: behind the car, beside it, or ahead of it?
???
The first three balls hit the water at the same instant. Each starts with no downward speed, and each is pulled down at 9.8 m/s² from the moment it is released; a sideways throw changes nothing about the fall (Section 4.4). Your friend has the impetus idea from Chapter 2: there is no "force" stored in the hard-thrown ball, only a larger sideways speed that stays constant. The dropped ball lands straight below the railing; the gently thrown one lands some way out; the hard-thrown one lands farther out still, each in proportion to the sideways speed it was given, because all three are in the air for the same time.

The fourth ball lands beside the car, directly below where the passenger's hand is at that moment. When it was released it already had the car's forward speed, and nothing acts to slow that sideways motion, so it keeps pace with the car while it falls. Seen from the car it drops straight down; seen from the railing it traces the same kind of curve as the thrown balls. (With air, all four would fall a little short and a little late; the hard-thrown ball would lose the most.)
:::
