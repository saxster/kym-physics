Chapter 4 left two facts lying side by side. With the air out of the way, everything falls alike: the hammer and the feather landed together on the Moon. And a thing moving sideways falls exactly as a dropped thing does: the flicked coin and the dropped coin clinked at the same instant. Hold on to both. Put together, they lift the Moon into the sky and keep it there, and they explain why a woman in the International Space Station can let go of her pen and watch it hang in front of her face.

That is this chapter's puzzle. The station circles about 400 km above the ground. Work out how strong Earth's pull is up there and the answer is about 89% of what it is in Mumbai: not zero, not even small. Yet everything inside floats. Something is wrong with the way most of us picture "weightless", and fixing it takes a cannon on a mountain, a careful look at the Moon, and a phone dropped onto a pillow.

## 5.1 The apple and the Moon

:::scene Woolsthorpe, 1666

In 1666, in the plague years, Isaac Newton was 23 and at home at Woolsthorpe, his family's farmhouse in Lincolnshire. Sixty years later he told a friend, William Stukeley, that his thinking about gravity had been "occasion'd by the fall of an apple, as he sat in contemplative mood". There really was an apple tree in the garden. The apple did not hit him on the head; that detail was added later, and the story itself rests on an old man's memory, so hold it lightly. But the question he asked is well recorded, and it is a wonderful one.

An apple falls. Everybody knows that. Newton's question was: how high does the pull that drops the apple reach? To the top of the tree, obviously. To the top of the air? And if it reaches that far, why should it stop? What if it reached the Moon?

Here is why the question had teeth. Chapter 2's law says a thing left alone moves in a straight line at a steady speed. The Moon does not: it goes round Earth, roughly in a circle, once in about 27.3 days. Chapter 4's last section says that anything going round must be pulled towards the centre by some force, or it would fly off along a tangent. So something is pulling the Moon towards the Earth, all the time. Newton's guess was that it is the very same pull that drops the apple, reaching out across 384,000 km of empty space.

:::recall 2
"Nothing is pushing the Moon along its orbit, so it ought to slow down and eventually stop." What is wrong here?
???
The Moon needs no push to keep moving; that is the lesson of Chapter 2. A force is needed only to change motion. Earth's pull does change the Moon's motion, but sideways: it bends the path into a circle without speeding the Moon up or slowing it down. No force along the orbit is needed, and none acts.
:::

A guess is cheap; Newton tested it with numbers. The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away, sixty times farther from Earth's centre than the apple is. Newton supposed that the pull weakens with distance in a particular way: double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter, treble it and the pull drops to a ninth. This is an ==inverse-square== rule. Sixty times the distance then means 60 × 60 = 3600 times weaker. So at the Moon, each kilogram should be pulled 1/3600 as hard as at the ground, and the Moon should be falling with an acceleration of 9.8 ÷ 3600 = 0.00272 m/s². Now, is it?

:::note Inverse-square rule
A pull that weakens with the square of the distance: twice as far, a quarter as strong; ten times as far, a hundredth. Gravity follows it, and the same arithmetic will come back later in the book.
:::

:::predict
A weather satellite is launched to a height of one Earth radius (6,371 km) above the ground, so it is twice as far from the Earth's centre as you are in Mumbai. How strong is the Earth's gravitational pull on the satellite up there, compared with its weight on the ground?
- Zero: it is far out in space, beyond the atmosphere
- Half as strong, because it is twice as far from the Earth's centre
- One quarter as strong, because it is twice as far from the Earth's centre
???
One quarter as strong. Distance in Newton's law is measured from the Earth's centre, so climbing one Earth radius above the surface doubles your distance from the centre (*r* goes from 1 *R*_E to 2 *R*_E). Under an inverse-square rule, twice the distance means 1/2² = 1/4 of the pull, so *g* up there is about 9.8 ÷ 4 = 2.45 m/s².
:::

Yes. We can check it ourselves, with nothing but the Moon's distance and its month. Going once round a circle of radius 384,000 km in 27.3 days means the Moon moves at about 1 km/s. A thing moving at 1 km/s in a straight line would, in one second, be 1 km further along and no closer to Earth. The real Moon, in that second, has curved towards Earth by 1.36 mm. That is its fall: 1.36 mm in the first second. An apple falls 4.9 m in its first second, and 4.9 m is 3600 times 1.36 mm. The Moon falls exactly as the inverse-square rule said it should. Newton did this comparison himself and found that the two agreed, as he put it, pretty nearly; the modern figures agree to better than 1%, and the leftover is the slightly oval shape of the orbit and the fact that Earth moves too.

:::fig c5_moon_fall() | The Moon, moving along its orbit at about 1 km/s. The dashed line is where it would go if nothing pulled it; the curve is where it really goes; the gap between them is how far it has fallen. In one minute that gap is 4.9 m, which is what an apple falls in one second. The curve is hugely exaggerated: in a real minute the Moon travels 61 km and drops only 4.9 m.

Put another way: in one minute the Moon falls 4.9 m below its straight line, which is what the apple falls in one second. A minute is 60 seconds, so a one-minute fall goes 60 × 60 = 3600 times as far as a one-second fall, and the Moon's acceleration is 3600 times gentler. The apple and the Moon are doing the same thing, under the same pull.

So the pull of the Earth is not a special property of the ground. Newton proposed that every piece of matter pulls every other piece, with a pull that grows with both masses and weakens as the inverse square of the distance between them. This is ==gravity== in Newton's sense, the universal law of gravitation, and it was the first time anyone had said that the rules on Earth and the rules in the sky are the same rules. Remember the luggage scales from Chapter 3, too: the Moon pulls Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls the Moon. Earth is far more massive, so it moves far less, but it does move: the two of them circle a shared balance point that lies about 1700 km beneath Earth's surface.

:::note Gravity (Newton's law of gravitation)
Every mass pulls every other mass. The pull is bigger for bigger masses, and it weakens with the square of the distance between their centres. It never switches off; it only gets weaker with distance.
:::

:::eq
*F* = *G m*₁ *m*₂ ÷ *r*²
:::

:::evidence Does Earth's pull reach the Moon? | Newton's test, with modern numbers
Two numbers from completely different places. From the Moon's distance and its month alone, its path curves towards Earth at 0.00272 m/s². From a falling apple and the inverse-square rule, the pull at 60 Earth radii should give 9.8 ÷ 3600 = 0.00272 m/s². They match to better than 1%, and that match is the evidence that one pull does both jobs. The MIT course notes run the sum the other way: from the law and the Moon's distance they predict the length of the Moon's month, and get 27.2 days.
:::

:::careful
> "The Moon falls 1.36 mm closer to Earth every second."

It falls 1.36 mm below the straight line it would otherwise have followed. But in that same second it also moves about 1 km sideways, and the straight line leads slightly *away* from Earth, so the two effects cancel and the Moon's distance stays about the same. It is always falling and never arriving.
:::

:::five Universal gravitation
? What is it?
The law that every mass in the universe attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres (*F* = *Gm*₁*m*₂/*r*²).
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *gravitas*, heaviness or weight (from *gravis*, heavy), combined with *universal* because Newton showed in 1687 that one and the same attraction governs falling stones on Earth and planets in the sky.
? What problem did it solve?
Since antiquity, Earth and the heavens were thought to obey different laws: heavy things fell toward the Earth while planets moved in eternal circles for reasons of their own. Nobody knew what held the Moon in its path or why Kepler's three laws held.
? Why does it work?
Because gravitational influence spreads out across three-dimensional space so its strength thins out with the surface area of a sphere (4π*r*²), weakening as 1/*r*² while pulling in proportion to how much mass each body has.
? How does it help?
It lets us weigh the Earth in a shed (Cavendish, 1798), weigh the Sun and Jupiter from the periods of their satellites, predict the return of Halley's comet, and discover Neptune by pen and paper from the wobble of Uranus.
?? How do we know?
Newton's apple-and-Moon test showed that the Moon's inward acceleration at 60 Earth radii (0.00272 m/s²) is 1/60² = 1/3600 of *g* at Earth's surface (9.8 m/s²), and Cavendish directly measured the tiny attraction between lead spheres in 1798.
?? What it isn't
It is not a force that requires air or stops at the top of the atmosphere: at the International Space Station 400 km up, Earth's gravity is still 89% as strong as on the ground in Mumbai.
?? Where does it stop working?
Newton's inverse-square law is extraordinarily accurate across the solar system and gives way to Einstein's general relativity only in very strong gravitational fields, such as near the Sun (where Mercury's orbit precesses by an extra 43 arcseconds per century) or around black holes.
:::

:::check
Suppose Earth kept the same mass but swelled to twice its radius, and you stood on the new surface. By what factor would your weight change?
???
It would fall to a quarter. Your weight is Earth's pull on you, and you would be twice as far from Earth's centre. Twice the distance, under an inverse-square rule, means one quarter of the pull. Your mass would not change at all.
:::

## 5.2 Newton's cannon

:::scene In orbit, 400 km above Mumbai

The International Space Station circles about 400 km above the ground; on some evenings it can be seen from Mumbai as a bright, steady point crossing the sky in a few minutes. Use Section 5.1's rule to find Earth's pull up there: the station is at 6771 km from Earth's centre against 6371 km for us, and the square of 6371/6771 is 0.885. Gravity on the station is 89% of gravity in Mumbai. And yet the astronauts float. Before reading on, decide what you think is going on.

:::predict
Gravity at the space station's height is about 89% of its strength on the ground. Why doesn't the station fall down?
- Its engines keep pushing it up
- It is above the air, and gravity cannot act where there is no air
- It is falling, but it moves sideways so fast that the ground keeps curving away beneath it
- There is no gravity in space, whatever the sum says
???
It is falling, and it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away as fast as it falls. The station is in free fall all the time, exactly like the dropped hammer, and it never lands because Earth is round. Its engines are off almost all the time, and gravity has nothing to do with air.
:::

Newton saw this with a cannon. Imagine a cannon on a mountain so tall that it stands above the air, firing horizontally. Fire a slow shot and it curves down and lands a little way off, like the flicked coin. Fire faster and it lands farther round the Earth. Keep increasing the speed and the landing point moves farther and farther away, because the ground is curving away under the falling shot. At one particular speed, about 8 km/s at Earth's surface, the ground curves away exactly as fast as the shot falls. The shot never lands. It falls all the way round the Earth and comes back to the cannon from behind, and if the cannon has been moved away, it carries on falling round for ever. That is an ==orbit==. Faster still, and the path stretches into a long loop. At about 11 km/s the shot never comes back at all: it has escaped.

:::note Orbit
The path of something falling freely under gravity but moving sideways fast enough never to land. A satellite in orbit is not held up; it is falling and missing.
:::

:::fig c5_cannon() | Newton's cannon, computed. From one mountain top, shots at five speeds: too slow, and it lands; faster, and it lands farther round; at about 8 km/s the ground curves away as fast as the shot falls, so it circles for ever; a little faster gives a long loop; at about 11 km/s it leaves for good. The mountain is drawn far too tall so that it can be seen.
:::live cannon3d

Now the station. At 400 km up, the speed that makes the ground curve away as fast as you fall is about 7.7 km/s, and that is how fast the station moves: NASA puts it at about five miles a second, once round the Earth in about 90 minutes, sixteen sunrises a day. Check the two numbers against each other. In one second the station falls about 4.3 m towards Earth, from the 8.7 m/s² of gravity up there. In that same second it moves 7.7 km sideways, and over 7.7 km the round Earth's surface curves away by, as it happens, 4.3 m. The fall and the curve match, so the station's height never changes. It is the Moon's story again, closer in and faster.

:::recall 4.1
In Chapter 4 you dropped a cricket ball and a coin. Now an astronaut of 70 kg and a space station of 420 tonnes are both falling round the Earth. Does the heavier one fall faster and pull away from the lighter one?
???
No. Everything falls alike when nothing but gravity acts, whatever its mass, because a heavier thing is pulled harder and is exactly that much harder to accelerate. The station and the astronaut fall round the Earth together, so neither drifts towards the other. That is why she floats in the middle of the cabin.
:::

That recall is the whole answer to the chapter's puzzle. The astronaut floats not because gravity is absent, but because she and the station are falling together. Think of a lift whose cable has just snapped: for the moments before it hits the bottom, everything in it falls at the same rate, so the floor no longer presses on your feet and a dropped pen hangs beside you. The station is that lift, except that it is falling *around* the Earth instead of into it, so the moments never end. There is a word people use for this, and it needs care. In the language of your syllabus, the astronaut still has ==weight==: Earth pulls on her mass, at 89% of the pull in Mumbai. What she has lost is the *feeling* of weight, which comes from a floor pushing up on you. Nothing pushes up on her, so she feels weightless. Physicists say she is in free fall, or in microgravity; "zero gravity" is a phrase to avoid, because it is simply false.

:::note Weight, and feeling weightless
Weight is the pull of gravity on an object: a force, in newtons. You *feel* your weight only through whatever pushes back on you, like the floor. Fall freely, and nothing pushes back, so you feel weightless although your weight is unchanged. Astronauts in orbit are in this state all the time.
:::

The scenes of floating in the film *Apollo 13* were filmed in an aircraft that climbs and then falls freely along a great arc: NASA's version gives about 25 seconds of free fall each time. A phone falling onto a pillow gives a third of a second, which is enough.

:::home The phone that reads zero
Install the free phyphox app, which uses the phone's own motion sensor, and open "Acceleration (with g)". Held still, it reads about 9.8 m/s²: that is the sensor feeling the phone's weight, the way you feel yours through your feet. Put a thick, soft pillow on the floor. Hold the phone flat above it, no higher than your knee, about 40 cm, start recording, and let it drop. Then look at the graph. For about 0.29 s, while the phone was falling, the reading dropped to about zero. Nothing was pushing on the sensor, so it felt no weight, although the Earth's pull on it never changed. For that third of a second your phone was an astronaut. The live figure for this section does the same thing with the book's own sensor reading.
:::

:::fig c5_phone_drop() | What a phone's motion sensor reports before, during and after a 40 cm fall onto a pillow: 9.8 m/s² while held still, about zero for the 0.29 s of the fall, a brief bump as the pillow stops it, then 9.8 again. The 9.8 and the zero are what the sensor really reads; the landing bump is drawn schematically.
:::live freefall

One more thing follows from the cannon. Once a satellite is above the air, it needs no engine to stay up: it is simply falling round, and nothing slows it. The hard and expensive part is getting it up there and up to speed. Very low orbits do slowly decay, because a little air still brushes past at that height and drags on the craft.

:::careful
> "There's no gravity in space. That's why astronauts float."

At the station's height gravity is about 89% of its strength on the ground, and it is what keeps the station from flying off in a straight line. The astronauts float because they and the station are falling round the Earth together, so nothing pushes up on them. They still have weight; they do not feel it.
:::

:::five Orbit
? What is it?
The closed or open path of a body falling freely under gravity while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing the body it circles.
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *orbita*, the wheel-rut or track left by a cartwheel (from *orbis*, a wheel or ring), adopted by medieval and Renaissance astronomers for the track of a planet across the sky.
? What problem did it solve?
Ancient astronomers imagined solid crystal spheres carrying the Moon and planets around, and modern intuition still imagines rockets must keep their engines firing to hold a satellite up.
? Why does it work?
Because at orbital speed (about 7.7 km/s at 400 km altitude), in each second the satellite falls toward Earth by the exact same 4.3 metres that the round Earth's surface curves away beneath it.
? How does it help?
It explains why astronauts float weightless inside the Space Station (craft and crew fall together with no floor pressing up), and how ISRO raised Chandrayaan-3 to the Moon using short engine burns at perigee and days of engine-off coasting.
?? How do we know?
Every satellite ever launched switches its main engine off once it reaches orbital speed and coasts for years, and a phone dropped 40 cm onto a pillow reads 0 m/s² on its accelerometer during the fall for the exact same reason.
?? What it isn't
An orbit is not a place where gravity has switched off; without gravity pulling inward at every instant, the spacecraft would fly off in a straight line into deep space.
?? Where does it stop working?
In low Earth orbit below a few hundred kilometres, wisps of thin upper air slowly drag on a satellite and bleed off its speed, causing the orbit to spiral down unless boosted from time to time.
:::

:::check
Anna says a satellite in orbit is in free fall. Tom says it cannot be, because its acceleration is not 9.8 m/s². Who is right?
???
Anna. Free fall means that gravity is the only force acting, and that is true of a satellite. The acceleration is smaller than 9.8 m/s² only because gravity is weaker up there (about 8.7 m/s² at 400 km), not because anything else is acting.
:::

:::school
IGCSE defines weight as "a gravitational force on an object that has mass", so in an exam an astronaut in orbit *has* weight; say that she "feels weightless" because she is in free fall. When the syllabus says that gravity "keeps an object in orbit", think of a satellite that keeps falling towards the Earth and keeps missing it. The orbital speed formula is *v* = 2π*r*/*T*: the circumference divided by the time for one circuit.
:::

## 5.3 Ellipses

:::scene Hven, 1576

Newton's law was the explanation. The thing it explained had been found a lifetime earlier by two men who could hardly have been more different. Tycho Brahe built an observatory, Uraniborg, on the small island of Hven, and for about twenty years measured the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets, without a telescope, more carefully than anyone before him. His positions disagreed with the printed tables, and his were the better ones.

:::scene Prague, 1600

Johannes Kepler arrived in Prague to work for Tycho in February 1600. Tycho died in October 1601, and Kepler got his data: twenty years of Mars. Kepler believed, as everyone did, that a planet moves in a circle, and he set out to find the circle that fitted Tycho's Mars. He could not. His best circle missed some of Tycho's positions by eight minutes of arc, a little more than an eighth of a degree. That is a tiny angle, small enough that many would have blamed the measurements. Kepler trusted Tycho. Eight minutes of arc, he decided, were enough to throw out two thousand years of circles.

:::predict
Mars's orbit, drawn to scale on a page, looks like which of these?
- A clearly squashed oval, like a rugby ball seen from the side
- A perfect circle with the Sun at its centre
- Almost a circle, but with the Sun noticeably off-centre
???
Almost a circle, with the Sun noticeably off-centre. Mars's orbit is only slightly stretched (its eccentricity is about 0.09), so the outline looks round. But the Sun is not at the middle: it sits about 9% of the way from the centre to the edge, so Mars is about 17% closer to the Sun at one end of its orbit than at the other.
:::

The shape that fitted was an ==ellipse==. You can draw one with two drawing pins and a loop of string: the pencil keeps the string taut and goes round, and the two pins are the ellipse's two ==foci==, a word Kepler invented. Push the pins together and the ellipse becomes a circle; pull them apart and it stretches. Kepler's first law is that each planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. The other focus is empty. Mars's ellipse is nearly a circle: pull the pins apart by only 9% of the ellipse's half-length and you have it. But that 9% puts the Sun clearly off-centre, and it was enough to break the circles.

:::note Ellipse and focus
An ellipse is the closed curve you get when a loop of string is kept taut round two pins: from any point on it, the distances to the two pins add up to the same total. The pins are its two foci. A circle is an ellipse with its two foci in the same place.
:::

:::figwide c5_ellipse() | Left: drawing an ellipse with two pins and a loop of string; the pins are the foci. Middle: Mars's orbit to scale, with a circle of the same size dashed over it. The outline is almost round, but the Sun sits 9% of the way from the centre to the edge. Right: Kepler's second law, with the ellipse exaggerated so that it can be seen. The two shaded areas, swept out in equal times, are equal: the planet races through the short fat one near the Sun and crawls round the long thin one far away.

Kepler was lucky in his planet. Among the planets other than Mercury, Mars has the most stretched orbit; Earth's is far rounder, with a stretch of only about 0.017, so our distance from the Sun varies only between about 147 and 152 million km over a year. Had Tycho spent twenty years on Venus, no naked-eye data could have revealed the ellipse. (That small change in Earth's distance, by the way, is not what makes seasons. Chapter 14 settles that.)

Kepler found two more rules in the same data. The second: a planet moves faster when it is nearer the Sun and slower when it is farther away, in such a way that a line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The first two laws were published in 1609, in his book *The New Astronomy*. The third came ten years later, in 1619: the square of a planet's year is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun. Measure time in Earth years and distance in Earth-distances (one astronomical unit, about 150 million km) and the rule is simply *P*² = *a*³. Mars takes 1.88 years to go round; 1.88² is 3.53, the cube root of 3.53 is 1.52, so Mars is 1.52 times as far from the Sun as we are, about 228 million km. Kepler got the distances of all the planets from their years, with a pencil.

Notice what these laws are. They describe the orbits with great precision. They do not say *why* the orbits are ellipses, or why the planet speeds up near the Sun, or why the squares and cubes should match. Kepler tried hard to explain them and could not. The explanation waited eighty years for Newton: an inverse-square pull from the Sun forces exactly these shapes and exactly these speeds, and nothing else. Keep the two apart in your mind. Kepler's laws are what the sky does; Newton's law is why.

:::home Two pins and a loop of string
Ask an adult for two drawing pins and push them into a flap cut from a cardboard box, about 5 cm apart. Tie thread into a loop that fits loosely round both pins, put a pencil inside the loop, pull it taut and trace all the way round. You have drawn an ellipse, and the pins are its two foci. Now move the pins closer together and draw again, then farther apart. **Predict first:** what happens to the shape each time? To draw Mars's orbit to scale, make the loop so that the pencil reaches about 10 cm from the midpoint, and put the pins 0.9 cm either side of the midpoint. Mark one pin "Sun". It barely looks like an ellipse at all, and that is the point.
:::

:::careful
> "The Sun sits at the centre of the ellipse, and planets move in long, squashed ovals."

The Sun sits at one focus, not at the centre, and the other focus is empty. And the planets' ellipses are very nearly circles: Earth's distance from the Sun changes by only about 3% over a year. Textbook drawings stretch the ellipse so that you can see its shape; the real thing is round with an off-centre Sun.
:::

:::check
A distant object goes round the Sun at an average distance of 50 astronomical units, fifty times Earth's distance. How long is its year?
???
About 354 Earth years. Kepler's third law gives *P*² = 50³ = 125,000, so *P* = √125,000 ≈ 354 years. The farther out, the slower the journey and the longer the path.
:::

## 5.4 Weighing the Earth

:::scene Clapham, 1798

Newton's law says that every mass pulls every other mass. Not just planets: a book pulls a cup, you pull the person next to you on the train. Nobody notices, because for everyday masses the pull is absurdly small, and for a hundred years after Newton nobody managed to measure it. Then Henry Cavendish, a man in his late sixties who lived at Clapham on the edge of London, and who was so shy that, the story goes, he spoke to his housekeeper only by written notes, set out to catch it.

:::predict
Two 1 kg bags of rice sit on a table 1 m apart. Do they pull on each other?
- No. Gravity is something planets and stars have, not bags of rice
- Yes, but far too weakly to notice, about a hundred-billionth of their weight
- Yes, about as strongly as a fridge magnet pulls a coin
???
Yes, far too weakly to notice. Each bag pulls the other with about 0.000 000 000 067 N, roughly 150 billion times less than the weight of either bag. It is real, and Cavendish measured a pull of this kind in 1798.
:::

His instrument, designed by Michell, was a torsion balance: a light rod hanging from a thin wire, with a small lead ball at each end. When a big lead ball was brought close to each small one, the tiny pull swung the rod round a little, twisting the wire until the wire's resistance balanced the pull. The twist was minute, and anything could spoil it: a draught, a change of temperature, the warmth of a human body. So Cavendish put the whole apparatus in a closed shed, stayed outside, and watched the rod through telescopes poked through holes in the shed wall. From the size of the twist and the stiffness of the wire he got the pull between lead balls whose masses he knew.

:::fig c5_cavendish() | Cavendish's experiment, seen from above and simplified. Inside a closed shed, a light rod hangs by a thin wire with a small lead ball at each end. A big lead ball is set beside each small one, on opposite sides, so that the two tiny pulls twist the rod the same way. Cavendish watched the twist through a telescope through a hole in the wall, so that his own body heat would not stir the air inside.

Why does measuring the pull between lead balls weigh the Earth? Because you can compare. The Earth pulls a small lead ball with a force you can measure on a scale: its weight. Cavendish now also knew how hard a big lead ball of known mass pulls it from a known distance. Newton's law says the pull grows with the mass of the puller and weakens with the square of the distance; the Earth's centre is 6371 km away, and the big lead ball's centre was a few centimetres away. Knowing both pulls, both distances and the lead ball's mass, the only unknown left is the mass of the Earth. Cavendish published his answer as a density: the Earth, as a whole, is 5.48 times as dense as water. The modern figure is 5.51. He was within 1%, in a shed, with a wire.

Today's books restate his result as a measurement of *G*, the ==gravitational constant== in Newton's law, which is the same information in modern dress. *G* is about 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ in the units of newtons, metres and kilograms, which is why two 1 kg bags a metre apart pull with 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N. It is the least precisely known of all the fundamental constants, because gravity between laboratory masses is so faint. From *G*, Earth's radius and the 9.8 m/s² of Chapter 4, Earth's mass comes out at about 6 × 10²⁴ kg: six million million million million kilograms.

:::note Gravitational constant (*G*)
The number in Newton's law that sets how strong gravity is: about 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N for two 1 kg masses 1 m apart. It is thought to be the same everywhere in the universe. Do not confuse it with *g*, the acceleration of free fall, which is different on every world.
:::

This is a good moment to be precise about two words that everyday speech runs together. Your ==mass== is how much matter you are made of, measured in kilograms, and it is the same wherever you go. Your weight is the pull of gravity on that mass, measured in newtons, and it changes from world to world. A person of mass 60 kg weighs about 588 N on Earth. On the Moon the same person, with the same 60 kg, weighs about 100 N, a sixth as much. When someone says "I weigh 40 kg", they mean their mass; in a physics answer, mass goes in kilograms and weight in newtons. One more fact worth knowing: careful experiments have shown that gravity does not care what a mass is made of. Lead, wood, water or feather, the pull per kilogram is the same, to a few parts in a billion.

:::note Mass
The amount of matter in an object, in kilograms. It does not change when you move to the Moon or into orbit. Weight does.
:::

:::careful
> "Gravity is a thing planets and stars have. Small objects don't have gravity."

Every mass pulls every other, including you and this book, and Cavendish measured the pull between lead balls in 1798. It is just extraordinarily weak unless one of the masses is enormous. Earth's pull on you is strong only because Earth has six million million million million kilograms of stuff doing the pulling.
:::

:::check
Titan, Saturn's largest moon, has a radius of 2.58 × 10⁶ m and a mass of 1.35 × 10²³ kg. What is the acceleration of free fall at its surface?
???
About 1.35 m/s², a little less than on our Moon. The acceleration at a world's surface is *GM*/*R*²: 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 1.35 × 10²³ ÷ (2.58 × 10⁶)² ≈ 1.35 m/s². A hammer dropped on Titan would take about 1.5 s to fall 1.6 m.
:::

:::school
IGCSE wording: mass is "a measure of the quantity of matter in an object", and weight is "a gravitational force on an object that has mass". Write mass in kg and weight in N, and remember that *G* (the constant in Newton's law) and *g* (9.8 N/kg at Earth's surface) are different things.
:::

## 5.5 Predicting the sky

:::scene Cambridge, August 1684

In August 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton in Cambridge with a question: if the Sun pulled on a planet with a force weakening as the inverse square of the distance, what path would the planet follow? Newton answered at once: an ellipse. He had worked it out, he said, but he could not find the paper. Halley pressed him to write it up properly. The result, three years later, was the *Principia*, the book that contains everything in this chapter so far. The Royal Society was supposed to print it, but it had spent its money on a *History of Fishes* that did not sell, so Halley paid for the printing himself.

What Halley had seen was that a law like Newton's does more than describe. If you know where a body is and how fast it is moving, the law tells you where it will be next, and next after that, for as long as you care to calculate. Newton's law and his laws of motion allow only certain paths around the Sun: circles, ellipses, and two open curves for things moving too fast to be held. Find which one a body is on, and its future is written.

The law also lets you weigh things you cannot visit. Kepler's third law, *P*² = *a*³, holds for the planets because they all circle the same Sun. Newton's version of it includes the mass of whatever is being circled: the heavier the central body, the faster things must go round it at a given distance. Turn that round and an orbit becomes a weighing scale. A planet going round another star at Earth's distance in 0.71 of a year must be circling a star twice the Sun's mass, and Jupiter can be weighed from the periods of its moons. That is how the Sun, and every planet with a moon, has been weighed.

:::note Perturbation
A small change in an orbit caused by the pull of bodies other than the main one, such as Jupiter's tug on a comet or Neptune's on Uranus. The word means "disturbance".
:::

There is a catch, and it is what made the next two stories hard. Newton's law is simple for two bodies. But every body pulls every other, so a comet rounding the Sun is also tugged by Jupiter and Saturn as it passes them, and the planets tug one another. These tugs are called ==perturbations==, and the moment there are three bodies there is no neat formula for the path. The future still follows from the law, but you have to work it out step by step, a small stretch of time at a time, with a very great deal of arithmetic. Halley predicted that a certain comet would return in 1758. He died in 1742 and never saw whether it did; the case file at the end of this chapter tells what happened.

:::fig c5_comet() | A comet's orbit: a long ellipse with the Sun at one focus, drawn schematically with an eccentricity of 0.9. The dots mark where the comet is after each twenty-fourth of its period, so equal time passes between neighbouring dots. Almost all of them crowd the far end, where the comet crawls for decades; it races through the few weeks near the Sun. Out there, far from the Sun's pull, a nudge from a giant planet has years to shift its path.

:::scene Berlin, 23 September 1846

The second story is about Uranus, the planet beyond Saturn, which refused to stay where the calculations said it should be. Year after year it drifted off its predicted path, by small amounts that grew. Either Newton's law failed far from the Sun, or something unseen was pulling on Uranus.

:::predict
Uranus kept drifting away from the position that Newton's law predicted for it. Which of these was the right explanation?
- Newton's law weakens even faster than the inverse square far from the Sun
- An unseen planet beyond Uranus was pulling on it
- The old measurements of Uranus were simply wrong
???
An unseen planet. Urbain Le Verrier treated the drift as a perturbation and calculated, from Newton's law alone, where a planet must be to cause it. He posted the position to the Berlin observatory. On the night the letter arrived, 23 September 1846, Galle and d'Arrest pointed their telescope at the predicted spot and found Neptune within about a degree of it.
:::

Think about what that night meant. A planet nobody had ever seen was found with a pen, from the pull it exerted on a planet people could see, using a law written down 160 years earlier for apples and the Moon. Another astronomer, Adams, had also tried the calculation; his answer was about 12 degrees off and played no part in the discovery, and Challis had actually seen Neptune twice without recognising it. Neptune is called the first planet discovered through mathematics, and the phrase is exact.

One honesty note. Newton's law predicts the sky very well, not perfectly: with many bodies tugging, every prediction is a long calculation and carries some uncertainty, as the comet's story at the end of this chapter shows.

:::careful
> "Halley discovered Halley's comet."

Comets had been seen for thousands of years. What Halley did was new in a different way: he predicted that one of them would come back, in 1758, because a body on a closed orbit under Newton's law must return. He discovered the comet's *future*. The farmer who first spotted it on its return, on Christmas night 1758, was Johann Georg Palitzsch.
:::

:::check
Why do astronomers say that Neptune was the first planet discovered through mathematics, when Galle found it with a telescope?
???
Because the telescope was pointed where the mathematics said to look. Le Verrier calculated Neptune's position from the way Uranus was being pulled off course, using Newton's law of gravitation; Galle only had to confirm that something was there. Uranus itself had been seen through a telescope before anyone calculated where to look.
:::

## 5.6 The slow road to the Moon

:::scene Sriharikota, 14 July 2023

At 14:35 on 14 July 2023, an LVM3 rocket lifted off from the Second Launch Pad at Sriharikota, carrying Chandrayaan-3: a propulsion module, a lander called Vikram and, inside the lander, a small rover called Pragyan, about 3.9 tonnes in all. The Moon is about 384,000 km away, and the rocket had put the spacecraft into an orbit whose far point was planned at about 36,500 km. It would not reach the Moon for another three weeks, and it would get there by going round the Earth, again and again, in loops that grew.

Why loops? Why not point the rocket at the Moon and fire? Here is the picture to hold on to from this chapter. Once a rocket's engine stops, the spacecraft is simply falling, coasting on an orbit under gravity alone, exactly like the cannonball or the Moon. It needs no push to keep going (Chapter 2). An engine firing does not "push it outward"; it changes the spacecraft's speed along its path, and that moves it onto a *different* orbit. The whole art of flying to the Moon is choosing a sequence of orbits and the short burns that hop between them. ISRO's own updates record the sequence, and it is a lesson in Kepler.

The first burn came on 15 July, from the control centre at ISTRAC in Bengaluru, and raised the orbit to 173 km at its low point and 41,762 km at its high point. The second, on 17 July, gave 226 km by 41,603 km. A fourth, on 22 July, gave 233 km by 71,351 km, and one more followed on 25 July. Each burn was made near the low point, the perigee, to speed the craft up along its path. Look at the figure and notice where the effect appears: not at the perigee where the engine fired, but on the far side, half an orbit later. Going faster at the low point means the craft climbs higher before gravity turns it back, so the apogee rises. And because each new orbit is bigger, Kepler's third law says each takes longer: about 12.5 hours for the 15 July orbit, about 24 hours for the 22 July one. The craft's "year" was being stretched, burn by burn.

:::figwide c5_chandrayaan() | Chandrayaan-3's orbits round Earth, drawn to one scale from the heights ISRO published: the planned parking orbit, the orbit after the first burn on 15 July 2023, the much longer one after the fourth burn on 22 July, and the translunar orbit of 1 August reaching out almost to the Moon. All the loops touch at the same low point, where the engine fired; each firing lifted the far side. The 17 July orbit lies almost on top of the 15 July one and is left out. The Moon is drawn at its average distance.

On 1 August came the big one, the translunar injection: a burn at perigee that stretched the orbit to 288 km by 369,328 km, a loop reaching out to where the Moon would be. For four days the craft coasted outward, slowing as it climbed, just as Kepler's second law says a body does far from what it circles.

:::predict
Chandrayaan-3 arrives near the Moon on 5 August, falling towards it faster and faster. To be captured into orbit around the Moon, what must it do with its engine?
- Fire to speed up, so that it can catch the Moon
- Fire against its motion, to slow down
- Nothing: the Moon's gravity will capture it by itself
???
Fire against its motion, to slow down. Arriving at the Moon it is moving too fast for the Moon's weak pull to hold: left alone, it would swing past and fly back out, as the fast cannonball did. ISRO's engine fired backwards for 1835 seconds, about half an hour, starting at 19:12 on 5 August, and the craft dropped into an orbit round the Moon of 164 km by 18,074 km.
:::

That first lunar orbit was itself a long loop, taking about 28 hours to go round. Then the process ran in reverse: burns on 6, 9 and 14 August shrank the far point step by step until, by 16 August, the orbit was 153 km by 163 km, nearly circular, a lap of about two hours. On 17 August the lander separated from the propulsion module, and two more burns brought it down to an orbit of 25 km by 134 km. From there, on 23 August, Vikram fired its engines against its fall one last time and touched down at 18:04 Indian time, near 69° south, in the Moon's south polar region. The landing site was named Shiv Shakti Point. Pragyan rolled down its ramp the next day.

Every stage of that journey is in this chapter: coasting with the engine off, burns at the low point that lift the far side of the loop, braking to be captured, and the hammer and feather of Chapter 4, since Vikram, Pragyan and every loose bolt inside fell round the Earth and then round the Moon together. ISRO's pages say what the spacecraft did at each step; they do not say why this route was chosen over a direct one, so we will not guess. You can fly the sequence yourself in [The Sky](#explore-sky) explorer at the front of the book, which has a mission tab built from these ISRO figures.

:::careful
> "The engine burns pushed Chandrayaan-3 outward, away from the Earth."

Each burn pushed the craft *along* its path, making it faster at the low point of its orbit. The effect showed up half an orbit later, on the far side, as a higher high point. Nothing pushed it outward; gravity did the turning, as it always does, and a faster start simply let it climb farther before gravity brought it round.
:::

:::check
Once the propulsion module was in its near-circular orbit of about 153 km by 163 km round the Moon, did it need to keep its engine running to stay there?
???
No. In orbit it was falling freely round the Moon, held in its curve by the Moon's gravity alone, like Newton's cannonball or the Moon round the Earth. An engine is needed only to change from one orbit to another. With no air at the Moon to drag on it, it could circle for a very long time with the engine off.
:::

## The comet that came back

:::scene Near Dresden, Christmas night 1758

:::case The comet that came back
Edmond Halley, the man who had paid to print Newton's *Principia*, predicted that a particular comet would return in 1758. He died in 1742. As 1758 approached, three people in Paris, Alexis Clairaut, Joseph Lalande and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, spent months on a calculation Halley had not done, working out step by step how the pulls of Jupiter and Saturn would alter the comet's path. They announced that it would pass closest to the Sun around the middle of April 1759, some months later than a simple return would suggest.

1. The comet was first spotted on 25 December 1758 by Johann Georg Palitzsch, a farmer and amateur astronomer near Dresden.
2. A comet spends almost all of its time far from the Sun, moving slowly, and races through its closest approach in a matter of weeks.
3. The Paris calculators included the pulls of Jupiter and Saturn, the two giant planets, and no others.
4. Newton's law gives an exact formula for the path of one body round the Sun, and no exact formula at all when a third body pulls as well.

A sceptic says: "Halley said 1758. It wasn't closest to the Sun until 1759. So Newton's law got it wrong, and the Paris sums were just luck." Was the comet obeying Newton's law? Why did the three calculators need months, and what would have happened to the prediction if they had ignored Jupiter and Saturn?
???
The comet obeyed Newton's law precisely; that is why the Paris prediction worked. The law says every mass pulls every other, so the comet is pulled not only by the Sun but by Jupiter and Saturn as it passes near them, and over decades in the outer dark, where it moves slowly, those tugs have time to shift its path and its timetable by months. Halley's prediction used the Sun alone and could only be rough. To do better you must follow the comet step by step, adding up the three pulls at each stage, and in 1758 that meant months of hand arithmetic shared among the three of them. Ignoring the two giant planets would have given a date well off the true one, so the sceptic has it backwards: the months of sums were not luck, they were the law being taken seriously. Palitzsch spotted the comet as it came in, on Christmas night, on its way to the closest approach that the calculators had placed in the spring. The comet carries Halley's name because he was the first to know it would come back.
:::

Yet the ship floats and the coin sinks, and the air does not crush you. The next chapter is about what pushes back.
