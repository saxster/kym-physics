Chapter 5 took gravity a long way from home: out to the Moon, round the Sun with a comet, and up the slow looping road that Chandrayaan-3 climbed. This chapter brings it back to the kitchen sink. Gravity pulls on everything with mass, and that includes two things you have spent your whole life ignoring because they are everywhere: the water in a glass, and the air in the room. Both are heavy. Both press on whatever they touch. And the way they press explains a surprising amount, from why a straw works to why a dam is fat at the bottom.

It also answers a puzzle that sounds like a trick. A ship of 50,000 tonnes is built from steel plates, and it floats. A ₹5 coin is steel too, and it sinks the moment it touches the water. Same material, opposite result. By the end of the chapter you will be able to say exactly why, and you will have floated a boat made of kitchen foil, loaded with coins, in a bucket.

## 6.1 Pressure

Press the flat end of a pencil into your palm, gently. Now turn it round and press the point in with exactly the same push. Same force; a completely different feeling. Your palm knows something a spring balance cannot measure: how much skin the push is spread over.

That is the whole idea of this section, and it is worth a number. Suppose an elephant of 4 tonnes stands on four feet, each about the size of a large dinner plate (we will call it 0.1 m², a made-up but reasonable figure). Its weight, about 39,000 N, is spread over 0.4 m². Now suppose a 60 kg person balances on the heel of one stiletto shoe, a heel about 1 cm² across (also a made-up figure). Her weight is about 590 N, spread over a hundredth of a hundredth of a square metre. Divide force by area in each case and the heel comes out pressing about sixty times harder on the floor than the elephant does. The elephant wins on force by a huge margin. The heel wins on force *per area*, and it is force per area that dents a wooden floor.

Force per area is called ==pressure==. Write it as pressure = force ÷ area, or *p* = *F*/*A*. The unit is the newton per square metre, which has its own name, the ==pascal== (Pa): 1 Pa is 1 N pushing on 1 m². A pascal is a very small pressure, so you will meet kilopascals (1 kPa = 1,000 Pa) and, later in this chapter, numbers in the hundred thousands.

:::eq
**pressure = force ÷ area**, or *p* = *F* ÷ *A*
:::

:::note Pressure
Force divided by the area it is spread over: *p* = *F*/*A*. The same force gives a small pressure on a big area and a huge pressure on a tiny one.
:::

:::note Pascal (Pa)
The unit of pressure. 1 Pa = 1 N/m². 1 kPa = 1,000 Pa.
:::

:::predict
A 1 kg packet of atta lies flat on the kitchen table. You stand it up on its narrow end. Compared with lying flat, the packet standing on its end pushes on the table with
- more force and more pressure
- the same force but more pressure
- the same force and the same pressure
???
The same force but more pressure. The packet's weight has not changed: about 9.8 N either way. But standing on its end it rests on a much smaller patch of table. If the packet is about 20 cm by 12 cm by 5 cm, the flat face is 240 cm² and the end is 60 cm², four times smaller. Same force, four times the pressure: about 400 Pa lying flat, about 1,600 Pa standing up.
:::

:::fig c6_pressure() | The same packet, two ways up. Its weight is the same 9.8 N in both positions, so the force on the table is the same. Spread over the small end the pressure is four times what it is spread over the big face. On the right, a box under water: a fluid presses on every face, at right angles to each one.

So far the pushing has been done by solids: a heel, a packet. Now put the pressing thing in a glass. Water and air have no shape of their own. Tilt the glass and the water slides; a solid would stay put. Scientists call anything that flows like this a ==fluid==: liquids and gases both. A fluid cannot resist a sideways push, so it cannot lean on one wall and leave the others alone. At rest, it presses on *every* surface it touches, and always at right angles to that surface. The air in a bicycle tyre pushes outward on every part of the tube. The water in a swimming pool pushes on your chest, your back, the top of your head and the soles of your feet, all at once.

:::note Fluid
A substance that flows because it cannot resist a sideways (shearing) push: liquids and gases. A fluid at rest pushes on every surface it touches, at right angles to it.
:::

Where does a fluid's push come from? From its molecules. In water and in air the molecules are forever moving, and the ones next to a surface keep colliding with it and bouncing off. Each collision is a tiny shove. Count the shoves landing on one square metre each second and you have the pressure. That is why the fluid pushes in every direction: the molecules fly in every direction, so whatever you dip into the fluid gets peppered from all sides.

One more thing to notice, because it will keep you out of trouble later. Pressure has no direction of its own. The number is the same whichever way you turn your measuring patch. The *force* that pressure produces does have a direction: it is always perpendicular to the surface it pushes on. Pressure is the "how hard per square metre"; the surface decides "which way".

:::careful
> "Pressure pushes downward."

In a pile of books it does. In a fluid it does not. Water at a given depth pushes sideways on the wall of the tank, upward on the underside of a boat and downward on the floor of the tank, all with the same pressure. If water only pushed down, the side holes in a leaking bucket would not spurt, and nothing would ever float. Most of this chapter depends on the sideways and upward pushes.
:::

:::five Pressure
? What does it measure?
How concentrated a force is over a surface: the perpendicular force divided by the area it presses on (*p* = *F*/*A*), measured in pascals (1 Pa = 1 N/m²).
? Why is it called this?
From the Latin *pressura*, a pressing or squeezing, from *premere*, to press. In everyday speech people use *force* and *pressure* as synonyms, which obscures the crucial part: the area.
? What problem did it solve?
Force alone cannot explain why a blunt knife squashes a tomato while a sharp knife slices it under the same push, why an elephant dents a wooden floor less than a stiletto heel, or why pump-makers in Florence could never lift water higher than 10.3 metres.
? Why does it work?
Because materials yield or punctured skins give way when the local force on each tiny patch of surface exceeds what its bonds can hold, and in a fluid at rest, moving molecules bombard every surface from all sides with a push proportional to depth (*p* = *ρgh*).
? How does it help?
It explains how the ocean of air holds up 76 cm of mercury in Torricelli's barometer, why a postcard stays on an upturned glass of water, why dams are built thick at the base, and how hydraulic brakes multiply force.
?? How do we know?
Florin Périer carried Torricelli's mercury tube up the Puy de Dôme on 19 September 1648 and watched the column drop by about 8 cm as a tenth of the atmosphere was left below his feet, while an identical tube down in Clermont stayed steady all day.
?? What it isn't
It is not a directed vector that points only downward, and low pressure never "sucks" or pulls: fluids only push, and things move toward low pressure because higher pressure on the other side pushes them there.
?? Where does it stop working?
The smooth continuum picture of pressure works down to microscopic scales and breaks down only in an ultra-high vacuum where single gas molecules hit a wall miles apart rather than as a steady rain.
:::

Which end made the deeper dent, and which end did you push harder with? (Your hand may give the answer away: most people cannot help easing off when the point goes in.)
:::

:::check
A sharp knife slices a tomato with a gentle push. A blunt knife, pushed just as hard, squashes it. Explain the difference using pressure.
???
The force is the same, but the sharp edge meets the tomato's skin along a far narrower strip, so the same force is spread over a much smaller area. Force ÷ area is much larger, and the skin gives way. The blunt edge spreads the same force over a wider strip; the pressure is too low to cut, so the tomato squashes instead.
:::

## 6.2 The ocean of air

:::scene Florence, 1643

In the 1640s, pump-makers in Italy had a problem they could not solve. A pump that drew water up a pipe worked beautifully up to a height of about ten metres, and then stopped. Build the pipe taller and the water simply would not follow. Nobody knew why. Evangelista Torricelli, a mathematician in Florence, turned to mercury. Mercury is far denser than water; if the ten-metre limit had anything to do with weight, a column of mercury should stop much lower.

He filled a glass tube, closed at one end, with mercury, put his finger over the open end, turned it upside down into a dish of mercury, and took his finger away. The mercury in the tube dropped, and then stopped, standing about 76 cm above the level in the dish. Above it, at the top of the sealed tube, was a space with almost nothing in it. On 11 June 1644 he wrote to his friend Michelangelo Ricci and gave the reason in one line: *we live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air*.

Here is the idea in that sentence. Air has weight. Not much per litre, but the atmosphere is many kilometres deep, and all of it is sitting on you. Picture a column of air one square centimetre across, standing on the back of your hand and reaching to the top of the atmosphere. It contains about 1.03 kg of air and weighs about 10 N: roughly the weight of a one-litre bottle of water, on every square centimetre. Scale that up to a square metre and the air above it weighs about 10 tonnes. That weight, divided by the area it stands on, is ==atmospheric pressure==: about 101,000 Pa at sea level, which physicists round to 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and call one atmosphere.

:::note Atmospheric pressure
The pressure of the air around us, caused by the weight of all the air above. At sea level it is about 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa, roughly 10 N on every square centimetre. It falls as you climb.
:::

Now go back to Torricelli's dish. The air presses down on the open surface of the mercury with 101,000 Pa. Inside the tube there is almost no air, so almost nothing presses down on the top of the mercury column. The air outside therefore pushes mercury up the tube until the weight of the raised column, spread over the tube's cross-section, pushes back with the same pressure. For mercury, which is 13.6 times as dense as water, that balance comes at a column about 76 cm tall. For water it would come at 0.76 m × 13.6 ≈ 10.3 m. And that is the pump-makers' ten metres. A pump never pulled water up. It only thinned the air above the water in the pipe, and the atmosphere outside did the lifting. The atmosphere can lift a column of water 10.3 m tall and not a centimetre more.

Torricelli's tube is a ==barometer==: an instrument that measures the pressure of the air. The column rises and falls a little from day to day as the weather changes, because a "high" on the weather map means a little more air overhead and a "low" a little less.

:::note Barometer
An instrument that measures atmospheric pressure. In Torricelli's, the height of a mercury column shows how hard the air is pressing on the mercury in the dish.
:::

:::fig c6_barometer() | Torricelli's barometer, and the water version it replaced, drawn to the same scale. Air pressing on the open dish holds up 76 cm of mercury. To hold up water, which is 13.6 times less dense, the air needs a column of about 10.3 m, as tall as a three-storey building with floors 3 m apart.

If the column really is held up by the weight of the air above, then there is an obvious test. Climb a mountain. Up there, less air is above you, so the air should press less and the column should be shorter. Blaise Pascal, in France, saw this and arranged the experiment.

:::predict
Pascal's brother-in-law carries a mercury barometer from the town of Clermont to the top of the Puy de Dôme, a mountain nearby. At the summit, compared with the town, the mercury column will stand
- taller, because the air is thinner and lets the mercury rise
- shorter, because there is less air above pressing on the dish
- exactly the same, because a column of mercury weighs the same wherever it is
???
Shorter. At the top of the Puy de Dôme the mercury stood about 3 inches (about 8 cm) lower than it had in the town. The column is held up by the air pressing down on the dish; take some of that air away by climbing above it, and it holds up less.
:::

:::evidence Up the Puy de Dôme | 19 September 1648
Pascal was too ill to make the climb, so his brother-in-law Florin Périer did it for him. Périer set up two mercury tubes in Clermont and left one there, in the care of a priest who watched it all day. He carried the other up the mountain, read it at the summit, and read it again on the way down. At the top the mercury stood about 8 cm lower than in the town; the tube left behind never changed. That second tube is the quiet hero of the story. Without it, someone could argue that the weather had shifted while Périer climbed. With it, the only thing that differed between the two readings was the height, and the only thing height changes is the amount of air overhead. A drop of 8 cm of mercury is a pressure drop of about 10,700 Pa, about a tenth of an atmosphere: a tenth of the ocean of air was below Périer's feet.
:::

A fair question now: if ten tonnes of air press on every square metre, why are you not crushed? Because the air presses on far more than the top of your head. It presses on every surface in every direction, and so does the air inside your lungs and the fluid inside your body. The pushes balance, so you feel nothing. You notice air pressure only where it is *unbalanced*, which is why your ears pop in a fast lift: the air outside your eardrum has changed and the air inside has not caught up.

:::careful
> "The empty space at the top of Torricelli's tube sucks the mercury up and holds it there."

Empty space cannot pull on anything; there is nothing in it to do the pulling. The mercury is *pushed* up, by the weight of the air pressing down on the open dish. Périer's climb is the proof: emptiness at the top of the tube was the same on the mountain, yet the column fell. What changed was the amount of air pushing from outside.
:::

:::recall 4
In Chapter 4 you saw that a hammer and a feather fell together on the Moon, where there is no air. Suppose an astronaut set up Torricelli's barometer there. Would the mercury be pulled down by gravity, and how tall would the column be?
???
Gravity on the Moon is real (the hammer fell), so the mercury would be pulled down into the dish like anything else. But with no air there is nothing to press on the mercury in the dish, so nothing pushes any of it up the tube. The column would drain to the level of the dish: a reading of zero. Gravity does not need air, and the barometer measures the air, not gravity.
:::

:::story ocean-of-air

:::check
Torricelli's mercury column is about 76 cm tall. How tall would the column be if he had used water instead, and why?
???
About 10.3 m. The air can only hold up a column whose weight per area matches the air's pressure. Water is 13.6 times less dense than mercury, so it takes 13.6 times the height to weigh the same: 0.76 m × 13.6 ≈ 10.3 m. That is also why pumps of Torricelli's day could not lift water more than about 10 m.
:::

## 6.3 Nothing sucks

:::scene Regensburg, 1654

Otto von Guericke, from Magdeburg, had an air pump. In 1654, or a few years later (the accounts disagree), he fitted two copper hemispheres together to make a hollow ball, pumped the air out of it, and hitched a team of horses to each side. The horses heaved. The two halves would not come apart. Let the air back in, and the same two halves come apart with no effort at all. How many horses were there? One letter says twelve, popular accounts say sixteen, one says thirty, so we will leave it at "teams of horses".

Ask almost anyone what held the hemispheres together and they will say the vacuum sucked them. It is one of the most natural wrong sentences in physics, and it is worth taking apart, because there is a true sentence underneath it that explains far more.

Think about what was on each side of the copper. Outside: ordinary air, pressing inward on every square centimetre of the ball with about 10 N, as it presses on everything. Inside: almost nothing. Before the pump ran, the air inside pushed outward exactly as hard as the air outside pushed in, and the two halves could be lifted apart with a finger. The pump removed the inside push and left the outside push alone. The horses were not fighting a vacuum. They were fighting the weight of the atmosphere, which was pressing the two halves together from outside with nothing left inside to resist it.

That is the rule for everything that looks like sucking. Air never pulls. A fluid's pressure comes from molecules hitting a surface, and a hit can only push. Whenever something seems to be sucked, there is a place where the air pressure is lower, and the higher pressure somewhere else is doing the pushing. Engineers even have two words for this. A tyre gauge reads how far the pressure is *above* the atmosphere's: that is ==gauge pressure==, and it is the number printed on the side of a tyre. The real total, air plus everything, is the absolute pressure. A gauge can read "negative" when a space holds less than atmospheric pressure, but for air the absolute pressure never goes below zero, because zero means nothing is left to push.

:::note Gauge pressure
Pressure measured relative to the atmosphere's: positive above it, negative below it. The absolute pressure is the gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure, and it cannot go below zero.
:::

:::predict
You fill a glass to the brim with water, lay a wet postcard flat over the top so that it seals the rim, hold the card in place, and turn the whole thing upside down over the sink. Then you take your hand away from the card.
- The card falls and the water comes out
- The card stays and the water stays in the glass
- The card stays but the water trickles out round its edges
???
The card stays and the water stays. The glass is a tiny Torricelli tube, upside down. Air presses up on the underside of the card with about 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa. For the water to fall out, something would have to take its place at the top of the glass, and nothing can, because the card seals the rim and no air can get in. A 10 cm column of water pushes down on the card with only about 980 Pa, under 1% of what the air pushes up with. The air can hold up 10.3 m of water; 10 cm is nothing to it. The card's one job is to keep the air out.
:::

:::fig c6_card() | Left: the upside-down glass. Air pushes up on the card with about 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa; the 10 cm of water above the card adds only about 1% to the push it feels from inside, so the pushes very nearly balance and the card stays. Right: a straw. Drawing air out of your mouth lowers the pressure there, and the air pressing on the surface of the drink pushes liquid up the straw.

A straw works the same way round. When you "suck", you lower the pressure inside your mouth and the top of the straw. Nothing pulls the drink. The air pressing down on the surface of the drink in the glass, at full atmospheric pressure, pushes liquid up into the lower-pressure straw. This is also why no straw can ever be longer than about 10 m, even for the strongest lungs in the world: a perfect vacuum in your mouth would leave the atmosphere to lift the drink, and the atmosphere's limit is 10.3 m of water.

The plastic hook on the bathroom wall, the one with the rubber cup, is a Magdeburg hemisphere for the price of a cup of chai. Pressing the cup flat pushes most of the air out from under it. The air in the room then presses the cup against the tile with about 10 N per square centimetre, and nothing inside pushes back. Days later the hook falls off, and the reason is never that the "suction wore out": air leaked in round the edge until the pushes balanced again.

:::careful
> "Sucking pulls the drink up the straw."

Nothing pulls. You lower the pressure in your mouth; the air pressing on the drink's surface pushes it up. The test is Torricelli's: however hard anyone sucks, no drink rises more than about 10 m, because that is as much water as the atmosphere can push up. If sucking were a pull, a strong enough suck could lift water any height at all.
:::

:::school
"Suction" is not in your IGCSE syllabus, and "upthrust" is not either. When an exam asks why the card stays on the glass or why a drink rises in a straw, write that the air pressure outside is greater and *pushes*. That sentence is always the one the examiner is looking for.
:::

:::home The card, the glass and the straw
Do this over the sink. Fill a glass right to the brim, lay a stiff card (a postcard or a square cut from a cereal box) over the top and press it down so that it is wet all round the rim and no air is trapped underneath. Hold the card flat with your palm, turn the glass upside down over the sink, wait a second, then slide your hand away. The card stays. Now tilt the glass until air can slip in at one edge, and watch what happens the instant it does. Second trick: dip a straw into a glass of water, put a fingertip over the top end, and lift the straw out. The water stays inside. Lift your finger and it falls. Your fingertip was keeping the air out of the top of the straw, exactly as the card kept it out of the glass.
:::

:::check

The air in the room then pressed the cup against the tile (about 10 N on every square centimetre of the cup) with nothing inside pushing back. ## 6.4 Deeper means more

Dive to the bottom of the deep end of a swimming pool and your ears tell you, sharply, that something has changed. The further down you go, the more it hurts. Nothing about the water has changed; there is simply more of it above you, and all of it is pressing.

Let us count the pressing. Mark out a square metre of pool floor and look at the water standing on it. If the pool is 1 m deep, the column above that square is 1 m³ of water, which has a mass of 1,000 kg and a weight of about 9,800 N. Spread over 1 m², that is 9,800 Pa of pressure from the water, on top of the 101,000 Pa the air was already pressing with at the surface. Make the pool 2 m deep and the column holds twice the water, so the water's pressure doubles to 19,600 Pa. The pattern is simple: the pressure from the weight of the water is the depth, times the water's density, times *g*. Physicists write it *p* = *ρgh*, where *ρ* (the Greek letter rho) is the density and *h* the depth.

Now notice what is *not* in that formula. The area of your square was 1 m². Make it 2 m² and the column above it holds twice the water, but the weight is spread over twice the area, and force ÷ area is unchanged. The size of the patch cancels out. So does the size of the pool.

:::predict
Two containers are filled with water to exactly the same depth, 2 m. One is a swimming pool 50 m long. The other is a vertical pipe 10 cm across. At the bottom, the water pressure is
- higher in the pool, because there is far more water
- higher in the pipe, because the water is squeezed into a narrow space
- the same in both
???
The same in both. The pressure at the bottom depends only on the depth and the density of the water: about 19,600 Pa from 2 m of water, on top of the air's 101,000 Pa. The pool holds thousands of times more water, but that water is spread over thousands of times more floor. The pipe's narrowness changes nothing either. Depth decides.
:::

:::fig c6_tank() | A tank of fresh water 1.5 m deep. The water's pressure grows in step with depth: 4.9 kPa at 0.5 m, 9.8 kPa at 1.0 m, 14.7 kPa at 1.5 m, all on top of the air's 101 kPa. Because pressure pushes in every direction, the holes in the side spurt, and the deepest hole spurts fastest. The jets are drawn schematically; the pressures are real.
:::live pressuretank

The sea is a little denser than fresh water, about 1,025 kg/m³ because of the salt, so each metre of it adds a little more. Work it out for 10 m of the Arabian Sea and you get about 100,000 Pa from the water alone, almost exactly one more atmosphere. That is a useful thing to remember: every 10 m of sea adds about one atmosphere. A diver at 10 m carries two atmospheres on every square centimetre of her body, one from the air above the sea and one from the water. At 20 m, three. At the bottom of the deep ocean, 4 km down, the water alone presses with about 40 million pascals, roughly 400 atmospheres.

The same arithmetic explains the shape of a dam. Take one from a physics textbook: a dam 500 m wide holding back a reservoir 80 m deep. Near the surface the water barely pushes on the wall. At the bottom it pushes with 80 × 1,000 × 9.8 ≈ 784,000 Pa, nearly eight atmospheres. Average the push over the wall's height and it comes to 392,000 Pa; multiply by the wall's area, 80 m by 500 m, and the water pushes on the dam with a total force of about 1.6 × 10¹⁰ N. That is enormous, and it is why dams are thick at the bottom, where the pressure is greatest, and thin at the top, where it is small.

But here is the number that surprises engineers' visitors. The reservoir behind that dam might stretch back for kilometres: say 50 km² of water, 40 m deep on average. All of it together weighs about 2 × 10¹³ N. The dam holds back a force of 1.6 × 10¹⁰ N, less than one part in a thousand of the water's weight. The ground under the reservoir carries almost all of the weight. The dam only has to deal with the pressure at the wall, and that pressure is set by the depth at the wall and nothing else. A reservoir ten times longer, at the same depth, would push on the dam exactly as hard.

There is one more thing a fluid does with pressure. Squeeze a sealed bottle of water anywhere, and the extra pressure arrives everywhere inside it, undiminished, at every point of the water and every part of the walls. This is ==Pascal's principle==, and it is the trick behind a car's hydraulic brakes and the jack that lifts a truck: a small push on a small piston raises the pressure throughout the liquid, and the same pressure on a big piston becomes a big force.

:::note Pascal's principle
A change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is passed on undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of its container.
:::

:::careful
> "A bigger lake pushes harder on the dam."

Only the depth at the dam matters, together with the water's density. The pressure at any point comes from the weight of the water directly above that point, spread over the area under it, and the rest of the lake is standing on the lake bed, not on the dam. A small reservoir and a huge one at the same depth push on their dams with exactly the same pressure.
:::

:::school
Your IGCSE syllabus writes the rule as Δ*p* = *ρg*Δ*h*. The Δ (delta) means "change in": go Δ*h* deeper, and the pressure rises by *ρg*Δ*h*. It is the same statement as *p* = *ρgh* measured from the surface.
:::

:::eq
Δ*p* = *ρ g* Δ*h*
:::

:::check
A river is in flood, and water is seeping under the levee that protects a town. Engineers stack sandbags in a ring round the leak on the town side and let the leak fill the ring. The small puddle inside the ring stops the leak. How can a little water hold back a whole river?
???
Only depth matters. Water rises inside the ring until its surface is level with the river's. Then the pressure at the leak from the water in the ring equals the pressure from the river at the same depth, the pushes balance, and the leak stops flowing. The river's enormous width is irrelevant, exactly as the length of a reservoir is irrelevant to its dam.
:::

## 6.5 Why ships float

:::scene Syracuse, c. 250 BCE

You may know the story. A king suspects that his goldsmith has mixed silver into a golden crown. Archimedes puzzles over it in the public bath, notices the water rise as he climbs in, shouts "Eureka!" and runs home through the streets with no clothes on. It is a wonderful story, and historians treat it as a legend. It was first written down by Vitruvius about two centuries after Archimedes lived, and nothing earlier backs it up.

The best critic of the story was a 22-year-old. In 1586 the young Galileo Galilei wrote a short paper called *La Bilancetta*, "the little balance". He pointed out that measuring a crown by the water it displaces would need a precision no bowl and no eye could manage: the difference between gold and gold-with-silver would be a change in water level too small to see. Then he proposed a method that would work. Hang the crown from one arm of a balance and weigh it in air. Lower it into water and weigh it again. The water pushes up on it, so it weighs less, and the amount it loses tells you the volume, and therefore the density. That instrument is a hydrostatic balance, and it rests on a principle Archimedes really did state. The bath is legend. The physics is his.

What is the physics? Go back to the last section. Pressure grows with depth. Now put a brick under water. The water pushes on all six faces. The sideways pushes come in equal and opposite pairs, so they cancel. The push on the bottom face, which is deeper, is larger than the push on the top face. The two do not cancel; what is left over is a net force upward. That upward push is the ==buoyant force==, and every object in a fluid feels it: a floating boat, a sinking anchor, a fish hanging motionless in the middle.

:::note Buoyant force
The net upward push a fluid gives to any object in it, because the fluid's pressure on the object's underside is greater than on its top. Some school books call it upthrust.
:::

How big is it? Here is the argument Archimedes made. Lift the brick out and let water fill the brick-shaped hole. That water just sits there, neither rising nor sinking, so the water around it must be pushing it up with a force exactly equal to its weight. Put the brick back. The surrounding water does not know the difference; it pushes on the brick's faces exactly as it pushed on the water that was there. So the buoyant force on any object equals the weight of the fluid it pushes aside. That is ==Archimedes' principle==, and it has no exceptions.

:::note Archimedes' principle
The buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces (pushes aside).
:::

:::fig c6_buoyancy() | Left: why there is a buoyant force. Water pushes on every face of the block; the sideways pushes cancel, and the push on the deeper bottom face is larger than the push on the top, leaving a net force upward equal to the weight of the water the block displaces. Right: the same 10,000 tonnes of steel as a solid lump and as a hollow hull. The lump displaces little water and sinks; the hull displaces a great deal and floats, because most of what is inside it is air.
:::live buoyancy

Now the question that opened the chapter. Whether a thing floats depends on a comparison: the buoyant force (the weight of the water it pushes aside) against its own weight. If the water it displaces weighs more than it does, the net force is upward and it rises until it displaces just its own weight; then it floats. If the water it displaces weighs less than it does, it sinks. And that comparison turns out to be a comparison of ==density==: mass per volume. Something whose average density is less than the fluid's floats; denser sinks. Water's density is 1,000 kg/m³ (1 g per cm³). Steel's is about 7,800 kg/m³, nearly eight times water's. A ₹5 coin is a lump of steel with no air in it, so its average density is steel's, and it sinks.

:::note Density
Mass divided by volume: *ρ* = *m*/*V*, in kg/m³. Water is 1,000 kg/m³, sea water about 1,025, steel about 7,800, ice 920. An object floats in a fluid if its *average* density is less than the fluid's.
:::

A ship is a different shape of the same steel. Take a textbook ship whose steel has a mass of 1.00 × 10⁷ kg, ten thousand tonnes. Melted into one solid lump, that steel would fill about 1,280 m³ and displace 1,280 m³ of water, which weighs about 1.26 × 10⁷ N. The lump weighs 9.80 × 10⁷ N, nearly eight times more, so it sinks. Now roll the same steel into thin plates and weld them into a hull that encloses 100,000 m³, almost all of it air. Push that hull right under and it would displace 100,000 m³ of water, weighing 9.8 × 10⁸ N: ten times the ship's weight. The water pushes up far harder than the ship pushes down, so the ship rises until only a tenth of the hull is below the waterline, displacing exactly its own weight of water. It floats, with most of its height in the air. The 50,000-tonne ship in Mumbai harbour is the same story with bigger numbers: it must push aside 50,000 tonnes of sea water, about 48,800 m³ of it, and since steel is 7.6 times as dense as sea water, the hull must enclose several times its own volume of air to manage it. A ship floats because of the air inside it. The coin has none.

:::predict
A whole orange floats in a bowl of water. You peel it carefully and put the peeled orange back in. It
- floats higher, because it is lighter now
- sinks
- floats exactly as before
???
It sinks. The peel is riddled with tiny pockets of air, which make the whole orange's average density a little lower than water's. Take the peel away and what is left, the fruit, is a little denser than water. Peeling made the orange lighter, and that is exactly why "heavy things sink" is the wrong rule: the orange lost weight and sank. What matters is density, mass compared with volume, and the peeled orange has a worse ratio than the whole one.
:::

The same comparison explains the egg. An egg sinks in a glass of tap water. Stir salt into the water, spoon by spoon, and at some point the egg lifts off the bottom and floats. The egg has not changed at all. The salt dissolved into the water and made it denser, until the salt water displaced by the egg weighed more than the egg did. (How much salt? Enough: keep stirring it in until the egg rises. The exact amount depends on your egg.) Ice floats for the same reason, and the amount it floats *by* is a density ratio: ice is 920 kg/m³, fresh water 1,000, so a floating block of ice has 92% of its volume under the surface and 8% above. In sea water, at 1,025 kg/m³, only about 90% is submerged, which is why ships ride a little higher at sea than in a river.

:::evidence Weigh it in air, weigh it in water | Galileo's method, with a modern coin
A coin that might be silver has a mass of 8.630 g in air. Hung from a thread and lowered into water, the balance reads 7.800 g. The difference, 0.830 g, is the weight of water the coin pushed aside, and since water has a mass of 1 g per cm³, the coin's volume is 0.830 cm³. Its density is 8.630 g ÷ 0.830 cm³ ≈ 10.4 g/cm³, close to silver's. No overflowing bowl was needed and no shouting: the water did the measuring by pushing up on the coin by exactly the weight of the water it displaced. This is the hydrostatic balance the 22-year-old Galileo proposed in 1586, and it is Archimedes' principle doing honest work.
:::

:::careful
> "Heavy things sink. Light things float."

A 50,000-tonne ship floats and a coin you can hide in your fist sinks, so weight cannot be the rule. The rule is density, and it is *average* density that counts: the steel of the hull plus all the air inside it, taken together, average out lighter than water. The coin is steel all the way through. Compare densities, never weights.
:::

:::recall 3
In Chapter 3 you met the idea that a table cannot push up on a book, because it is "just in the way". Now a 50,000-tonne ship sits on the sea. Is the water pushing up on it? With how much force, and how does water, which has no stiffness at all, manage to push?
???
The water pushes up on the hull with a force exactly equal to the ship's weight, otherwise the ship would be sinking or rising. A table pushes by being squashed a tiny bit and springing back. Water pushes by its pressure: the molecules of water keep hitting the hull's underside, and because pressure grows with depth, the hits on the bottom of the hull add up to more than the hits on anything above. The push is the sum of countless small collisions. It is as real a force as the table's, and it points up.
:::

:::school
Some school books call the water's upward push "upthrust". The IGCSE syllabus itself does not use that word, and neither do most physicists; we say *buoyant force*. If you meet "upthrust" in a worksheet, it means the same thing: the weight of the fluid the object displaces.
:::

:::five Buoyancy (Archimedes' principle)
? What is it?
The net upward force a fluid exerts on any object immersed in it, equal in size to the weight of the fluid the object pushes aside (displaces).
? Why is it called this?
*Buoyancy* comes from a floating marker *buoy* (from Old French *boie* or Middle Dutch *boeye*), and the principle is named after Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 250 BCE), who proved it in his treatise *On Floating Bodies*.
? What problem did it solve?
People naturally assume that heavy things sink and light things float, which makes it a mystery how a 50,000-tonne steel ship floats in Mumbai harbour while a tiny steel ₹5 coin sinks straight to the bottom.
? Why does it work?
Because fluid pressure increases with depth (*p* = *ρgh*), the upward push of water on the deeper underside of a submerged hull or brick is always greater than the downward push on its shallower top, leaving a net upward force equal to the weight of the missing water.
? How does it help?
It tells you immediately whether something will float or sink by comparing its average density with the fluid's density, explains why ships ride higher in the salty Arabian Sea than in a river, and lets you measure the density of an irregular crown or coin by weighing it in air and in water.
?? How do we know?
Weigh any solid on a spring balance in air and then submerged in water while catching the overflow: the apparent weight lost by the solid matches the weight of the displaced water to the gram.
?? What it isn't
It is not a force that vanishes when an object sinks: a sunken stone at the bottom of a bucket still feels the full buoyant force upward, which is why boulders feel lighter to lift underwater than on land.
?? Where does it stop working?
Archimedes' principle requires gravity (so that pressure increases with depth); inside a freely falling spacecraft in orbit, pressure is uniform throughout a water blob and buoyancy disappears.
:::

## The egg that changed its mind

:::case The egg that changed its mind
Kym puts a raw egg into a tall glass of tap water. It sinks to the bottom and stays there.

1. She takes the egg out, stirs salt into the water a spoon at a time until no more will dissolve easily, and puts the egg back. Now it floats, with a little of its shell above the surface.
2. Very gently, she pours fresh tap water on top of the salt water, down the side of the glass so that the two barely mix. The egg sinks a little and then stops, hanging in the middle of the glass, well below the surface and well above the bottom.
3. Her cousin says: "The salt made the egg lighter, and the fresh water on top made it heavier again."

What really changed between the three stages, and why does the egg hang in the middle at stage 2 instead of floating at the top or sinking to the bottom?
???
The egg never changed. Its weight was the same in all three stages, and so was its volume. What changed was the water around it.

In plain tap water, the water the egg displaced weighed less than the egg, so the buoyant force was smaller than its weight and it sank. Dissolving salt made the water denser, so the same volume of displaced water weighed more. Once that weight passed the egg's weight, the net force pointed up and the egg rose until just enough of it was submerged to displace exactly its own weight.

Pouring fresh water gently on top made two layers: dense salt water below, lighter fresh water above. The egg is denser than the fresh water, so it sinks through that layer. It is less dense than the salt water, so it cannot sink into that. It settles at the boundary, with its lower part in salt water and its upper part in fresh, at the depth where the weight of the two kinds of water it displaces adds up to exactly its own weight. Nothing was made lighter or heavier; the egg found the one level where the water's push matched its weight. Buoyant force is the weight of the fluid displaced, and dissolving salt changes how much a litre of fluid weighs. That is the whole case.
:::
