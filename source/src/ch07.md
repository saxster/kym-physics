At the end of Chapter 6 the water behind a dam was pushing on the wall with sixteen thousand million newtons and achieving nothing. Open a gate at the bottom and everything changes. Water rushes down a pipe and hits the blades of a turbine, the turbine spins a generator, and somewhere far away a bulb lights. Something has been handed along that chain, from the high water to the glowing filament. Physicists call it energy, and they say a strange thing about it: it can be moved about and changed from one kind into another, but it can never be made and never destroyed.

Then the electricity bill arrives and says *units consumed*. If nothing is ever destroyed, what exactly was consumed? Hold on to that question. It is the chapter's puzzle, and answering it properly turns out to tell you what the word "energy" really means.

## 7.1 What is energy?

Here is a sentence from a thousand school books: "The ball rolls because it has energy." Now swap one word: "The ball rolls because it has *movability*." Both sentences feel like explanations. Neither is one. A name is a label, and a label is not a reason. Richard Feynman, the physicist whose way of teaching this book borrows from, called this the difference between knowing the name of a thing and knowing the thing. So before we use the word energy, we have to earn it. What is it, if saying it explains nothing by itself?

Feynman had a story for that, told with a child's toy blocks. Here it is in our own words, with marbles. A boy has 28 marbles, and his mother counts them every evening. Twenty-eight. Twenty-eight. Twenty-eight. One evening she counts 26. She looks under the sofa and finds two. Rule one: before you say that any have vanished, look harder. Another evening: 25, and the window is open, and three marbles are lying on the lawn outside. Rule two: things can leave the room, so count what has gone out as well as what is in. Another evening the marble box is locked and she cannot open it. But she knows that the empty box weighs 400 g and each marble 10 g (made-up numbers), so she weighs the box: 480 g. Eight marbles inside, by arithmetic. Rule three: you can count a thing you cannot see if you have a formula for it. Another evening the boy's bath water is too murky to see through, but she knows the water rises a quarter of a centimetre for every marble dropped in, so she reads the level. More counting by formula. By now the mother rarely sees a marble at all. She sees a number on a scale, a mark on a bath, a count on the lawn, adds them up, and the total is always 28.

Now the twist that turns this into physics. Energy is that kind of total, and there are no marbles. There is nothing to see. There are only the formulas, one for each place energy can sit: a formula for a moving thing (its mass and speed), one for a raised thing (its mass and height), one for a stretched spring, one for warm stuff, one for fuel, one for light. Each formula gives a number, measured in a unit called the joule (you will meet it properly in the next section). Add up all the numbers for everything involved in some event, before and after, and the total is the same. That is the content of the law called ==conservation of energy==, and since it was first stated in the 1840s, nobody has caught it failing.

:::note Energy
A number we can calculate for every store that takes part in an event: motion, height, stretch, warmth, fuel, light and others, each with its own formula. Add them up and the total never changes.
:::

:::note Conservation of energy
The total energy of everything involved in any process stays constant. Energy moves between stores and changes form; it is never created or destroyed.
:::

What happens when the sum does appear to change? Exactly what happened to the mother. Either one of your formulas is wrong, or there is a place you have not yet counted. Physicists have been through this many times, and each time the missing marbles turned up in a store nobody had thought to measure. That is why the rule is so powerful. It is a promise that the books always balance, and a hunting licence for whatever is making them look unbalanced.

The places where energy can sit have names, and your school uses a particular list of them: kinetic (moving things), gravitational potential (raised things), chemical (fuel, food, batteries), elastic or strain (stretched and squashed things), nuclear, electrostatic, and internal or thermal (the warmth of stuff). Energy moves from one store to another in only a few ways: by a force pushing something along (physicists call that *doing work*), by an electric current, by heating, and by waves such as light and sound. This chapter is mostly about three stores, kinetic, gravitational and thermal, and about what happens when energy moves between them.

:::school
School calls the place where energy sits a *store*, and the way it moves from one place to another a *transfer*. The IGCSE list of stores is kinetic, gravitational potential, chemical, elastic (strain), nuclear, electrostatic and internal (thermal); transfers happen by forces (mechanical work done), by electrical currents (electrical work done), by heating, and by electromagnetic, sound and other waves. This chapter's "places" are exactly those stores.
:::

:::predict
A cup of hot chai is left on the table and goes cold. The chai had thermal energy and now has less. Which is true?
- The missing energy was destroyed when the chai cooled
- The missing energy is now in the table, the cup and the air of the room, which are all a tiny bit warmer
- The missing energy turned into coldness
???
It is in the table, the cup and the air. The chai's thermal energy moved, by heating, into everything around it. The room is so much bigger than the cup that its temperature barely rises, which is why you cannot feel where the energy went. But the books balance: what the chai gave out, the room took in, to the joule. Coldness is a lack of thermal energy, never a thing that energy turns into.
:::

:::fig c7_ledger() | An energy ledger for a 100 g ball dropped from 1.00 m, at four moments. Each column is one moment; the bars show how the same 0.98 J is shared between the gravitational store, the kinetic store and the thermal-and-sound store. After the first bounce the ball only reaches 0.64 m, and the missing 0.35 J has moved into warmth and sound. Every column adds to 0.98 J.

:::careful
> "Energy is a kind of fuel inside things. When it runs out, they stop."

Energy is a number, worked out from formulas, and the number for the whole system never runs out. A ball rolling across the floor does stop, but its energy is still there, spread as a little extra warmth through the floor, the ball and the air. The fuel picture gets one thing right: a store can empty. It gets the big thing wrong: whatever leaves one store turns up, in full, in another.
:::

:::five Conservation of energy
? What is it?
The law that the total energy of all the stores involved in any event stays exactly the same before and after: energy shifts between stores and never appears from nowhere or vanishes into nothing.
? Why is it called this?
*Energy* comes from Greek *energeia*, activity or operation (coined by Aristotle from *ergon*, work), and *conservation* comes from Latin *conservare*, to keep safe or preserve unbroken.
? What problem did it solve?
Before the 1840s, motion, heat, light, electricity and food seemed like separate worlds, and when a rolling ball stopped or a cup of chai cooled, its vigour seemed to disappear. Conservation of energy revealed that nothing disappeared: one currency was paying for all of them.
? Why does it work?
Because every transfer between stores has a strict exchange rate (1 N·m of mechanical work = 1 J of kinetic energy = 1 J of thermal energy), so whenever one store decreases by a number of joules, other stores increase by that exact same number.
? How does it help?
It lets you calculate the speed of a roller coaster at the bottom of a hill without tracking every curve on the way down, audit a power station's efficiency, and unmask any perpetual-motion machine as impossible before looking at its gears.
?? How do we know?
Julius Robert Mayer, James Prescott Joule (stirring water with paddle-wheels driven by falling weights in the 1840s) and generations of experiments since have checked the exchange rates across mechanical, electrical, chemical and nuclear stores and never found a missing joule.
?? What it isn't
It does not mean *useful* energy stays constant: friction and heating continually spread concentrated energy out into random molecular jiggling in the surroundings, which is why your electricity bill charges for "units consumed".
?? Where does it stop working?
In nuclear reactions where mass changes noticeably, you must include mass itself as an energy store via Einstein's *E* = *mc*²; with mass-energy included, the books balance everywhere from atoms to stars.
:::

:::story conservation

:::check
A friend says, "Energy is what makes things go." Try the swap test on that sentence: replace "energy" with a made-up word, and say whether the sentence still sounds like an explanation. What does that tell you?
???
"Movability is what makes things go" sounds exactly as convincing, and it says nothing at all. So the original sentence was a label, not an explanation. Energy explains things only when you use its formulas: the ball reaches 4.4 m/s at the bottom because the 0.98 J it had as height has become 0.98 J of motion, and ½*mv*² = 0.98 J gives 4.4 m/s. The numbers do the explaining; the name never does.
:::

## 7.2 mv or mv²?

:::scene Leiden, 1722

In the early 1700s the best minds in Europe were quarrelling about a quantity. A moving body carries something: the heavier and faster it is, the more it carries, and when it hits something it hands it over. The followers of Descartes said the right measure of this "force of motion" was mass times speed, *mv*. The followers of Leibniz said it was mass times speed *squared*, *mv*², which they called *vis viva*, "living force". The two sides argued for years, and the argument was partly about words. It was settled, as far as experiment can settle it, in Leiden, with a box of clay.

Willem 's Gravesande dropped brass balls into soft clay at different speeds and measured the dents. A ball arriving twice as fast made a dent four times as deep. Twice the speed, four times the effect: that is *mv*² behaviour, since doubling *v* doubles *mv* but quadruples *mv*².

:::scene Cirey, 1740

Eighteen years later, Émilie du Châtelet wrote 's Gravesande's result into her book *Institutions de physique*, and took Leibniz's side. She did not prove the case, and in a sense there was nothing to prove: both quantities are real, and both are conserved in their own way. The quarrel was about which deserved the name "force". Today neither does. *mv* is momentum, which you met in Chapter 3. *mv*² is, within a factor of ½, what we now call ==kinetic energy==, the energy a thing has because it is moving.

:::note Kinetic energy
The energy of a moving object: KE = ½*mv*², with *m* in kilograms and *v* in metres per second, giving joules. Double the speed and the kinetic energy becomes four times as large.
:::

Why squared? Here is the simplest honest reason, and it uses what you already know about falling. Drop a marble from 25 cm and it hits the floor at about 2.2 m/s. Drop it from 1 m, four times the height, and it hits at about 4.4 m/s. Four times the height, twice the speed: that is how free fall works, because falling four times as far takes only twice as long, and a falling thing's speed grows in step with the time it has been falling. Now think about energy. To lift the marble four times as high you did four times the work, so the raised marble had four times the energy to give. All of that energy becomes motion by the time it lands. So four times the energy of motion goes with twice the speed. Energy must go with the *square* of speed, and 's Gravesande's clay was saying exactly that.

:::predict
You drop a marble into a tray of flour, first from 25 cm and then from 1 m. From 1 m it hits the flour at twice the speed. Compared with the first dent, the second dent will be about
- twice as deep
- four times as deep
- eight times as deep
???
About four times as deep. The flour pushes back on the marble with a roughly steady force as it goes in, so the depth of the dent measures how much energy the marble brought with it, and twice the speed means four times the kinetic energy. Expect "roughly four times", since flour is not perfectly uniform and the resisting force is not perfectly steady.
:::

The exact formula is KE = ½*mv*². Check it with the marble. Suppose it has a mass of 20 g (0.020 kg). Lifted 1 m, it gained 0.020 × 9.8 × 1.0 = 0.196 J. Landing at 4.43 m/s it has ½ × 0.020 × 4.43² = 0.196 J of kinetic energy. The two numbers match, which is no accident: the ½ is exactly what makes "work done lifting" equal "energy of motion on landing".

The unit, the ==joule==, is a force of one newton pushing something along one metre: 1 J = 1 N·m. It is a small amount. A 30 kg parcel sliding along an airport conveyor at 0.5 m/s has ½ × 30 × 0.5² = 3.75 J of kinetic energy, which is why a worker can stop one with a hand and not get tired. The square is what makes speed dangerous. A car at 100 km/h has four times the kinetic energy it has at 50 km/h, and all of it has to go somewhere when the car stops.

:::note Joule (J)
The unit of energy and of work. One joule is the work done by a force of one newton moving something one metre along its direction: 1 J = 1 N·m.
:::

:::fig c7_dent() | 's Gravesande's test, with today's words. The ball on the right arrives at twice the speed of the one on the left. Its momentum, *mv*, is twice as large; its kinetic energy, ½*mv*², is four times as large; and so is the depth of the dent it makes in the clay.

:::careful
> "Twice as fast means twice the energy."

Twice the *momentum*, yes. Energy goes with speed squared, so twice as fast means four times the energy. The clay knew this in 1722, and it is why the difference between 50 km/h and 100 km/h is a difference of four, not two, in what a crash has to absorb.
:::

:::five Kinetic energy (½mv²)
? What does it measure?
The energy an object has because it is moving, equal to half its mass times the square of its speed (½*mv*²), measured in joules.
? Why is it called this?
From the Greek *kinetikos*, of motion (from *kinein*, to move). Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz called *mv*² *vis viva* ("living force") in 1686; William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and William Rankine introduced the modern term *kinetic energy* in the 1850s.
? What problem did it solve?
Seventeenth-century thinkers quarrelled over whether a moving body's "force of motion" was *mv* or *mv*². Willem 's Gravesande and Émilie du Châtelet showed that *mv* counts how long a force acts (momentum), while ½*mv*² counts how far a force pushes (work and dent depth).
? Why does it work?
A ball dropped from four times the height has four times the gravitational work (*mgh*) done on it, yet because distance in free fall grows as *t*², it lands at only twice the speed; for energy to balance work, motion energy must grow with the *square* of speed.
? How does it help?
It explains why a marble dropped from four times the height makes a dent four times as deep in flour or clay, and why a car at 100 km/h needs four times the braking distance of the same car at 50 km/h.
?? How do we know?
's Gravesande's brass balls dropped into soft clay in Leiden in 1722, and our own marble-into-atta test at home, show dent depth scaling with drop height (*v*²) rather than with impact speed (*v*).
?? What it isn't
It is not a vector with a direction like momentum (*mv*): two cars crashing head-on can have zero total momentum between them and a huge combined kinetic energy that crumples both bonnets.
?? Where does it stop working?
The formula ½*mv*² is the low-speed approximation to Einstein's relativistic energy and is accurate to better than 1% for every speed up to about 40,000 km/s (over a tenth of the speed of light).
:::

:::story vis-viva

:::home Marble into flour
Spread atta or maida about 3 cm deep in a shallow tray and smooth the top flat. Hold a marble 25 cm above the surface (measure with a ruler) and let it go. Push a toothpick gently into the dent until it touches the marble, mark the depth with your fingernail, lift the marble out with a spoon and measure the toothpick. Smooth the flour and repeat from 1 m. Predict first (see above), then compare the two depths. Do each height three times, because flour is lumpy and single tries are unreliable; use the average. Then try 50 cm and see whether your dents fit "depth goes with height".
:::

:::check
A scooter is going at 20 km/h. The rider speeds up to 60 km/h. By what factor has the scooter's kinetic energy increased?
???
Nine times. The speed has tripled, and kinetic energy goes with the square of speed: 3² = 9. Its momentum has only tripled.
:::

## 7.3 Stores and transfers

:::scene Mumbai, a monsoon afternoon

The lift is out of order, and you are climbing. Each floor is about 3 m higher than the last. By the fourth floor your legs have an opinion about this. In physics there is a precise word for what your legs are doing, and it is a word you already use for something else.

==Work==, in physics, is done when a force moves something along its own direction: work = force × distance moved along the force. Gravity pulls down on you with your weight. Suppose you have a mass of 40 kg; then that pull is 40 × 9.8 ≈ 392 N. To climb one floor you must push yourself up 3 m against it, so the work is 392 N × 3 m ≈ 1,176 J. Climb four floors and it is about 4,700 J. Notice what does *not* count. Standing on the landing holding your school bag out at arm's length is exhausting, and does no work on the bag at all: the force on the bag points up, and the bag does not move. Homework is also not work in this sense, however it feels.

:::note Work
Energy transferred by a force pushing or pulling something along its own direction: work = force × distance. A force that moves nothing does no work, however hard it pushes.
:::

Where did your 1,176 J per floor go? Into a store that belongs to you and the Earth together. Lift anything and you pull it apart from the Earth against gravity's attraction, and the energy you spend sits in that separation, ready to come back as motion the moment the thing falls. This is ==gravitational potential energy==, and the formula is as simple as the work that filled it: mass × *g* × height gained, written *mgh*. Strictly the energy belongs to the pair, you and the planet, and we will say that once and then speak loosely, as everyone does, of "the energy of the ball".

:::note Gravitational potential energy
The energy stored by lifting a mass *m* through a height *h* against gravity: PE = *mgh*. It belongs to the object and the Earth together, and only *changes* in it matter, so you may call any convenient level "zero".
:::

Now a point that is easy to miss and worth a great deal. The *mgh* you store depends only on how high you end up, never on the route. Climb the stairs, take a ramp, ride the lift: for the same floor, the same 1,176 J goes into the gravitational store. A zigzag path up a hill is easier because each step is gentler; the total height, and so the total energy stored, is unchanged.

Here is the other half. Let the energy come back. A marble released at the rim of a smooth bowl rolls down, speeds up, crosses the bottom at full speed, climbs the far side and comes to rest almost exactly at the rim again. On the way down, gravitational energy is becoming kinetic; on the way up, kinetic is becoming gravitational; the sum stays fixed. At the bottom, all the *mgh* has become ½*mv*², and setting the two equal gives the speed: *v* = √(2*gh*). From 1 m, that is 4.43 m/s, the number from the last section.

:::predict
Two marbles, a glass one and a steel one three times heavier, are released together from the rim of the same smooth bowl. At the bottom of the bowl, which is moving faster?
- The steel marble, because it had more energy to begin with
- The glass marble, because it is lighter and easier to get moving
- Both have the same speed
???
Both have the same speed. The steel marble did start with three times the energy (three times the mass, same height). But it also needs three times as much energy to reach any given speed, because kinetic energy is ½*mv*². The mass appears on both sides of *mgh* = ½*mv*² and cancels out, leaving *v* = √(2*gh*), which has no *m* in it. More energy, more mass, same speed. The hammer and feather on the Moon were saying the same thing.
:::

:::fig c7_track() | A marble on a smooth track, released from rest 1.0 m up. Where the track is 0.5 m high it moves at 3.1 m/s; at the bottom, 4.4 m/s; at the far bump, 0.5 m high again, 3.1 m/s again. The speeds come from *mgh* = ½*mv*² and do not depend on the marble's mass. With no friction, the marble would climb the far side back to exactly 1.0 m.
:::live energytrack

The figure's track is the idea behind every roller coaster. Energy is spent once, hauling the cars to the top of the first hill, and after that the ride is nothing but trading height for speed and speed for height. No later hill can be taller than the first, because the cars have only the gravitational energy they started with to turn into climbing. In the live version of this figure you will see three bars, kinetic, gravitational and thermal, that always add up to the same total however the marble moves, and a friction slider that starts moving energy into the third bar. That third bar is the next section.

:::recall 4
In Chapter 4 a hammer and a feather, dropped together on the Moon, landed together. Back on Earth, in a vacuum tube, a 1 kg ball and a 0.1 kg ball are dropped from 2 m and land together at the same speed. Does the heavier ball arrive with more kinetic energy, the same, or less, and how can that be if their speeds are equal?
???
Ten times more. Kinetic energy is ½*mv*²: same *v*, ten times the *m*, ten times the energy. There is no contradiction, because the heavier ball also started with ten times the gravitational energy (*mgh* with ten times the *m*). Same speed does not mean same energy; it means energy and mass grew in step. Equal falling speeds are a statement about *v*, and energy is about *mv*².
:::

:::school
IGCSE asks you to show energy transfers with flow diagrams (Core) and Sankey diagrams (Supplement). A Sankey diagram is an arrow that splits, with each branch's width showing how much energy went that way; the widths of all the branches add up to the width of the arrow that came in. That adding-up is conservation of energy drawn as a picture. You will meet one in Section 7.6.
:::

:::careful
> "I held my bag out for a whole minute and my arm ached, so I did a lot of work on it."

In physics, none. The force on the bag pointed up and the bag went nowhere, so force × distance is zero. Your muscles did spend energy: to hold a force they keep tightening and relaxing, and their chemical store ran down a little and became warmth in your arm. All of that energy went into you. None of it went into the bag.
:::

:::

:::check
Does the energy that goes into the gravitational store when you carry a 2 kg bag of rice up one floor depend on whether you take the stairs or the lift? On how fast you climb? On how high the floor is?
???
Only on the height (and on the mass and *g*): PE = *mgh*. Stairs, ramp or lift give the same answer for the same floor. Climbing faster changes how quickly the energy is transferred (that is power, Section 7.5), never how much.

## 7.4 Where does it go?

Bounce a tennis ball on the hard floor of the compound and listen. Thock. Thock. Thock. Each bounce is lower than the last, and the gaps between the sounds get shorter, until the ball is just buzzing against the ground and then lying still. If energy is never destroyed, the ball's energy has to be somewhere. Where?

:::predict
A ball dropped from 1.0 m bounces back to 0.64 m. Where is the energy that would have taken it the other 0.36 m?
- It was used up in the bounce
- It is in the ball, the floor and the air, as a little extra warmth and as the sound you heard
- It went back into the ground's gravity
???
In the ball, the floor and the air, as warmth and sound. The ball squashed and sprang back, and squashing is never a perfect spring: some of the ordered motion of the ball became disordered jiggling of the molecules of the rubber and the floor. That is thermal energy. A little more left as the sound wave you heard. The books balance: 0.64 of the energy came back as height, 0.36 went into warmth and sound.
:::

You can measure this with your phone. The free phyphox app has an acoustic stopwatch that starts and stops on loud sounds. Set it going and drop the ball. The time between two bounce sounds is the time the ball spent in the air for that bounce, up and down, and a ball that spends *t* seconds in the air reaches a height of *gt*²/8. Suppose your first gap is 0.60 s and the next is 0.50 s (your numbers will differ). Then the first bounce rose to 9.8 × 0.60²/8 ≈ 0.44 m and the second to 9.8 × 0.50²/8 ≈ 0.31 m. The second bounce kept 0.31/0.44 ≈ 69% of the first bounce's height, and since gravitational energy is *mgh*, 69% of its energy. The other 31% went into warmth and sound.

The thief here is friction, and friction's cousins: the rubbing of surfaces, the squashing and stretching of the ball, the push of the air. In Chapter 2 you met friction as the force that makes a rolling ball stop. Now you can say what it does with the energy. When a force like gravity transfers energy, it moves it from one tidy store to another: height to speed, speed to height, and back again, as often as you like. Friction is different. It turns the ordered motion of a whole object into the disordered motion of its molecules and its surroundings' molecules. We call that ==thermal energy==, and once energy is spread among the random jiggling of a billion billion molecules, there is no way to gather it back up and lift the ball with it. The energy is all still there. It has stopped being useful.

:::note Thermal energy
The energy of the random jiggling of the molecules inside stuff. Friction and squashing turn ordered motion into it. School calls this the internal (thermal) store.
:::

:::recall 2
Back in Chapter 2, people said a rolling ball stops because it "runs out of push". You learned that nothing was pushing it in the first place: friction was slowing it. Now a classmate says the ball stops because it "runs out of energy". Is that better?
???
A little, and still wrong. The ball's own store of kinetic energy does empty. But the energy has not run out of existence; friction moved it, joule for joule, into warmth in the ball, the floor and the air. "Runs out of push" imagined a push that was never there. "Runs out of energy" imagines a destruction that never happens. The honest sentence is: friction spread the ball's energy into the surroundings as thermal energy, where it can no longer move the ball.
:::

This is the pattern behind every machine. Engineers measure how much of the energy put into a device comes out in the form they wanted, and call the fraction its ==efficiency==: useful energy out ÷ total energy in. A coal-fired power station turns about 40% of the coal's chemical energy into electrical energy; the other 60% warms the air and the cooling water. A petrol engine manages about 30%. An electric motor is excellent, around 98%. An old-fashioned bulb with a glowing wire is dreadful: of the 60 W fed into it, about 5 W came out as light and the rest warmed the room, an efficiency of about 8%. These are typical figures, not exact ones; real machines vary. But notice what none of them is: 100%. Whenever energy is turned into a useful form, some always ends up spread out as thermal energy. That is the rule, and it is why the words "energy saving" make sense even though energy is conserved. We never run short of energy. We run short of energy in *useful* form.

:::note Efficiency
The fraction of the energy put into a device that comes out in the useful form: useful energy output ÷ total energy input, often given as a percentage.
:::

A Mumbai local arriving at a platform shows the same thing on a grand scale. All its kinetic energy has to go somewhere in the seconds of braking, and almost all of it goes into the brakes, the wheels and the rails as warmth. Nothing is destroyed. The motion of a whole trainload of people has become a quantity of warmth that nobody can use, drifting off into the evening air.

:::fig c7_bounce() | A ball dropped from 1.0 m, drawn as if each bounce kept 64% of the height before it: 1.0, 0.64, 0.41, 0.26 m. Under each peak, a bar shows the ball's energy at the top of that bounce split between the gravitational store and the warmth-and-sound store. The bars are all the same height: the total never changes, and the useful part drains into the useless part bounce by bounce.

:::careful
> "The ball stops bouncing because its energy ran out."

Its *useful* energy ran out. The energy itself went, bounce by bounce, into slightly warmer rubber, slightly warmer floor and a series of sounds, and it is all still there, spread far too thin to lift anything. "Lost" is a word to avoid in physics, and your IGCSE syllabus never uses it. Say "spread out into the surroundings as thermal energy", which is both true and exactly what happened.
:::

:::school
If a teacher or a textbook says energy is "lost" or "wasted", they mean it has spread out into the surroundings as thermal energy and is no longer useful. The IGCSE subject content never uses "lost" or "wasted"; it uses *useful energy output* and *total energy input*, and defines efficiency as the first divided by the second.
:::

:::home Listen to the bounce
Open phyphox and choose the acoustic stopwatch. Set it to record several intervals, hold a tennis ball or a cricket ball 1 m above a hard floor, keep quiet, and drop it. The app lists the times between the bounce sounds. For each gap *t*, the bounce height was 9.8 × *t*²/8 metres. Work out the first three heights and the fraction kept from each bounce to the next. Is the fraction roughly the same each time? Try a different ball and a different floor (a rug, a wooden board) and see which pairs keep the most energy. Then put your palm on the floor where the ball has been bouncing for a minute: can you feel where some of the energy went?
:::

:::check
An old-style bulb is fed 60 W and gives out 5 W of light. What is its efficiency, and where does the other 55 W go?
???
Efficiency = 5/60 ≈ 0.083, about 8%. The other 55 W goes out as thermal energy: the bulb and the air around it get warm. All 60 W leaves the bulb every second; only 5 W of it leaves as the light you wanted.
:::

## 7.5 Power and the bill

Look at the labels in the kitchen. The mixer-grinder might say 500 W. An LED bulb might say 10 W. The geyser in the bathroom might say 2000 W. None of these numbers is an amount of energy. They are *rates*: how many joules the device moves every second while it is on. That rate is ==power==, and its unit, the ==watt==, is one joule per second. A 500 W mixer moves 500 J of electrical energy into motion, noise and warmth every second it runs; in a minute, 30,000 J.

:::note Power
How fast energy is transferred: power = energy ÷ time, in watts.
:::

:::note Watt (W)
One joule per second. A kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 W.
:::

Power is about time; energy is not. Haul a television up four floors with a rope and pulley in two minutes, or carry it up the stairs in five, and you have done the same work either way, the same *mgh*. The pulley just did it at two and a half times the power. Your own climb from Section 7.3 gives a feel for the numbers. Climbing one floor stored about 1,176 J in the gravitational store. If it took you ten seconds, your power output was about 118 W. Engineers still sometimes quote power in horsepower, where 1 hp = 746 W; your climb was about 0.16 hp. Measure yours: time yourself up a flight of known height and divide.

Now the bill. The electricity company does not sell power; it sells energy, and it chooses a unit that fits a household. One ==kilowatt-hour== is the energy moved by 1 kW running for one hour: 1,000 J every second for 3,600 seconds, which is 3,600,000 J. The bill calls it a *unit*. Here is what one unit buys. A 10 W LED bulb for 100 hours. A 500 W mixer-grinder for 2 hours. A 2,000 W geyser for half an hour. A human being for about ten hours, because a person's body, even at rest, runs at roughly 100 W, all of it ending up as warmth. You are, for bookkeeping purposes, a 100 W heater that reads books.

:::note Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
The energy transferred by 1 kW in one hour: 3.6 × 10⁶ J. The "unit" on an electricity bill.
:::

That 100 W comes from food, and food energy is the same stuff as the energy on the bill. A packet lists its energy in kilocalories (sometimes written kcal, sometimes "Calories" with a capital C), and one kilocalorie is about 4,200 J. Do the division and one kilowatt-hour is about 860 kcal. A small biscuit might carry 30 kcal, which is about 125,000 J, or 35 watt-hours: enough to run a 10 W LED for three and a half hours. A day's food for a grown-up, about 2,000 kcal, is about 8.4 million joules spread over 86,400 seconds, which works out at a steady 97 W. There is the 100 W heater again, from the other side of the ledger.

:::fig c7_bill() | What one unit (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ) will run, and for how long: a 10 W LED, a 100 W person at rest, a 500 W mixer-grinder, a 2 kW geyser. The same energy, four different rates. The wattages are typical label values; read the ones in your home.

:::predict
The bill arrives and says 320 units consumed this month. Which of these is true about that energy?
- It was destroyed by the appliances in the flat
- It is all still in the flat somewhere, stored up
- It was delivered into the flat and has since left it as light, sound and, most of all, warmth into the surroundings
???
It was delivered and has since left, mostly as warmth. "Units consumed" measures energy *delivered* to the flat: 320 kWh, about 1.15 × 10⁹ J. Every joule of it is still in existence. The fan turned it into moving air, which rubbed itself to a stop and became warmth; the bulbs turned it into light, which was absorbed by the walls and became warmth; the geyser turned it into hot water, which went down the drain or cooled in the tank. The flat is not warmer, because the warmth leaked out into the Mumbai evening, where it is spread across a city and an ocean and is useless to anyone. "Consumed" is the bill's word for "delivered and then spread out". Nothing was destroyed, and that is the answer to the chapter's question.
:::

:::careful
> "A kilowatt-hour is the amount of power we used."

A kilowatt-hour is an amount of *energy*: a kilowatt (power) kept up for an hour (time), which is 3.6 million joules. The power at any moment is what the appliance labels say. You will also see "watts per hour" written in advertisements; it is a muddle. Watts are already "joules per second", so nothing needs dividing by hours.
:::

:::try Investigation 7.5
Walk round the flat and read the wattage label on five things that plug in. Guess how many hours each one runs in a day, multiply, and add up the watt-hours. Divide by 1,000 to get units. Then look at last month's bill. Which single appliance do you think accounts for most of the bill, and why does running time matter as much as wattage?
:::

:::check
Which transfers more energy in a day: a 2,000 W geyser switched on for 15 minutes, or a 10 W LED bulb left on for 24 hours?
???
The geyser. 2,000 W × 0.25 h = 500 Wh, half a unit. The LED: 10 W × 24 h = 240 Wh, about a quarter of a unit. The high-power devices that run for long stretches, geysers and air conditioners, decide most bills; a bulb left on matters far less than it feels like it should.
:::

## 7.6 Energy for a city

:::scene Mumbai, 2022

Stand on a terrace in Mumbai after dark and look at the lights. Every one of them is the end of a chain, and the chain almost always starts with something falling or something burning.

The burning chain is the common one. In a thermal power station, coal, oil or gas is burned to boil water into high-pressure steam (chemical store to thermal store). The steam pushes against the blades of a turbine and spins it (thermal to kinetic). The turbine spins a generator, which pushes current out along the wires (kinetic to electrical). At every step the books balance, and at every step some energy goes where nobody wanted it. The result, for a coal-fired station, is that about 40% of the coal's chemical energy leaves as electrical energy and about 60% leaves as warmth, through the chimney and through the cooling towers that let the leftover heat into the air. This is unavoidable for any station that works by burning something, and it is why power stations have cooling towers at all: they have an enormous amount of thermal energy to get rid of.

The falling chain is the one from the start of this chapter. Rain fills a reservoir in the hills; the water's gravitational store is filled by the Sun, which lifted the water as vapour in the first place. Let it fall through a pipe to a turbine far below, and *mgh* becomes kinetic energy of water, then of the turbine, then electrical energy from the generator, with no fire and no chimney.

How does Mumbai do it? Here is what one public document says, and only that. Tata Power's generating business, in a filing to Maharashtra's electricity regulator, listed 1,377 MW of generating capacity: 930 MW in three thermal units (one of 500 MW that can burn coal, oil or gas; one of 180 MW burning gas; one of 250 MW burning coal and oil) and 447 MW of hydro at three stations, Bhira (300 MW), Bhivpuri (75 MW) and Khopoli (72 MW). All of it is contracted to two companies that distribute electricity in the city, BEST and Tata Power's own distribution arm. A megawatt is a million watts, a *rate*: 1,377 MW running flat out for one hour would deliver 1,377,000 units.

:::predict
Of Tata Power's 1,377 MW, 447 MW is hydro, about a third of the capacity. Over the year 2021–22, what fraction of the electricity it actually generated came from the hydro stations?
- About a third, the same as the capacity share
- More than a third, because falling water is free and the hydro stations would be run as much as possible
- Less than a third
???
Less than a third: about 22%. The filing reports 5,512 million units from the thermal units that year and 1,596 million from hydro, and 1,596 out of 7,108 is 22.5%. Capacity is a maximum rate. How much energy a station actually delivers depends on how much of the year it runs near that rate, and the hydro stations ran well below their maximum for much of the year while the thermal units ran closer to theirs. Megawatts tell you what *could* be delivered; million units tell you what *was*.
:::

That distinction, between a rate and an amount, is the whole of Section 7.5 showing up in a city's accounts. The regulator's tables count energy in *million units*, MU, and one unit is the same kilowatt-hour that is on your bill. So 1,596 MU of hydro is 1.596 × 10⁹ kWh, or about 5.7 × 10¹⁵ J, in one year. How many homes is that? We have no figure for a Mumbai flat's use, so estimate: if a flat used 300 units a month, 3,600 a year, then 1,596 million units would run about 440,000 such flats for a year. Change the 300 and the answer changes with it; that is what an estimate is for.

Two honest limits on all this. First, these are one company's numbers for one year, from a filing covering 2021–22; the city's supply today may differ. Second, Mumbai is supplied by several companies, and they buy electricity from outside the city as well. BEST and Tata Power distribute in parts of the city, and a third licensee, Adani Electricity, distributes in the suburbs. What share of all Mumbai's electricity comes from coal, gas, water, sun or wind is a question these documents do not answer, and this book will not guess.

:::figwide c7_city() | Left: a Sankey diagram for a coal-fired station. The arrow's width is energy: of every 100 J of chemical energy in the coal, about 40 J leaves as electrical energy and about 60 J as thermal energy into the air and cooling water; the two branches add up to the arrow that came in. Right: Tata Power's generating capacity and its actual generation in 2021–22, from its regulatory filing. Hydro is a third of the capacity (a rate, in MW) but under a quarter of the energy (an amount, in million units).

For scale, the world used about 15.7 trillion units of electricity in the year 2005, which is 5.7 × 10¹⁹ J. Every one of those joules took the same journey: out of a store, through a chain of transfers, and finally into the warmth of the surroundings. Conservation of energy is why the chain never adds up to more than went in. Efficiency is why it always adds up to less useful energy than went in. Between them, those two facts are the physics of every power station ever built.

:::careful
> "We're told to conserve energy. But physics says energy is always conserved anyway, so why bother?"

Two different meanings of one word. The *law* of conservation says the total never changes. The *advice* to conserve says: do not turn useful, concentrated energy (coal, gas, the water behind a dam) into useless spread-out warmth faster than you need to. The law is about the total. The advice is about the useful part, which really does run down.
:::

:::check
A coal-fired power station is about 40% efficient. Does that mean 60% of the coal's energy was destroyed?
???
No. All of the coal's chemical energy is accounted for: about 40% left as electrical energy, and about 60% left as thermal energy, warming the air and the cooling water. Conservation holds exactly. Efficiency measures how much came out in the form we wanted, never how much survived.
:::

## The perpetual-motion machine

:::case The perpetual-motion machine
A video has been going round the class. A wheel studded with magnets spins on a stand, and a bulb wired to it glows. The man in the video says the wheel "runs on magnetism", that it has been turning for a year with no battery and no plug, and that for ₹4,999 he will sell you the plans to build one that powers your whole flat.

1. The video is 40 seconds long and loops.
2. The man says the bearings are "so smooth there is almost no friction".
3. He will sell the plans, but not a working machine.
4. He says the bulb is a 100 W bulb.

Using nothing but the bookkeeping rule from Section 7.1, say whether such a machine could light a 100 W bulb for a year with nothing going in. Then say whether it could even keep turning for a year with no bulb at all.
???
Lighting the bulb is impossible. A 100 W bulb moves 100 J every second out of the electrical store into light and warmth. For the books to balance, 100 J every second must be coming *out* of some other store: a battery emptying, fuel burning, water falling, something getting colder. Magnets sitting on a wheel are not a store that empties by being near a bulb; nothing about them changes from one hour to the next. So there is no store to pay 100 J a second from, and the bulb cannot be lit by the wheel. Either the video is edited, or something off-camera is feeding it.

Keeping turning with no bulb is also impossible, though it would take longer to fail. "Almost no friction" is not none. Every turn, the bearings and the air take a little of the wheel's kinetic energy and spread it as warmth, and no store is refilling it. Every real device has an efficiency below 100%, and a machine fed only by its own output is being fed less than it spends. It slows, and it stops. That is why the machine is not on sale, only the plans: a plan cannot be tested.

The rule to carry away: whenever anyone claims to get energy out, ask which store it is coming from. If there is no store emptying, there is no energy coming out. In nearly two centuries of careful counting, the books have never once failed to balance.
:::
