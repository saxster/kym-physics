In Chapter 7 you followed energy from store to store and found that none of it was ever used up. A bouncing ball lost height, bounce by bounce, and the energy went into the floor, the air and the ball itself as a little warmth that spread out and did not come back. Warmth is where energy ends up. This chapter is about that warmth: what it is, how it moves, and why your own skin is so easily fooled by it.

Here is the puzzle to carry. Tonight, leave a steel thali and a wooden chopping board side by side on the kitchen counter. In the morning, lay one hand on each. The steel will feel colder; it always does. Now ask the question a physicist asks: *is* it colder? A thermometer laid on each will have something to say about that, and the answer runs through a cannon in Munich, a bucket of bath water, a block of melting ice in Glasgow, and the matka on your own counter.

## 8.1 Hot and cold aren't substances

:::scene Munich, 1798

Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford, worked at the arsenal in Munich, among cannon. A cannon barrel starts as a solid metal casting, and the bore is drilled out with a slowly turning borer. Everyone knew the metal got hot. What struck Rumford was that it never stopped getting hot. Drill for an hour, and the metal was hot; drill for another hour, and it was just as hot again.

That was a problem for the best theory of the day. Most scientists then said heat was a fluid, called *caloric*, that sat inside things and flowed from hot to cold the way water runs downhill. Drilling, they said, squeezed caloric out of the metal, as wringing squeezes water from a cloth. But a cloth runs dry. Rumford fitted a blunt borer to a cannon barrel, sank the barrel in a box of water, and set the borer turning. No fire anywhere.

:::predict
If heat were a fluid stored inside the metal, what should happen as the boring goes on, hour after hour?
- The metal runs out of heat, and the water stops warming
- The water keeps warming for as long as the borer keeps turning
- The water cools, because the borer is scraping heat away
???
The water kept warming. After two and a half hours it boiled, and the metal showed no sign of running dry. Whatever the water was getting, the metal was not storing a fixed amount of it. Something else was supplying it, for as long as the borer turned.
:::

What the turning supplied was work. Something pushed the borer round, and the pushing warmed the water. Nearly fifty years later, in Manchester, James Joule measured how much. He let weights fall and turn a paddle wheel in a tub of water, and read a thermometer in the water before and after. So much falling made so much warming, every time he tried it. Heating water with a flame and churning it with a paddle did the same thing to the water. You cannot tell afterwards which happened.

:::fig c8_joule() | Joule's measurement, in outline. A falling weight turns a paddle in a tub of water, and the thermometer climbs. The same work always makes the same warming, which is why we can measure energy in joules whether it arrives by pushing or by heating.

So we drop the idea of a heat fluid. What an object has is ==internal energy==: the energy of its particles jiggling, spinning and vibrating. Chapter 7 called the store "thermal". Push on an object, rub it, or hold it near a flame, and that store grows. We use the word ==heat== for one thing only: energy *on the move* from a hotter thing to a colder one, because of the difference in their temperatures. Heat is a transfer, like a payment. The cannon had no heat inside it, any more than your bank account has payments inside it. It had energy, and the turning borer kept adding more.

:::note Internal energy
The total energy of all the particles in an object: their jiggling, spinning and vibrating. Also called thermal energy. It grows when you heat an object and when you do work on it.
:::

:::note Heat
Energy transferred from a hotter object to a colder one because of the temperature difference between them. Heat is energy moving; an object does not contain heat.
:::

Try the oldest version of Rumford's experiment. Rub a pencil eraser hard against your desk for ten seconds and touch it to your lip. It is warm. Nothing hot touched it. Your arm did work against friction, and that work ended up as internal energy in the rubber. The unit tells the story: the energy that warms one kilogram of water by one degree is 4186 joules, which is also what a kilocalorie is. The "calories" on a biscuit packet are kilocalories, so your food is measured in the same currency as Joule's falling weights.

:::story caloric

:::careful
> "When I open the fridge, the cold comes out into the kitchen."

Nothing comes out. The kitchen air is warmer than the inside of the fridge, so energy flows *in*, from the warm air to the cold shelves, and the air near the door loses some of its internal energy and feels cool on your legs. There is no cold substance. There is only energy, and it moves from hotter to colder.
:::

:::school
At school this topic is called *transfer of thermal energy*. IGCSE uses "heating" as the name of a process and "internal energy" for what an object holds. The old noun survives in one place: "specific heat capacity", which you meet in the next section.
:::

:::check
Sample A is warmed with a flame until it has gained 10 kJ. Sample B, identical, is stirred hard by a paddle until it has gained 10 kJ. Afterwards, can a thermometer tell you which was which?
???
No. Both samples have gained 10 kJ of internal energy, and their particles jiggle in the same way. Heating and doing work are two ways of adding energy; once added, the energy carries no label saying how it arrived.
:::

## 8.2 Heat vs temperature

A bucket of warm bath water sits on the bathroom floor at 40 °C. On the kitchen counter is a spoonful of water from the chai pan, at 90 °C. Which one has more "heat" in it? Most people answer "the spoon, obviously", and the question is a trap, because after Section 8.1 you know that neither has any heat in it. What each one has is internal energy, and that is a different question, with a surprising answer.

:::predict
The bucket (10 kg of water at 40 °C) and the spoonful (5 g at 90 °C) are both left in a room at 30 °C until they match it. Which gives out more energy to the room as it cools?
- The spoonful: it is far hotter
- The bucket: it holds far more water
- About the same: the spoon is hotter, the bucket is bigger, and the two balance
???
The bucket, by a very long way. The spoonful cools by 60 °C and gives out about 1,256 J. The bucket cools by only 10 °C and gives out about 418,600 J, roughly 333 times more. Hotter is not the same as having more energy to give.
:::

Here is the picture to keep. ==Temperature== is about the *average* particle: how fast, on average, each one is jiggling. A thermometer reads that average. Internal energy is the *total* over every particle in the object. The spoonful's few particles jiggle hard; the bucket's enormous crowd jiggle more gently, but there are so many of them that their total is far larger. When two objects touch, energy flows from the one whose particles jiggle faster on average to the one whose particles jiggle slower, until the averages match. Then the flow stops. It does not flow from the one with more energy; a bucket of lukewarm water warms a cold spoon, and a hot spoon warms a bucket, each by passing energy from faster particles to slower ones.

:::note Temperature
A measure of how fast, on average, the particles of an object are moving. It is what a thermometer reads. Energy flows from higher temperature to lower, whatever the sizes of the objects.
:::

:::fig c8_bucket_spoon() | The bucket at 40 °C and the spoonful at 90 °C, both cooling to a 30 °C room. The spoon's particles jiggle faster, so its temperature is higher. But the bucket has vastly more particles, and the energy it gives out is about 333 times greater. The bars are drawn to that ratio: the spoon's bar is a hair's width.

How much energy does it take to warm something? Three things decide it: how much stuff there is, how many degrees you want, and what the stuff is made of. Each kilogram of each substance needs its own amount of energy per degree, called its ==specific heat capacity==. Water needs 4186 J for each kilogram and each degree, which is one of the largest values of any common material. Aluminium needs 900, steel about 450, wood about 1700, glass about 840, and dry sand about 800. Multiply the three together and you have the energy:

:::note Specific heat capacity
The energy needed to warm one kilogram of a substance by one degree Celsius, in J/(kg·°C). Water: 4186. Aluminium: 900. Steel: about 450. Wood: about 1700. Sand: about 800.
:::

:::eq
energy = mass × specific heat capacity × temperature change
:::

:::work
The bucket: 10 kg × 4186 J/(kg·°C) × 10 °C = 418,600 J
The spoonful: 0.005 kg × 4186 J/(kg·°C) × 60 °C ≈ 1,256 J
418,600 ÷ 1,256 ≈ 333
:::

The same arithmetic explains why the sea stays cool while the beach burns your feet. Give one kilogram of sand and one kilogram of water the same sunshine, and the sand's temperature climbs about five times as far, because 4186 ÷ 800 is about 5.2. Water is slow to warm and slow to cool. That one number is behind the sea breeze at Marine Drive and, as you will see in Section 8.6, behind a three-hundred-year-old argument about the monsoon.

:::careful
> "A thermometer measures how much heat something has."

A thermometer measures the average jiggle of the particles touching it, which is temperature. It knows nothing about how many particles there are. The spoonful of chai water reads 90 °C and has about 1,256 J to give; the bucket reads 40 °C and has 418,600 J. To know the energy you need the mass and the material as well as the temperature.
:::

:::recall 7
The bucket of bath water is left overnight and is at room temperature by morning. Its 418,600 J have gone. Were they used up?
???
No. They spread into the room: into the air, the floor, the walls, and out through the windows. The room is warmer by an amount too small to notice, because the energy is now shared among a far larger crowd of particles. The total is unchanged; it has only spread out, which is the pattern Chapter 7 found everywhere.
:::

:::try Investigation 8.2
Fill one steel tumbler with dry sand and another with the same mass of water. Stand both in the sun on a windowsill and read a kitchen thermometer in each every five minutes. Predict first: which climbs faster, and by about how many times? Then watch them cool in the shade.
:::

:::school
IGCSE writes the specific heat capacity as c = ΔE/(mΔθ), with θ for temperature. It also describes a temperature rise as "an increase in the average kinetic energies of all of the particles". The word *average* is the whole of this section.
:::

:::check
A block needs 25 kJ to warm from 25 °C to 30 °C. How much does it need to go from 45 °C to 50 °C?
???
The same 25 kJ. The mass, the material and the size of the temperature change (5 °C) are the same, so the energy is the same. It does not matter where on the scale the five degrees sit.
:::

## 8.3 Why metal feels colder

Morning. The thali and the chopping board have sat on the counter all night, side by side, breathing the same air. You lay a hand on each. The steel is cold. The wood is, if anything, faintly warm. Everyone who has ever stepped from a bathroom's tiles onto a bedroom rug knows this feeling, and almost everyone draws the same conclusion from it.

:::predict
A kitchen thermometer is laid flat on the thali for a minute, then on the board for a minute. What does it read?
- Lower on the steel
- Lower on the wood
- The same on both
???
The same on both. Two objects left together in one room for a night end up at the room's temperature, both of them. Your hand reported something real, but it was not temperature.
:::

What your skin feels is the *rate* at which energy leaves it. Your hand is warmer than the room; the counter and everything on it are at the room's temperature, perhaps 30 °C. Energy flows from your hand into whatever you touch. The steel carries that energy away from the contact point quickly and keeps asking for more, so your skin loses energy fast, and fast loss is what "cold" means to your nerves. The wood takes energy slowly; the surface under your palm soon warms to nearly skin temperature, the flow dwindles, and your hand reports "not cold". Same temperature, two different speeds.

The passing of energy through a material by contact is called ==conduction==. Picture the surface where your palm meets the steel. On your side, skin particles jiggle fast; on the other side, steel particles jiggle more slowly. Where they touch, they collide, and in a collision the faster one tends to lose and the slower one to gain. Energy is handed across the boundary, then handed on, particle to particle, into the metal. In a metal the hand-over is especially quick, partly because metals are full of loose electrons that carry energy between the atoms. That is why the materials that conduct electricity well also conduct heat well, a connection Chapter 12 returns to.

:::note Conduction
The transfer of energy through a material, or between two touching materials, by particles colliding with their neighbours. No material moves; only the energy is passed on.
:::

:::fig c8_conduction() | Where a warm hand touches a cool surface, fast skin particles collide with slower surface particles and hand energy across. In steel (left) the energy is passed on quickly and the surface stays cool, so your hand keeps losing energy. In wood (right) it is passed on slowly, the surface warms, and the flow dies down. The scale underneath shows how quickly each material conducts.

How quickly depends on a number for each material, its ==thermal conductivity==. Aluminium scores about 220, ordinary steel about 80, stainless steel about 14, glass under 1, water 0.6, wood between 0.08 and 0.16, wool 0.04 and still air 0.023. A stainless thali conducts about a hundred times better than the board beside it. The flow also grows with the area in contact and with the temperature difference, and it shrinks as the material gets thicker. Put all of that together and you have the rule engineers use: energy per second = conductivity × area × temperature difference ÷ thickness.

:::note Thermal conductivity
How quickly a material passes energy along by conduction, in W/(m·K). Metals score in the tens and hundreds; wood, wool and air score well below one.
:::

The thali and the board can settle this for themselves. An ice cube is a thermometer that cannot be fooled: it melts only when energy arrives, and the faster the energy arrives, the faster it melts.

:::home Which plate melts the ice?
Take an aluminium kadhai or pan that has been sitting at room temperature (never one from the stove) and turn it upside down. Beside it put the wooden chopping board. Touch both: the metal feels colder. Now predict: on which one will an ice cube melt first? Put one cube from the freezer on each and watch. On aluminium the cube slumps into a puddle within a few minutes; a commercial version of this demonstration melts its cube in under about four minutes. On the wood it is still a cube long after. The "colder" plate melted it faster, because it fed energy into the ice faster. Melting a 10 g cube takes about 3,340 J whichever plate it sits on; the plate only decides how quickly that energy arrives.
:::

:::careful
> "Metal is colder than wood. You can feel it."

You can feel energy *leaving your hand* faster. A thermometer finds the same temperature on both. "Cold" is a report from your nerves about the rate of loss, and metal conducts your hand's energy away about a hundred times faster than wood does. Nor does the metal pull cold into you: energy only ever flows out of the warmer thing, which is your hand.
:::

:::school
IGCSE lists this under *2.3.1 Conduction*, and asks you to describe experiments that show which materials are good thermal conductors and which are good insulators. "Insulator" here means a poor conductor, which is the next section.
:::

:::check
A cook lifts the steel lid off a boiling pot with a cloth, then picks up the wooden spoon that has been standing in the same pot with bare fingers. Both have been in the same steam. Why does only the lid need a cloth?
???
Both are hot. The steel lid conducts energy into the cook's fingers quickly, so the skin heats fast and it hurts. The wooden spoon conducts about a hundred times more slowly: the handle's surface gives up its energy to the fingers slowly, and the handle is cooler to begin with because the wood could not carry the pot's energy along its length. The difference is the rate, as it was with the thali.
:::

## 8.4 Sweaters don't make heat

Mumbai gives you about one week a year that calls for a sweater, so the sweater lives in a cupboard and comes out smelling of naphthalene. When you pull it on, it feels warm within a minute. Where did the warmth come from? Ask a class of children and many will tell you the sweater makes heat, or that it keeps the cold out. Both sound right. Both are tested by the same experiment.

:::predict
Two ice cubes come out of the freezer. One is wrapped snugly in a woollen sweater; the other sits bare on a saucer beside it. Which melts first?
- The wrapped one: sweaters are warm
- The bare one
- They melt together: the room is the same for both
???
The bare one melts first. The wrapped one lasts far longer. If the sweater made heat it would melt its cube faster. It does the opposite, because what a sweater does is slow down the flow of energy, whichever way that energy is going.
:::

A sweater makes nothing. Your body does: it runs at about 37 °C and gives out energy all day. In the open, that energy conducts into the air next to your skin, and the warmed air drifts away and is replaced by cooler air, so the loss goes on and on. A sweater slows the loss. The wool fibres themselves are poor conductors, scoring about 0.04 on the scale in Section 8.3. More important is what sits between them: air. Still air is one of the worst conductors there is, at 0.023, more than five thousand times worse than aluminium. The trouble with air is that it does not stay still. Free air that is warmed rises and carries energy away with it, which is how a room's air moves energy from a heater to the ceiling. Wool, fur, feathers and the fibreglass in a building's walls all work the same trick: they trap air in pockets too small for it to flow. Trapped, the air is only a conductor, and a very poor one.

:::fig c8_sweater() | A sweater in cross-section, between skin at 37 °C and a cool room. Wool fibres trap air in pockets too small for the air to circulate, and trapped air conducts very badly. Energy still leaks out, but slowly. Wrapped round an ice cube, the same sweater slows energy leaking in.

Thickness counts too. Halve the thickness of a layer and energy flows through it twice as fast; double it and the flow halves. Two layers add their resistance, the way two sweaters do. A lecture course on the physics of energy works through a building wall with a 20-degree difference across it: 20 cm of bare concrete leaks about 5 GJ a day, and a sandwich of 3 cm of fibreglass between boards cuts that to about 160 MJ, roughly thirty times less. The wall, like the sweater, makes nothing. It slows a flow.

One more thing, because this book must not fool you. An answer key in one of the textbooks this chapter leans on says woollen clothes keep you warm because of their "large specific heat". That is a different quantity (the one from Section 8.2, about storing energy) and it is the wrong reason. Wool insulates because it traps air and conducts badly. A good book can still be wrong in a line, and the test is the ice cube, which does not read answer keys.

:::home The sweater and the ice
Put two ice cubes of the same size on two saucers. Wrap one saucer, cube and all, in a woollen sweater or a thick folded towel; leave the other bare. Predict how long each will take to melt. Check every fifteen minutes. The bare cube goes first. When the wrapped one is finally a puddle, ask: did the wool make heat, or slow it?
:::

:::careful
> "A sweater keeps the cold out."

Nothing comes in. Cold is not a substance (Section 8.1). Energy leaks *out* of you into cooler surroundings, and the sweater slows that leak. Your school book may say woollens "keep warmth close to the body"; read "keep" as "slow its escape", never "make".
:::

:::check
A Styrofoam box keeps ice cold for a picnic. Does the box make cold, keep cold in, or slow energy flowing in from outside? One such box, with walls 2.5 cm thick, sitting in 35 °C air, still lets about 3.4 kg of ice melt a day. What would make it lose less?
???
It slows energy flowing in: Styrofoam conducts at about 0.010, worse than wool, because it is mostly trapped air. The ice melts anyway, because the flow is slowed and never stopped. Thicker walls, a smaller box (less area), or a cooler place to stand it would all cut the flow. Nothing can "keep cold in", because cold is not a thing.
:::

## 8.5 Hidden heat

:::scene Glasgow, 1761

Joseph Black, who lectured at Glasgow, noticed something that everyone before him had walked past. Take a lump of ice that is already melting and warm it steadily. The thermometer does not rise. The ice goes on melting, you get more water, and the water stays at the ice's temperature until the last of the ice is gone. Only then does the thermometer begin to climb. Energy was flowing in the whole time. Where was it going? Black called it *latent* heat, from the Latin for hidden. He never properly published the idea; it survives in notes of his lectures.

:::predict
A steel tumbler holds ice and water, well stirred, with a kitchen thermometer in it. Plenty of ice is still floating. Over the next ten minutes, in a warm Mumbai kitchen, the reading will:
- Rise steadily, as the warm room heats the water
- Stay at 0 °C until the ice is gone, then rise
- Fall, as the ice cools the water further
???
It stays at 0 °C for as long as there is ice to melt, and only then starts to rise. Energy is pouring in from the kitchen the whole time. All of it is going into melting, and none into raising the temperature.
:::

Where does it go? In ice, the water particles hold one another in a fixed pattern. To melt, they must be pulled a little apart and set free to slide, against the attraction that held them. That costs energy, and while the energy is being spent on loosening the pattern it is not being spent on faster jiggling. The thermometer, which reads the jiggling, sees nothing. For water the price is 334 kJ for every kilogram, the ==latent heat== of melting. Compare that with Section 8.2: the same 334 kJ would warm a kilogram of liquid water from 0 °C to nearly 80 °C. Melting is expensive.

:::note Latent heat
The energy needed to change the state of one kilogram of a substance (solid to liquid, or liquid to gas) with no change in temperature. For water: 334 kJ/kg to melt, 2256 kJ/kg to boil at 100 °C.
:::

Boiling is far more expensive still. To turn a kilogram of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C takes 2256 kJ, almost seven times the cost of melting, because the particles must be torn fully apart from one another. The figure below follows one kilogram of ice from −20 °C to steam and draws every stretch to scale. Warming the ice takes 42 kJ; melting it, 334; warming the water from 0 °C to 100 °C, 419; boiling it, 2256. The two flat stretches are the hidden heat, and the boiling stretch is longer than everything else put together.

:::fig c8_heating_curve() | One kilogram of ice from −20 °C to steam, drawn to scale. The temperature climbs, pauses at 0 °C while 334 kJ go into melting, climbs again, and pauses at 100 °C while 2256 kJ go into boiling. The flat stretches are latent heat; on them, energy flows in and the thermometer does not move.

Hidden heat is only hidden, never lost. When steam condenses on a cold lid, the 2256 kJ for each kilogram come back out and warm the lid. When water freezes, the 334 kJ come back out. Fruit growers in cold places spray their orchards with water on frosty nights for exactly this reason: as the water freezes it gives out energy and keeps the fruit from falling far below 0 °C. In Mumbai you can watch the same accounting in reverse on a glass of iced water. Beads of water form on the outside as vapour from the humid air condenses on the cold glass. Condensing gives out energy, and that energy goes into the glass and melts the ice faster. The sweating glass is helping your ice disappear.

:::work
A glass of nimbu pani: 250 g at 20 °C, with three ice cubes of 6 g each at 0 °C.
Melting the ice: 0.018 kg × 334 kJ/kg ≈ 6.0 kJ
The drink has 0.25 kg × 4.186 kJ/(kg·°C) × 20 °C ≈ 20.9 kJ above 0 °C.
After melting, the 268 g of water share the remaining 14.9 kJ above 0 °C: about 13 °C.
:::

For what is going on among the particles when a solid melts or a liquid boils, *The Invisible World* has the pictures: [Chapter 3, on the states of matter](https://saxster.github.io/kym-chem/). The energy accounting here and the particle story there are two views of the same event.

:::careful
> "While the ice melts, the heat just disappears."

It is spent, and stored. It went into pulling the particles out of their fixed pattern, and it is held in the looser arrangement of the liquid. Freeze the water again and every joule comes back out. Nothing disappeared; it changed form, as energy always does.
:::

:::try Investigation 8.5
Stir ice and water in a steel tumbler with a kitchen thermometer in it, and read it every minute for twenty minutes. Plot the readings. Mark the minute the last sliver of ice vanishes. Where on your graph is Black's hidden heat?
:::

:::school
IGCSE puts this under *2.2.3 Melting, boiling and evaporation*. Evaporation is the part this section has not touched yet: water leaving as vapour well below 100 °C. It is where the next section begins.
:::

:::check
Ice at exactly 0 °C is dropped into water at exactly 0 °C in a perfectly insulated box. Does any ice melt, or any water freeze?
???
Neither, on balance. There is no temperature difference, so no energy flows from one to the other. Melting needs energy to arrive; freezing needs energy to leave. With nothing flowing, the amounts of ice and water stay as they are.
:::

## 8.6 The matka and the monsoon

Section 8.3 made a promise: nothing left in a room ends up colder than the room. Yet in May, the water in the matka on the counter is cooler than the kitchen air around it, and has been all afternoon. Ask why, and the usual answer is that clay is cold. You now know that cannot be right: clay left in a room is at the room's temperature, like steel and wood. Something is going on at the surface of that pot that was not going on with the thali.

:::predict
Fill two steel tumblers with tap water. Wrap one in a cloth soaked in water and the other in a dry cloth, and stand both in front of a fan for an hour. Can the wet-wrapped tumbler's water end up *colder than the room's air*?
- No. Moving air can only bring something down to the air's own temperature
- Yes, a little below the air temperature
- Yes, as cold as a fridge
???
Yes, below the air temperature, by an amount that depends on how damp the air already is. Moving air alone never cools anything below its own temperature; a fan on a dry tumbler does nothing. The wet cloth is the whole trick, and the reason is evaporation.
:::

Water does not have to boil to become vapour. At any temperature, the fastest particles at a liquid's surface are moving quickly enough to break free of their neighbours and leave. That is ==evaporation==, and it has a cost. The ones that leave are the fastest, so the average speed of the ones left behind falls, and a falling average speed is a falling temperature (Section 8.2). Each gram that escapes from warm water carries away about 2.4 kJ, slightly more than a gram boiled away at 100 °C takes. A matka is a leaky pot by design. Water seeps through the pores in the fired clay and evaporates from the outside, and every gram that leaves takes energy from the water inside. Cooling ten litres by 10 °C means evaporating about 170 g through the clay. Your body runs the same cooling system: a resting person giving out 120 W must evaporate about 3 g of sweat a minute to carry it away.

:::note Evaporation
Particles leaving the surface of a liquid as vapour, at any temperature below boiling. The fastest particles leave, so the liquid left behind cools.
:::

:::fig c8_matka() | A matka in cross-section. Water seeps through the pores of the clay and evaporates from the outer surface; every escaping particle carries energy away, so the water inside cools below the air around it. Damp air slows the escape, so the cooling is smaller in July than in May.

Why, then, does the matka work so poorly in July? Because the air around it is already full of water vapour. At a given temperature, vapour can build up in the air only to a certain amount; beyond that, as many particles return to the water as leave it, and evaporation stops. How close the air is to that limit is its ==relative humidity==: 100% means the limit has been reached. People say the air "holds" water like a sponge, but nothing is holding anything; it is a balance of particles leaving and particles returning. When the humidity is 81%, as it typically is on a Mumbai afternoon in July, the air is already close to the balance point, and the matka's water barely cools. Warm air has more room: the amount of vapour that can be present at 37 °C is about twice the amount at 25 °C.

:::note Relative humidity
How much water vapour the air has, compared with the most that can be present at that temperature, as a percentage. At 100% there is no net evaporation.
:::

There is a floor. An evaporating surface can only cool until the air right next to it is at the balance point; the temperature it reaches then is the ==wet-bulb temperature==, and no matka, sweat or wet cloth can go below it. In dry inland air at 38 °C and 20% humidity the floor is about 21 °C, nearly 17 degrees below the air, which is why a matka in a dry town makes water that is almost cold. Mumbai's damp sea air leaves far less room; how much less, in May and in July, is the case at the end of this chapter. Measured matkas drop their water by anywhere from 5 to 15 °C, depending on the humidity; one measurement in humid Kolkata managed 7 °C. A real pot never quite reaches the floor, because energy also leaks in from the room through the clay. The fan helps because it sweeps away the damp air next to the cloth and brings drier air to take its place, so evaporation keeps going. A fan on your skin feels cool for the same reason: the air is no cooler, and your sweat evaporates faster.

:::note Wet-bulb temperature
The lowest temperature that evaporation alone can bring a wet surface to, in air of a given temperature and humidity. Damp air gives a floor only a little below the air temperature; dry air gives a floor far below it.
:::

:::story matka

Evaporation moves energy through a wall of clay. Air moves energy across a whole city. Warm a patch of air and it expands, so it is less dense than the air around it, and the denser air pushes it upward, exactly as water pushes up a floating orange in Chapter 6. Cooler air flows in to take its place, warms in turn, and rises too: a loop. That loop is ==convection==, and you have already met its villain in this chapter; it is the reason free air is a poor insulator and trapped air a good one.

:::note Convection
The transfer of energy by the movement of a fluid. Warmer fluid is less dense and is pushed up by the denser fluid around it, so the fluid circulates in loops.
:::

:::recall 6
"Hot air rises because heat rises." Chapter 6 found a floating orange and a sinking peeled one. Use it to say what really pushes warm air up.
???
Heat does not rise; it is not a thing that can rise. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, and the denser air pushes it up, just as water pushes up anything less dense than itself. The orange floated for the same reason. Take away the surrounding cooler air and warm air would go nowhere.
:::

Now think of Mumbai on a May afternoon. Section 8.2 showed that a kilogram of sand warms about five times as fast as a kilogram of water. So by midday the land is hotter than the sea. The air over the land warms, is pushed up, and cooler air from the Arabian Sea flows in to replace it: the afternoon breeze on Marine Drive. At night the land cools faster than the sea, the loop turns the other way, and a gentle wind blows out to sea. Weather services call the pair the sea breeze and the land breeze.

:::fig c8_sea_breeze() | The sea breeze, by day. Sunshine warms the land about five times faster than the sea for the same energy, so the air over the land warms, is pushed up by the denser air around it, and cooler sea air flows in underneath. At night the land cools faster, and the loop reverses.

In 1686 Edmond Halley, whom you met in Chapter 5, proposed that the monsoon is this same loop at the scale of a continent: all of India heats up through the summer, the air over it rises, and moist air from the ocean flows in for months and brings the rain. Then the land cools, and the winds turn around. It is a beautiful idea, and it is the one most books still give. In 2021 Sulochana Gadgil and her colleagues argued that it fails several tests, and that the monsoon rains are better understood as a shift of the band of rain that circles the Earth near the equator. The argument is still going on. Nobody disputes the sea breeze; what is in dispute is whether the monsoon is a giant one. You are reading this during an unsettled scientific question, which is the normal condition of science. What is settled is that on a normal year the rains reach Mumbai around 11 June, that the instruments measuring them were standardised for India by the physicist Anna Mani, and that the first week of rain finishes the matka's season for the year.

:::home A matka without a matka
Fill two identical steel tumblers with water from the same tap. Wrap one in a cloth soaked in water and wrung out; wrap the other in a dry cloth. Stand both in front of a fan. Read a kitchen thermometer in each every ten minutes for an hour, and keep the wet cloth damp. Write down the room's temperature too. The wet tumbler ends up below the room; the dry one does not. Do it once in May and once in July, and compare how far below the room each one got.
:::

:::careful
> "The matka's water is cold because clay is cold."

Clay left in a room is at the room's temperature, like the thali and the board. The water is cooler because of what happens at the pot's surface: water seeps out and evaporates, and the escaping particles take energy with them. A glazed pot of the same clay, which cannot sweat, does nothing.
:::

:::school
IGCSE places evaporation under *2.2.3* and convection under *2.3.2 Convection*. Cambridge Lower Secondary calls what the matka does "evaporative cooling". When an exam asks why evaporation cools, name the escaping particles: the fastest ones leave, so the average energy of the rest falls.
:::

:::check
A fan blows air at the room's own temperature, 30 °C, over your damp skin, and you feel cool. Why, when the air is no cooler than before you switched it on?
???
Moving air itself cannot cool you below 30 °C. It sweeps away the damp air next to your skin and replaces it with drier air, so your sweat evaporates faster. Each gram that evaporates carries about 2.4 kJ out of your skin, and your skin's temperature drops below the air's. Switch off the fan and the damp air builds up again, evaporation slows, and the cooling fades.
:::

## May or July?

:::case May or July?
A cousin visiting from a dry inland town is baffled by Mumbai's matkas, which never make the water as cold as hers at home. "Mine drops the water by more than ten degrees," she says. "Yours barely does anything. When does it work best here?" You pull two typical afternoons out of the weather record for the airport at Santacruz:

- **May:** air 34.3 °C, relative humidity about 65%. The weather table gives the wet-bulb temperature as 28.7 °C.
- **July:** air 29.4 °C, relative humidity about 81%. Wet-bulb temperature 26.7 °C.
- Her town, for comparison: air 38 °C, humidity 20%, wet-bulb about 21 °C.

A perfect matka could bring its water down to the wet-bulb temperature and no lower; a real one stops a little above it.

Two questions. In which month does a Mumbai matka cool its water *further below the air*? And in which month is the water in it actually *colder*? Explain both with the numbers.
???
**Further below the air: May.** In May the floor is 34.3 − 28.7 = 5.6 °C below the air; in July it is 29.4 − 26.7 = 2.7 °C below. May has about twice the room to cool, because its air, though hotter, is drier. In her town the gap is 38 − 21 = 17 °C, which is why her matka seems to work and ours seems not to.

**Colder water: possibly July.** The lowest a perfect matka could reach is 28.7 °C in May and 26.7 °C in July. July's water can be the colder drink, because July's air is cooler to begin with; the matka simply adds less to it. Both pots will sit a degree or two above these floors in practice, because energy leaks in through the clay from the room.

So the cousin is right that the matka does very little in July: the air is already close to its balance point, and evaporation is slow. She is wrong that it does nothing in Mumbai. It works in May, and it works best of all in the dry months before the monsoon, when the air has the most room for vapour.
:::
