Every transfer of energy in Chapter 8 needed stuff to carry it: particles colliding in conduction, air flowing in convection, water escaping from a matka. This chapter is about a different way for energy to get from one place to another, one in which the stuff stays put. A swell rolls in at Marine Drive during the monsoon. It came from far out over the Arabian Sea, and it arrives carrying enough energy to throw spray over the sea wall. Yet a plastic bottle floating beyond the breakers only bobs up and down as the swell passes under it, and is still there, bobbing, when the next one comes. The water did not make the journey. Something did.

That something is a wave, and this chapter follows the idea from the simplest swinging thing to the sound of a tabla and the horn of a passing local train. Begin with a swing.

## 9.1 Things that swing

:::scene Pisa, about 1583

The story goes that a young Galileo, bored during a service in the cathedral at Pisa, watched a lamp swinging on its chain and timed it against his own pulse, and found that each swing took the same time whether the lamp swung wide or barely moved. It is a lovely story, and it comes from one source only: Viviani, Galileo's assistant in his old age, writing long afterwards. The lamp that hangs in the cathedral today was installed after Galileo had left Pisa. So treat the lamp as a legend. What is true is that Galileo did study swinging pendulums, carefully and for years, and that the thing the legend has him notice is real.

You can notice it yourself on the swing in the building compound. Push off and let it go. Each swing takes the same time as the one before, and as the swing dies down the time between swings hardly changes at all. The time for one complete swing, out and back, is the ==period==; the number of swings in a second is the ==frequency==, which is one divided by the period. A swing on chains about 2.5 m long has a period of about 3.2 s.

:::note Period
The time for one complete repeat of a motion that repeats, such as one full swing out and back. Symbol T, in seconds.
:::

:::note Frequency
How many repeats happen each second, in hertz (Hz). Frequency = 1 ÷ period.
:::

:::predict
Two pendulums hang from strings of the same length. One has a 10 kg bob, the other 100 kg. Both are pulled back 12° and released. Which swings faster?
- The heavy one
- The light one
- Neither: they keep time with each other
???
They keep time with each other. Mass makes no difference to a pendulum's period. The heavier bob is pulled ten times harder by gravity, and it is ten times harder to get moving, and the two cancel exactly, as they did for falling objects in Chapter 4.
:::

What sets the period, then? Watch what pulls the bob back. At the bottom of the swing the string holds the bob up and gravity pulls it straight down; nothing pulls it sideways, so it sails through the middle at its fastest. Pull it to one side and part of its weight now points back along the arc, towards the middle. The further out it is, the bigger that pull back. For small swings the pull back grows in proportion to how far out the bob is, and anything whose pull back is proportional to its displacement swings in the same regular way, with a period that does not depend on how far you pull it. MIT's course on vibrations makes a sharp point here: there is no special law that says "pull back proportional to displacement". It is simply what almost any smooth system does when it is nudged a little way from a resting place, whether it is a swing, a plucked string, a bridge or a bell. Nudge it far and the rule breaks down, slowly at first.

:::fig c9_pendulum() | Two pendulums, one four times as long as the other. On the displaced bob, gravity's pull has a part along the arc, back towards the middle, which grows with the displacement. The period depends on the length and on gravity, never on the mass: four times the length gives twice the period.

For a pendulum the period depends on only two things, its length and the strength of gravity:

:::eq
period = 2π × √(length ÷ g)
:::

A pendulum one metre long has a period of 2.0 s; one four metres long, 4.0 s. Four times the length gives twice the period, because of the square root. This is why a short swing in a playground flicks back and forth while a long one at a temple festival moves with a slow grandeur. How far you pull it back barely matters: a swing released from 15° takes less than half a percent longer per swing than a tiny one, which is why the legend's lamp kept time. Pull it to 60° and each swing takes about 7% longer, so Galileo's rule is a rule for small swings. And since the period depends on g, a pendulum is a way to measure gravity. A pendulum of 75.000 cm timed at 1.7357 s per swing gives g = 9.83 m/s², which is the standard textbook example and is within a fraction of a percent of the accepted value.

:::home Weigh the Earth's pull with a key
Tie a key or a nut to a thread exactly one metre long, measured from the knot you hold to the middle of the key. Hold it against a door frame so the top cannot move, pull it aside less than 10° (about 15 cm at the bottom) and let go. Time ten complete swings with a phone stopwatch and divide by ten. You should get close to 2.0 s. Then square the period, multiply by the length, multiply by 39.5, and divide by the squared period again: that is, g = 39.5 × length ÷ period². With 1.00 m and 2.01 s you get 9.8 m/s². Try a thread twice as long and see what happens to the period.
:::

Real swings die away. Friction at the hinge and the push of the air drain a little energy every swing, and the arc shrinks until the swing hangs still. To keep a child swinging, a parent has to push, and *when* they push turns out to matter enormously. That is the next section.

:::careful
> "A heavier child makes the swing go faster, because gravity pulls harder."

Gravity does pull harder on the heavier child, and the heavier child is harder to get moving by exactly the same factor. The period comes out the same. Only the length of the chains (and the strength of gravity) decides how fast a swing swings; a heavier child only makes the chains creak more.
:::

:::school
At school, a motion that repeats like this is called an *oscillation*, and the period and frequency are defined exactly as here. The pendulum formula belongs to IB Diploma physics (topic C.1, simple harmonic motion); IGCSE asks for period and frequency only.
:::

:::check
A pendulum clock keeps perfect time in Mumbai. It is moved to a city where g is very slightly larger. It now runs fast. Should its pendulum be lengthened or shortened to fix it?
???
Lengthened. Larger g makes the period shorter, so the clock ticks too often and runs fast. A longer pendulum has a longer period, which cancels the change. Clockmakers once did exactly this with a screw under the bob.
:::

## 9.2 Resonance

:::scene London, June 2000

The Millennium Bridge, a new footbridge over the Thames, opened on 10 June 2000 with crowds pouring across it. Almost at once it began to sway from side to side, so much that people grabbed the rails. The engineers closed it two days later. The sway came from the walkers themselves. As the deck moved a little, people adjusted their steps to keep their balance, and in doing so they fell into step with the sway and pushed it further; the more it swayed, the more of them fell into step. Dampers costing about £5 million were fitted, and the bridge reopened in 2002. One textbook says it was closed "for the same reason" as a famous American bridge you will meet below. It was a different reason, and the difference is the point of this section.

Every swinging thing has a rhythm of its own, the rate at which it swings when you let it go and leave it alone: its ==natural frequency==. The swing in the compound, with its 3.2 s period, has a natural frequency of about 0.3 Hz. Now push it. Push once every 3.2 s, each time it comes back to you, and each push adds a little to the last; after ten pushes the child is flying. Push at random, and half your pushes fight the swing and half help it, and nothing much happens. Push very fast, several times a second, and the swing barely moves at all: it just shivers. Driving something at its own natural frequency so that small pushes add up into a big motion is ==resonance==.

:::note Natural frequency
The frequency at which something oscillates when it is set going and left alone, with nothing driving it.
:::

:::note Resonance
What happens when something is driven at or near its natural frequency: each small push arrives in time with the motion and adds to it, so the motion grows large.
:::

:::predict
A singer, the story always goes, can shatter a wine glass with her voice. For the glass to break, which matters more?
- Being loud enough: any note will do if it is loud
- Singing the right note: the glass's own natural frequency, even at moderate volume
- Both equally
???
The right note. A glass rings at its own natural frequency (run a wet finger round the rim and you can hear it). Sing that exact note and each wave of sound arrives in time with the glass's flexing and adds to it; the rim swings further and further until it cracks. MIT physics lecturers break a glass this way in front of their class, with a loudspeaker tuned to the glass's note. A different note, however loud, just fights itself.
:::

:::fig c9_resonance() | How far a driven oscillator swings, against the frequency of the pushes. The swing is largest when the pushes come at the natural frequency. With little damping (little friction) the peak is tall and narrow; with more damping it is lower and broader. The shape is schematic; the position of the peak is the physics.

How tall the peak is depends on damping, the friction that drains energy away. With little damping, the pushes keep adding up for a long time before the drain catches up, so the peak is very tall and very narrow: you must hit the frequency almost exactly. With heavy damping the peak is low and broad. The Millennium Bridge's engineers fixed it by adding damping, which is why the cure was dampers and never a change to the bridge's stiffness.

:::story resonance

:::evidence Why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell | 1940
Film of the suspension bridge at Tacoma Narrows, in the state of Washington, twisting itself to pieces in a steady wind in 1940 is shown in every physics course, usually with the explanation that gusts of wind happened to match the bridge's natural frequency. MIT's course on differential equations, following a careful 1991 analysis by Billah and Scanlan, calls that explanation "worthless": the wind was not gusting. What happened was subtler. A steady wind made the deck twist slightly; the twisted deck changed how the wind pushed on it; and the changed push drove the next twist. Each twist fed the one after. The bridge was pushing itself, using the wind's energy, the way a flag flaps in a steady breeze. Engineers call this flutter. Resonance needs a driver with the right rhythm; flutter makes its own. One car and one dog, Tubby, were lost; Tubby bit the professor who tried to rescue him.
:::

:::fig c9_flutter() | Two stories for the same bridge. Left, the textbook version: gusts of wind pushing in time with the deck's swing, which is resonance. Right, what the engineers found: a steady wind, a deck that twists, and a push that changes with the twist, so each twist drives the next. That is flutter, and it needs no rhythm from outside.

:::recall 7
A parent gives a swing one small push every 3.2 s, and after a minute the child is swinging two metres high. Is energy being made from nothing?
???
No. Each push does a little work and adds a little energy; resonance makes the twenty small contributions add up instead of cancelling. Meanwhile friction and the air drain some away every swing. The swing grows until the drain per swing matches the push per swing, and then it stays at that height. Nothing is created; it accumulates.
:::

:::try Investigation 9.2
Hang a small ball or a bunch of keys from a 40 cm string and hold the top in your hand. Move your hand gently from side to side, first very slowly, then at the ball's own rhythm, then as fast as you can. At which rhythm does the ball swing furthest? Then shorten the string and find the new rhythm.
:::

:::careful
> "Resonance is just a fancy name for shaking something hard."

Shaking hard is not the point; shaking *in time* is. A loud wrong note leaves the glass intact, and a modest right note breaks it. Nor is every violent oscillation resonance: the Tacoma bridge drove itself with a steady wind, which is flutter, and the Millennium Bridge was driven by walkers who adjusted their steps to it. Resonance means a driver whose rhythm matches the natural frequency.
:::

:::check
Soldiers crossing a bridge are ordered to break step and walk out of rhythm. Why?
???
Marching in step delivers a push to the bridge at one steady frequency. If that frequency is near the bridge's natural frequency, the pushes add up and the bridge could swing dangerously. Breaking step scatters the pushes across many rhythms, so they mostly cancel.
:::

## 9.3 What a wave carries

Fill a bucket to the brim and wait for the water to go still. Float a scrap of paper in the middle. Now dip a finger in at the edge, once. Rings spread out across the surface, reach the paper, and pass under it. Watch the paper.

:::predict
As the ripples reach the paper scrap and pass it, the scrap will:
- Be carried along with the ripples to the far side of the bucket
- Bob up and down, and stay roughly where it was
- Be pushed backwards, away from the ripples
???
It bobs and stays. Each ripple lifts it, moves it a little forward and then a little back, and sets it down almost exactly where it was. The ripples cross the bucket; the water, and the paper riding on it, does not.
:::

A ==wave== is a disturbance that travels away from the place where it was made. Your finger pushed the water down in one spot. That water pushed its neighbours, which pushed theirs, and the *push* travelled outward in a ring while each bit of water went up and down and came back to where it started. Out at sea it is the same, on a bigger scale: a bit of water near the surface moves round in a small loop as a swell passes, forward at the crest, back in the trough, and ends up where it began. The swell crosses the whole sea; the water goes round in circles. What travels is the shape, and the energy. That answers the chapter's question, and it is the third big idea of this book: waves carry energy without carrying stuff.

:::note Wave
A disturbance that travels away from where it was made, carrying energy. The material it travels through moves back and forth, or round in small loops, and ends up where it started.
:::

:::fig c9_wave_carries() | Ripples spreading from a dipped finger, seen from above and from the side. The crests move outward at a steady speed. The paper scrap, and each bit of water, goes round a small loop and returns; the dashed loop is its path. Wavelength is the distance from crest to crest; amplitude is the height of a crest above the still level.

Three numbers describe any wave. The distance from one crest to the next is the ==wavelength==. The height of a crest above the still level is the ==amplitude==. And the time between one crest passing and the next is the period, exactly as for the swing; its inverse is the frequency. Put the first and the last together and you have the wave's speed: a crest moves one wavelength in one period. If swells pass the paper scrap every 5.0 s and the crests are 10.0 m apart, the swell is travelling at 10.0 ÷ 5.0 = 2.0 m/s. For every wave there is, from ripples to sound to light:

:::note Wavelength
The distance from one crest to the next (or trough to trough). Symbol λ, in metres.
:::

:::note Amplitude
The largest displacement from the resting position: for a water wave, the height of a crest above the still level.
:::

:::eq
wave speed = frequency × wavelength
:::

The energy a wave carries goes with the *square* of its amplitude: a swell twice as high carries four times the energy, and one three times as high carries nine times. This is why a modest increase in wave height at Marine Drive turns a pleasant evening into spray over the wall. It also explains why the rings in the bucket fade as they spread. The energy of one ring is shared around a longer and longer circle, so the amplitude must fall.

:::recall 2
"The swell carries the water along, the way a thrown ball carries its push inside it." Chapter 2 had something to say about pushes carried inside things. What really travels in the swell?
???
Nothing carries a push inside it, and the swell carries no water. Each bit of water is pushed by its neighbour, moves round a small loop, and pushes the next; it is the pattern of pushes that moves on, together with the energy. The ball in Chapter 2 kept moving because nothing stopped it; the water here stops after every loop, because its neighbours pull it back.
:::

Waves come in two kinds. In the bucket, the water moves up and down while the ripple moves outward, across the direction of travel: a ==transverse== wave. A rope flicked at one end makes the same kind. In the other kind the material moves back and forth *along* the direction of travel, like a push running down a line of people standing close together: a ==longitudinal== wave. Sound is one of these, and it is the next section. Two waves can also cross each other. Where they meet, their displacements simply add, and then they carry on as if nothing had happened; the ripples from two fingers in the bucket pass straight through one another. Keep that; it matters for the tabla.

:::note Transverse wave
A wave in which the material moves across the direction the wave travels, like ripples on water or a flicked rope.
:::

:::note Longitudinal wave
A wave in which the material moves back and forth along the direction the wave travels, like a push running down a line of people. Sound is longitudinal.
:::

:::home Measure a ripple
In a bucket or a wide parat of still water, float a paper scrap and dip one finger at the edge at a steady rhythm, once a second. Watch the scrap stay put. Then estimate the distance between crests with a ruler held above the water, and the time between crests from your dipping. Divide one by the other for the speed. Does dipping harder change the spacing of the crests, or only their height?
:::

:::careful
> "Waves at the beach push the water up the sand, so waves do carry water."

At the shore, yes: when a wave runs into shallow water it breaks and the water in it tumbles forward. That is a wave falling apart. Out at sea, where the wave is a wave, the water goes round in loops and stays. The bottle beyond the breakers tells you so.
:::

:::school
IGCSE puts all of this under *3.1 General properties of waves*, with the same words: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, and the two kinds, transverse and longitudinal. Learn to draw a wave and label the first two.
:::

:::check
Drop a stone into still water. The rings spread out and, as they spread, get lower. Why?
???
The stone gave the water a fixed amount of energy. As a ring spreads, that energy is shared around a circle whose circumference keeps growing, so each metre of the ring carries less. Energy goes with amplitude squared, so the amplitude falls as the ring grows.
:::

## 9.4 Sound is a push passed on

Tie a steel spoon to the middle of a metre of string. Wind the two ends of the string round your index fingers and press those fingertips gently into your ears. Now let the spoon swing and knock against the edge of a table. Through the string you hear a deep, clear bell, as if from inside your head. Let the string go slack and tap the spoon again: a faint tink. The spoon vibrated the same way both times. What changed was the road the vibration took to your ear.

:::predict
A friend taps one end of a long steel railing with a coin. You are at the other end, 100 m away, with one ear pressed against the rail and the other in the air. Which ear hears the tap first?
- The ear in the air
- The ear on the rail
- Both at once
???
The ear on the rail, and by a clear margin. Sound travels through steel at about 5960 m/s and through warm air at about 349 m/s, so the rail delivers the tap in about 0.017 s and the air takes 0.29 s. The vibration travels about eighteen times faster in the steel.
:::

Here is what sound is. The spoon, struck, vibrates: its surface moves out and back thousands of times a second. Each time it moves out it squeezes the layer of air next to it; each time it moves back it leaves that layer stretched. The squeezed layer pushes on the next layer, which pushes on the next, and a pattern of squeezed and stretched air, ==compressions== and rarefactions, runs outward through the air at the speed of sound. No air travels from the spoon to your ear. Each layer shoves its neighbour and swings back, like the line of people in Section 9.3. Sound is a longitudinal wave of pushes passed on, and at your ear the pushes arrive one after another and set your eardrum moving in step with the spoon.

:::note Compression
A region where the material a sound passes through is momentarily squeezed closer together. Between compressions come rarefactions, where it is stretched apart.
:::

:::fig c9_compressions() | A vibrating spoon squeezes and stretches the air beside it, and the pattern of squeezed (compression) and stretched (rarefaction) air runs outward. Each air particle only shoves back and forth about its place. Below, the same wave drawn as a graph of air pressure along the line.

Take the air away and there is nothing to pass the push on. A bell ringing inside a jar goes silent as the air is pumped out, though you can still see the clapper strike. You cannot do that at home: a bag with the air sucked out is nowhere near empty, so do not trust that test. Trust the spoon instead. The string carried the vibration better than air did, because a tight string is stiff and passes a push along almost without loss. That is the rule for the speed of sound everywhere: stiffer material, faster sound; and, for a given stiffness, denser material, slower sound. Sound crosses water at about 1480 m/s and steel at 5960, eighteen times its speed in cold air. Lead, which is very dense, carries it at only 1960 m/s, slower than aluminium at 5120; so "denser is faster" is wrong, and "stiffer is faster" is right.

In air the speed depends on the temperature: 331 m/s at 0 °C, about 343 m/s at 20 °C, and 349 m/s on a 30 °C Mumbai afternoon. A clap aimed at a building 50 m away comes back as an echo 0.29 s later, because the sound has to go there and back, 100 m, at 349 m/s. At that speed a note of 20 Hz, the lowest the ear can hear, has a wavelength of 17 m, and the highest, 20,000 Hz, a wavelength of 1.7 cm.

:::evidence The speed of sound, measured in a glass tube | MIT, 2016
Newton worked out what the speed of sound in air should be, assuming the air's temperature stays fixed while it is squeezed, and got a number about 15% too low (with today's values for air, about 290 m/s). Laplace saw what was missing: a sound wave squeezes the air so quickly that the warmth made by the squeeze has no time to flow away, as Chapter 8 would lead you to expect, and warm squeezed air pushes back harder. His correction gives about 342 m/s. In an MIT lecture on waves the class sets up a standing sound wave in a glass tube, measures a wavelength of 34 cm at a known frequency, and gets 340 m/s, within 1% of Laplace's value and far from Newton's. The students vote for Newton or Laplace before the measurement. Most of them, like Newton, pick wrong.
:::

:::home The spoon bell
Tie a steel spoon to the middle of a metre of thread. Wrap the ends round your index fingers, press those fingertips lightly into your ears, lean forward and let the spoon knock the edge of a table. Then take your fingers out and knock it again. Try a fork, a steel ladle and a wooden spoon: which gives the deepest bell? The thread carries the vibration to your ear far better than the air does.
:::

:::careful
> "The air from the loudspeaker travels across the room to my ear."

If it did, every concert would be a gale. Each layer of air moves back and forth a tiny distance about its own place and shoves the next layer. The push travels; the air stays. It is the paper scrap in the bucket again, a thousand times a second.
:::

:::school
IGCSE topic *3.4 Sound* asks for this: sound is a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions, it needs a medium, and an echo is a reflection. Cambridge Lower Secondary puts it as "no sound in a vacuum".
:::

:::check
On Diwali night you see one firework burst and hear its bang at the same instant. Another, further off, bursts in silence, and the bang arrives a moment later. Why?
???
Light reaches you almost instantly from both (Chapter 10 says how fast). Sound from the near one takes so little time that you cannot notice the gap. Sound from the far one, at about 349 m/s, takes about 2.9 s for every kilometre, so its bang arrives late. Count the seconds between flash and bang and you can tell how far away the firework was.
:::

## 9.5 Loud and high

A baby wails in the next seat of the local train; a whistle shrieks on the platform; a friend hums in your ear. Each sound has two things you can tell at once: how loud it is, and how high. Children, and plenty of adults, run the two together, and say that a high sound is a loud one. The microphone in your phone can take them apart.

:::predict
You sing one steady note into a phone app that draws the sound wave, and then sing the same note twice as loud. On the screen, the wave becomes:
- Taller, with the same spacing
- The same height, with the crests closer together
- Both taller and closer together
???
Taller, with the same spacing. Loudness is the size of the vibration, the wave's amplitude. The spacing of the crests is set by how often your vocal cords vibrate, which is the note, and you did not change the note.
:::

What you hear as ==pitch== is the frequency of the vibration reaching your ear: how many compressions arrive each second. A low hum is a few hundred a second; a whistle, a few thousand. Human ears work from about 20 Hz to about 20,000 Hz, and the range shrinks at the top as people get older. Below 20 Hz is infrasound, above 20,000 Hz ultrasound, which a dog hears and you do not. The ear is remarkably fine about pitch: two notes only about 0.3% apart in frequency can be told apart.

:::note Pitch
How high or low a sound seems. It is the ear's reading of the frequency of the sound wave.
:::

What you hear as loudness is mostly the amplitude of the vibration, and, as for any wave, the energy goes with the amplitude squared. Double the amplitude and four times the energy arrives at your eardrum each second. The ear copes with an enormous range: the loudest sound you can stand delivers about a million million times the energy of the faintest you can hear. A scale that ran straight from one to a million million would be useless, so loudness is measured in ==decibels== (dB), on a scale in which every step of 10 dB means ten times the energy. The threshold of hearing is 0 dB; a whisper a metre away is about 20 dB; ordinary conversation, 60 dB; busy traffic, 70 dB, which is where a Mumbai road sits. The step from 40 dB to 70 dB is three tens, so it is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times the energy. Doubling the amplitude adds just 6 dB.

:::note Decibel
A unit for how loud a sound is. Every 10 dB more means ten times the sound energy arriving per second; 20 dB more means a hundred times.
:::

:::fig c9_loud_high() | Four sounds as a microphone sees them. Across: the same note, soft and loud, so the wave grows taller with the same spacing. Down: a low note and a high one at the same loudness, so the crests crowd together at the same height. Loudness is amplitude; pitch is frequency; they are separate knobs.

The sensor lab for this section is your own voice. The live version of the figure above listens through the phone's microphone and draws the pressure at the microphone against time, the way an oscilloscope does. Hum a low note softly, then louder: the trace grows taller and keeps its spacing. Hum higher: the crests crowd together. Whistle, and they crowd very close. Nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the phone; it only draws.

There is one wrinkle, which is why the figure says "mostly". Loudness depends a little on frequency as well, because the ear is less sensitive at the extremes of its range: a very low or very high note has to carry more energy to sound as loud as a middle one. For school, loudness goes with amplitude and pitch with frequency, and that is right at any single pitch.

:::story tabla

:::try Investigation 9.5
Put a phone playing music face down on a light table or a cardboard box and rest your fingertips on the surface. Turn the volume up: you feel the surface vibrate harder, and the vibration you feel is the amplitude. Then play a bass-heavy track and a thin, high one at the same volume and compare how much you can feel. The low one shakes the table more, because the big slow pushes move it further.
:::

:::careful
> "That whistle is so high it's deafening. High sounds are loud sounds."

Height and loudness are different properties of the same wave. A whistle can be high and faint; a drum can be low and deafening. The whistle hurts because the ear is very sensitive to frequencies in that range, so a modest amplitude there sounds loud; the loudness still comes from the amplitude.
:::

:::school
Cambridge Lower Secondary states it as "loudness ↔ amplitude, pitch ↔ frequency", which is this section in six words. IGCSE adds the decibel scale under *3.4 Sound*.
:::

:::check
A mayor announces that the new trains will add "only 30 dB" to the noise beside the line. Should the residents be reassured?
???
No. Each 10 dB is ten times the sound energy, so 30 dB is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times. "Only 30 dB" on top of 40 dB is 70 dB, the level of busy traffic.
:::

## 9.6 Why a tabla sings

:::scene Calcutta, 1920

Strike an ordinary drum and you get a thud with a vague sense of height, and no note you could hum. Strike a tabla and a clear, singing note rings out, one the player tunes to the singer. In 1920 C. V. Raman and his student Sivakali Kumar published a short paper in *Nature* explaining why. Most drums, they showed, give out overtones that are out of tune with one another, so the sound is a clash with no clear pitch. The tabla and the mridangam are different: their overtones fall in tune, at whole-number multiples of the lowest one, as a string's do, and the reason is the *syahi*, the black loaded patch on the drumhead, which tunes the drumhead's modes into line. To see why that is remarkable, start with a string.

:::scene Florence, about 1589

The old story says Pythagoras discovered the laws of harmony by hearing blacksmiths' hammers and weighing them. It was first written down about six centuries after his time, and the physics in it is wrong: the weight of a hammer does not set its note. The true version has Galileo's father in it. Vincenzo Galilei, a musician in Florence, hung weights from strings to tighten them and measured what he heard. He found that to raise a string's note by an octave, doubling its frequency, you must pull it with four times the weight. Four times the pull for twice the pitch: the pattern is a square root, and it is one of the first laws of physics found by careful measurement.

A string fixed at both ends cannot vibrate any way it likes. Its ends are held still, so the only patterns that fit are the ones with a whole number of half-wavelengths between the ends: one bump, two bumps, three. These patterns are its modes. The one-bump pattern has the lowest frequency, the ==fundamental==, and gives the note you hear. The two-bump pattern vibrates at exactly twice that frequency, the three-bump at three times, and so on: the string's higher patterns, its ==overtones==, are all whole-number multiples of the fundamental, which is what makes them ==harmonics==. When you pluck a string it vibrates in many of these patterns at once, all added together as in Section 9.3, and the mixture is what reaches your ear.

:::note Fundamental
The lowest frequency at which a string, pipe or drumhead can vibrate. It sets the note you hear.
:::

:::note Overtone
Any of the higher frequencies at which something can vibrate, above its fundamental.
:::

:::note Harmonic
An overtone that is a whole-number multiple of the fundamental: 2×, 3×, 4× and so on. Strings and open pipes have only harmonics; most drums do not.
:::

:::fig c9_harmonics() | Left: the first three patterns a string can vibrate in, at the fundamental frequency f, then 2f, then 3f. Middle: the ladder of overtones for a string (all whole-number multiples) and for an ideal flat drumhead (1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30 times the lowest: out of tune). Right: the tabla's syahi loads the middle of the drumhead and pulls its overtones onto the whole-number rungs.

The mix of overtones is what gives an instrument its character. A violin and a harmonium playing the same note have the same fundamental and different mixes, and that is why you can tell them apart with your eyes shut; the word for it is ==timbre==. Air in a pipe has modes too. A pipe open at both ends behaves like a string, with every multiple present. A pipe closed at one end can only hold patterns with a still point at the closed end, and that allows only the odd multiples: 1, 3, 5. A bottle 30 cm deep blown across the top on a warm day sounds at about 290 Hz, with overtones near 870 and 1450 Hz; the same tube open at both ends would sound an octave higher, at about 580 Hz.

:::note Timbre
The quality that makes two instruments playing the same note sound different: the particular mix of overtones each one produces.
:::

:::predict
Blow across the mouth of an empty glass bottle and you get a low toot. Pour water into the bottle, so the air column above the water is shorter, and blow again. The note:
- Goes up
- Goes down
- Stays the same, because the bottle is the same bottle
???
It goes up. The note comes from the air inside, and a shorter column of air fits a shorter wavelength, which is a higher frequency. The bottle's glass has nothing to do with it; the water simply shortens the air.
:::

Now the drum. A drumhead is a flat circular skin held still all round its rim, and its modes are not bumps along a line but patterns across a surface: one hump in the middle, then two halves rocking, then rings and quarters. Work out the frequencies of an ideal flat drumhead and they come out at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65 times the lowest. None of those is a whole number. The overtones do not fit the fundamental, so when you strike the drum you hear a clash that soon dies into a thud. Sprinkle salt on a flat plate driven by a loudspeaker and you can see the modes: the salt gathers along the lines that stay still, in rings and spokes, a different pattern for each frequency. Raman and Kumar found that the tabla's syahi, a heavy patch built up in layers at the centre of the head, loads the middle of the skin and shifts its modes until several of the overtones sit at whole-number multiples of the lowest. The drum becomes, acoustically, a kind of string, and sings a note. The patch is tuned by the maker, layer by layer, by ear. Whole-number overtones are the sound of a note; out-of-tune overtones are the sound of a thud, and the live version of the figure above lets you build both from the same ingredients.

:::evidence The syahi | Calcutta, 1920
Raman and Kumar's paper, in *Nature* for 15 January 1920, reported that the Indian drums they tested gave harmonic overtones, unlike the drums of the European orchestra, and traced the difference to the loaded patch on the drumhead. The point of the experiment is a comparison: the same kind of skin stretched over the same kind of shell, with and without the black patch, gives a thud without it and a note with it. Raman went on to study the tabla's modes in detail. It is a small example of what he did again and again: take something everyone around him had heard a thousand times, and ask why.
:::

:::home A bottle organ
Line up three identical glass bottles. Leave one empty, half-fill the second with water and nearly fill the third. Blow gently across the mouth of each (practise: a thin, steady stream of air just over the rim). Predict which gives the highest note before you blow. Then tap the bottles with a spoon instead: now which is highest? Blowing sounds the air, tapping sounds the glass and water, and the two orders are opposite.
:::

:::careful
> "All overtones are whole-number multiples of the fundamental."

That is true for an ideal string and for a pipe open at both ends, and textbooks often say it as if it were true of everything. A pipe closed at one end gives only the odd multiples, and a plain drumhead's overtones are not whole-number multiples at all; that is why most drums have no clear note. The tabla is the exception, and the syahi is the reason.
:::

:::check
Two wind instruments are the same length. One is a pipe open at both ends; the other is closed at one end. Which can play the lower note?
???
The closed one. Its fundamental fits a quarter of a wavelength into the pipe, so its wavelength is four times the length; the open pipe's fundamental fits half a wavelength, so its wavelength is only twice the length. The closed pipe's fundamental is an octave lower, at half the frequency.
:::

## 9.7 The moving whistle

:::scene Utrecht, 1845

In 1842 Christian Doppler, in Prague, read a short paper to a handful of members of the Royal Bohemian Society. If a source of waves moves towards you, he argued, the waves should reach you more often and the pitch should rise; if it moves away, the pitch should fall. His main example was the colours of double stars, and it was wrong. Three years later a Dutch meteorologist, Christophorus Buys Ballot, put the idea to a test that sounds like a prank. On the new railway line between Utrecht and Maarssen he had horn players stand on an open wagon pulled by a locomotive, sounding one steady note, while musicians with perfect pitch stood by the track and wrote down what they heard. The note rose as the wagon came towards them and fell as it went away, exactly as Doppler had said. A well-known textbook credits Doppler himself with the train. It was Buys Ballot.

You have done his experiment on every station platform. Stand behind the yellow line as a fast local comes through with its horn sounding. The note holds steady while the train is far off, then, as it passes, drops, and holds a new, lower note as it recedes. The driver, meanwhile, hears none of this.

:::predict
The driver of the train hears the horn, which is mounted on the train with her. Compared with the horn's true note, she hears:
- A higher note, because the train is moving fast
- A lower note
- The true note
???
The true note. She and the horn move together, so each compression reaches her exactly as often as the horn makes it. The shift happens only between things moving towards or away from each other.
:::

Here is why the note changes, with no formula needed. The horn makes compressions at a steady rate, say 300 a second, and each one spreads out through the air at the speed of sound from the point where it was made. But the horn does not stay at that point. By the time it makes the next compression it has moved forward, so the new compression starts a little closer to you than the last. Ahead of the train the compressions are bunched together: a shorter wavelength. Behind, each one starts further away than the last, so they are stretched out: a longer wavelength. The compressions still travel at the fixed speed of sound, so bunched-up ones arrive more often, which your ear reads as a higher note, and stretched-out ones arrive less often, a lower note. This is the ==Doppler effect==, and it is as real as a thermometer reading: a microphone measures the changed frequency, so it is no trick of the ear.

:::note Doppler effect
The change in the frequency of a wave heard or seen when the source and the observer are moving towards or away from each other. Approaching: higher. Receding: lower.
:::

:::fig c9_doppler() | Compressions spreading from a horn that is moving to the right. Each ring started where the horn was when it made that compression, so the rings are bunched ahead and spread out behind. The listener ahead receives them more often (a higher note) and the one behind less often (a lower note). The ring spacing is drawn for a horn moving at about a fifteenth of the speed of sound.

How big is the change? For a horn of frequency f on a train moving at speed v towards you, with sound travelling at c, the frequency you hear is f × c ÷ (c − v); moving away, it is f × c ÷ (c + v). Take a textbook example first: a 150 Hz horn at 35 m/s, with sound at 340 m/s, is heard at 167 Hz approaching and 136 Hz receding. Notice that the rise (17 Hz) is bigger than the fall (14 Hz): the two shifts are never quite symmetric. Now a Mumbai local. Nobody has published its horn's frequency or the speed through a station, so take a 300 Hz horn at 80 km/h, which is 22 m/s, on a 30 °C afternoon when sound travels at 349 m/s:

:::work
Approaching: 300 Hz × 349 ÷ (349 − 22) ≈ 320 Hz
Receding: 300 Hz × 349 ÷ (349 + 22) ≈ 282 Hz
The ratio 320 ÷ 282 ≈ 1.14, a drop of about 2.2 semitones: a bit more than a whole tone.
:::

That matches what your ear tells you on the platform: a drop of about a tone as the train passes, with the note steady before and steady after. The drop happens quickly because, while the train is far off, almost all of its motion is straight towards you, and as it passes, the part of its motion that is towards you shrinks to nothing and then reverses. A listener who moves while the source stands still hears a shift too, for the same reason: she meets the compressions more often as she runs towards the horn. And a source that moves at the speed of sound itself catches up with its own compressions, which pile into one great pressure wall, the start of a sonic boom.

:::story doppler

:::try Investigation 9.7
With an adult, stand on a platform well behind the yellow line as a fast local passes with its horn sounding. Hum the note you hear as it approaches, then the note as it recedes, into a tuner app, and read off the two frequencies. Does the ratio come out near 1.14? Trains differ, so your number will be your own.
:::

:::careful
> "The horn gets louder as the train comes, and that is why it sounds higher."

Louder and higher are separate (Section 9.5). The pitch rises because the compressions arrive more often, and that depends on the train's speed, never on how loud the horn is. A faint horn on a fast train shifts more than a deafening one on a slow train.
:::

That is the horn's falling note, for light. IB Diploma physics covers both as topic C.5. You will meet redshift again in Chapter 15.
:::

:::check
Two cars sound identical horns as they drive towards you, one at 20 m/s and one at 40 m/s. Which horn sounds higher?
???
The faster one. The faster the source moves towards you, the more each new compression starts ahead of the last, so the more bunched they are and the more often they arrive. The shift grows with the speed.
:::

## The echo off the building

:::case The echo off the building
A hot afternoon, 30 °C. You stand in the compound facing the building across the open ground and clap once, hard. The clap comes back to you from the wall. Your cousin says the wall "makes a new sound" when the clap hits it. You set the phyphox app's acoustic stopwatch going and clap again. It reads 0.29 s between the clap and its return.

Three questions. How far away is the wall? Is the echo a new sound? And would the answer to the first question change much on a cool January morning at 20 °C?
???
**Distance.** The sound went to the wall and came back, so it travelled twice the distance in 0.29 s. At 30 °C sound travels at about 349 m/s, so the round trip is 349 × 0.29 ≈ 101 m, and the wall is about 50 m away.

**Not a new sound.** The compressions you made with your hands ran to the wall, pushed on it, and were pushed back: the same pattern of pushes, turned around. The wall adds nothing of its own, which is why an echo repeats what you said and never says anything else.

**January.** At 20 °C sound travels at about 343 m/s, so the same 0.29 s would mean 343 × 0.29 ÷ 2 ≈ 50 m. The temperature changes the answer by less than a metre, far less than the stopwatch's own uncertainty. For a rough distance, any warm-day speed will do; the 2% difference matters only to engineers.
:::
