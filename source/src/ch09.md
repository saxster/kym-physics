Every transfer of energy in Chapter 8 needed stuff to carry it: particles colliding in conduction, air flowing in convection, water escaping from a matka. This chapter is about a different way for energy to travel, one in which the stuff stays put. A swell rolls in at Marine Drive during the monsoon. It came from far out over the Arabian Sea, and it arrives with enough energy to throw spray over the sea wall. Yet a plastic bottle floating beyond the breakers only bobs as the swell passes under it, and is still there, bobbing, when the next one comes. The water did not make the journey. Something did.

That something is a wave, and this chapter follows the idea from the simplest swinging thing to the sound of a tabla and the horn of a passing local train. Begin with a swing.

## 9.1 Things that swing

:::scene Pisa, about 1583

The story goes that a young Galileo, in the cathedral at Pisa, watched a lamp swinging on its chain and noticed that each swing took the same time whether the lamp swung wide or barely moved. It is a lovely story, and it comes from one source only: Viviani, Galileo's assistant in his old age, writing long afterwards. The lamp that hangs in the cathedral today was installed after Galileo had left Pisa. So treat the lamp as a legend. What is true is that Galileo did study swinging pendulums, carefully and for years, and that what the legend has him notice is real.

You can notice it yourself on the swing in the building compound. Push off and let it go. Each swing takes the same time as the one before, and as the swing dies down the time between swings hardly changes. The time for one complete swing, out and back, is the ==period==; the number of swings in a second is the ==frequency==, which is one divided by the period. A swing on chains about 2.5 m long has a period of about 3.2 s.

:::note Period
The time for one complete repeat of a motion that repeats, such as one full swing out and back. Symbol T, in seconds.
:::

:::note Frequency
How many repeats happen each second, in hertz (Hz). Frequency = 1 ÷ period.
:::

:::predict
Two pendulums hang from strings of the same length. One has a 10 kg bob, the other 100 kg. Both are pulled back 12° and released. Which swings faster?
- The heavy one
- The light one
- Neither: they keep time with each other
???
They keep time with each other. Mass makes no difference to a pendulum's period. The heavier bob is pulled ten times harder by gravity and is ten times harder to get moving, and the two cancel exactly.
:::

What sets the period, then? Watch what pulls the bob back. At the bottom of the swing nothing pulls it sideways, so it sails through the middle at its fastest. Pull it to one side and part of its weight points back along the arc, towards the middle, and the further out it is, the bigger that pull. For small swings the pull back grows in proportion to the displacement, and anything with that property swings in the same regular way, with a period that does not depend on how far you pull it. MIT's course on vibrations makes a sharp point here: there is no special law behind this. It is what almost any smooth system does when it is nudged a little from a resting place, whether a swing, a plucked string, a bridge or a bell. Nudge it far and the rule breaks down, slowly at first.

:::fig c9_pendulum() | Two pendulums, one four times as long as the other. On the displaced bob, gravity's pull has a part along the arc, back towards the middle, which grows with the displacement. The period depends on the length and on gravity, never on the mass: four times the length gives twice the period.

For a pendulum the period depends on only two things, its length and the strength of gravity:

:::eq
**frequency and period**: *f* = 1 ÷ *T*
**pendulum period**: *T* = 2π × √(*L* ÷ *g*)
:::

A pendulum one metre long has a period of 2.0 s; one four metres long, 4.0 s. Four times the length gives twice the period, because of the square root. How far you pull it back barely matters: a swing released from 15° takes less than half a per cent longer per swing than a tiny one, which is why the legend's lamp kept time. At 60° each swing takes about 7% longer, so Galileo's rule is a rule for small swings. And since the period depends on g, a pendulum measures gravity: 75.000 cm timed at 1.7357 s per swing gives g = 9.83 m/s², within a fraction of a per cent of the accepted value.

:::home Weigh the Earth's pull with a key
Tie a key to a thread exactly one metre long, measured from the knot you hold to the middle of the key. Hold it against a door frame so the top cannot move, pull it aside less than 10° (about 15 cm at the bottom) and let go. Time ten complete swings with a phone stopwatch and divide by ten; you should get close to 2.0 s. Then work out g = 39.5 × length ÷ (period × period); the 39.5 is 2π squared. With 1.00 m and 2.01 s you get 9.8 m/s². Try a thread twice as long and see what happens to the period.
:::

:::careful
> "A heavier child makes the swing go faster, because gravity pulls harder."

Gravity does pull harder on the heavier child, and the heavier child is harder to get moving by exactly the same factor. The period comes out the same. Only the length of the chains (and the strength of gravity) decides how fast a swing swings.
:::

:::five Simple harmonic motion
? What is it?
Any back-and-forth oscillation in which the pull back towards the resting position grows in direct proportion to the displacement from it.
? Why is it called this?
Nineteenth-century physicists called it "harmonic" because a pure musical tone has the exact same smooth sine-wave shape in time, and "simple" because it is the single fundamental rhythm out of which more complicated vibrations are built.
? What problem did it solve?
It explained why a pendulum, a mass on a spring, a plucked guitar string and a tuning fork all keep steady time as their swings die down, with a period independent of amplitude.
? Why does it work?
Pull the object twice as far from the middle and it has twice as far to travel back, but the restoring force is also twice as strong, accelerating it twice as hard; the extra distance and the extra acceleration cancel in the time of each swing.
? How does it help?
It lets clocks keep accurate time with pendulums or quartz crystals, and lets you measure local gravity (*g*) simply by timing a pendulum of known length.
?? How do we know?
Time ten swings of a 1.00 m pendulum pulled back 5° and then 12°: both give 2.01 s per swing, and swapping a light key for a heavy stone changes nothing.
?? What it isn't
Not every repeating motion is simple harmonic: a ball bouncing up and down on a hard floor repeats, but the force on it is not proportional to distance from a midpoint and its period shrinks as the bounces get smaller.
?? Where does it stop working?
Pull a pendulum out to large angles (such as 60°) and the restoring pull along the arc grows more slowly than the angle itself, making the period about 7% longer than Galileo's small-swing rule predicts.
:::

:::school
At school, a motion that repeats like this is called an *oscillation*, and the period and frequency are defined exactly as here. The pendulum formula belongs to IB Diploma physics (topic C.1, simple harmonic motion); IGCSE asks for period and frequency only.
:::

:::check
A pendulum clock keeps perfect time in Mumbai. It is moved to a city where g is very slightly larger. It now runs fast. Should its pendulum be lengthened or shortened to fix it?
???
Lengthened. Larger g makes the period shorter, so the clock ticks too often and runs fast. A longer pendulum has a longer period, which cancels the change.
:::

## 9.2 Resonance

:::scene London, June 2000

The Millennium Bridge, a new footbridge over the Thames, opened on 10 June 2000 with crowds pouring across it. Almost at once it began to sway from side to side, and the engineers closed it two days later. The sway came from the walkers themselves. As the deck moved a little, people adjusted their steps to keep their balance, fell into step with the sway, and pushed it further; the more it swayed, the more of them fell into step. Dampers costing about £5 million were fitted, and the bridge reopened in 2002. One textbook says it was closed "for the same reason" as a famous American bridge you will meet below. It was a different reason, and the difference is the point of this section.

Every swinging thing has a rhythm of its own, the rate at which it swings when you let it go and leave it alone: its ==natural frequency==. The swing in the compound, with its 3.2 s period, has a natural frequency of about 0.3 Hz. Push it once every 3.2 s, each time it comes back to you, and each push adds a little to the last; after ten pushes the child is flying. Push at random, and half your pushes fight the swing. Push very fast, several times a second, and the swing barely moves: it just shivers. Driving something at its own natural frequency, so that small pushes add up into a big motion, is ==resonance==.

:::note Natural frequency
The frequency at which something oscillates when it is set going and left alone, with nothing driving it.
:::

:::note Resonance
What happens when something is driven at or near its natural frequency: each small push arrives in time with the motion and adds to it, so the motion grows large.
:::

:::five Resonance
? What is it?
The large build-up of oscillation that happens when a system is pushed repeatedly at a frequency matching its own natural frequency.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *resonare*, to sound back or echo, first used in acoustics for a musical string or air column that begins to hum in sympathy when its own note is sung nearby.
? What problem did it solve?
It explained how very gentle periodic forces, too weak to move an object far in a single push, can selectively make one particular string vibrate, shatter a wine glass, or set a footbridge swaying.
? Why does it work?
When each push arrives in step with the object's natural swing, it always pushes in the direction the object is already moving, so every cycle adds energy instead of cancelling the previous cycle.
? How does it help?
Engineers use it to tune radios and musical instruments so they respond strongly to one frequency, and to design dampers on bridges and tall buildings so they do not sway dangerously.
?? How do we know?
Drive an oscillator across a range of frequencies and measure its amplitude: the swing peaks sharply at the natural frequency, and adding friction (damping) lowers and broadens that peak.
?? What it isn't
It is not just shaking something violently at any frequency, and not every bridge failure is resonance: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisted itself apart in a steady wind by aerodynamic flutter, making its own rhythm.
?? Where does it stop working?
If the swing grows so large that the restoring force is no longer proportional to displacement, the natural frequency itself shifts with amplitude and the simple resonance curve bends over.
:::

:::predict
A singer, the story always goes, can shatter a wine glass with her voice. For the glass to break, which matters more?
- Being loud enough: any note will do if it is loud
- Singing the right note: the glass's own natural frequency, even at moderate volume
- Both equally
???
The right note. A glass rings at its own natural frequency. Sing that exact note and each wave of sound arrives in time with the glass's flexing and adds to it; the rim swings further and further until it cracks. MIT physics lecturers break a glass this way in front of their class, with sound at the glass's own note. A different note, however loud, just fights itself.
:::

:::fig c9_resonance() | How far a driven oscillator swings, against the frequency of the pushes. The swing is largest when the pushes come at the natural frequency. With little damping (little friction) the peak is tall and narrow; with more damping it is lower and broader. The shape is schematic; the position of the peak is the physics.

How tall the peak is depends on damping, the friction that drains energy away. With little damping the pushes keep adding up for a long time before the drain catches up, so the peak is tall and narrow: you must hit the frequency almost exactly. With heavy damping the peak is low and broad. That is why the Millennium Bridge's cure was dampers: they drain the sway's energy faster than the walkers can feed it.

:::story resonance

:::evidence Why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge fell | 1940
Film of the suspension bridge at Tacoma Narrows, in the state of Washington, twisting itself to pieces in a steady wind in 1940 is shown in physics courses everywhere, usually with the explanation that gusts of wind happened to match the bridge's natural frequency. MIT's course on differential equations, following a 1991 analysis by Billah and Scanlan, calls that explanation "worthless": the wind was not gusting. A steady wind made the deck twist slightly; the twisted deck changed how the wind pushed on it; and the changed push drove the next twist. The bridge was pushing itself, using the wind's energy, the way a flag flaps in a steady breeze. Engineers call this flutter. Resonance needs a driver with the right rhythm; flutter makes its own. One car and one dog, Tubby, were lost; Tubby bit the professor who tried to rescue him.
:::

:::fig c9_flutter() | Two stories for the same bridge. Left, the textbook version: gusts of wind pushing in time with the deck's swing, which is resonance. Right, what the engineers found: a steady wind, a deck that twists, and a push that changes with the twist, so each twist drives the next. That is flutter, and it needs no rhythm from outside.

:::recall 7
A parent gives a swing one small push every 3.2 s, and after a minute the child is swinging two metres high. Is energy being made from nothing?
???
No. Each push does a little work and adds a little energy; resonance makes the twenty small contributions add up instead of cancelling. Meanwhile friction and the air drain some away every swing. The swing grows until the drain per swing matches the push per swing.
:::

:::careful
> "Resonance is just a fancy name for shaking something hard."

Shaking *in time* is the point. A loud wrong note leaves the glass intact, and a modest right note breaks it. Nor is every violent oscillation resonance: the Tacoma bridge drove itself with a steady wind, which is flutter, and the Millennium Bridge was driven by walkers who adjusted their steps to it.
:::

:::check
Soldiers crossing a bridge are ordered to break step and walk out of rhythm. Why?
???
Marching in step delivers a push to the bridge at one steady frequency. If that frequency is near the bridge's natural frequency, the pushes add up and the bridge could swing dangerously. Breaking step scatters the pushes across many rhythms, so they mostly cancel.
:::

## 9.3 What a wave carries

Fill a bucket to the brim and wait for the water to go still. Float a scrap of paper in the middle. Now dip a finger in at the edge, once. Rings spread out across the surface, reach the paper, and pass under it. Watch the paper.

:::predict
As the ripples reach the paper scrap and pass it, the scrap will:
- Be carried along with the ripples to the far side of the bucket
- Bob up and down, and stay roughly where it was
- Be pushed backwards, away from the ripples
???
It bobs and stays. Each ripple lifts it, moves it a little forward and then a little back, and sets it down almost exactly where it was. The ripples cross the bucket; the water, and the paper riding on it, does not.
:::

A ==wave== is a disturbance that travels away from the place where it was made. Your finger pushed the water down in one spot. That water pushed its neighbours, which pushed theirs, and the *push* travelled outward in a ring while each bit of water went up and down and came back to where it started. Out at sea it is the same on a bigger scale: a bit of water near the surface moves round in a small loop as a swell passes, forward at the crest, back in the trough, and ends up where it began. The swell crosses the whole sea; the water goes round in circles. What travels is the shape, and the energy. That answers the chapter's question, and it is the third big idea of this book: waves carry energy without carrying stuff.

:::note Wave
A disturbance that travels away from where it was made, carrying energy. The material it travels through moves back and forth, or round in small loops, and ends up where it started.
:::

:::five Wave
? What is it?
A travelling disturbance that carries energy and information from one place to another without carrying matter along with it.
? Why is it called this?
From the Old English *wagian*, to move to and fro, first applied to the ridges on the sea and then extended by physicists to sound, ripples on strings, and light.
? What problem did it solve?
It showed how energy can cross a bucket, a concert hall, or an ocean while the water or air stays in its own neighbourhood, and gave one mathematical rule (*v* = *f*λ) for ripples, sound and light alike.
? Why does it work?
Each piece of the medium is coupled to its neighbours: displace one bit and it pulls or squeezes the next bit, handing the motion and energy onward while restoring forces pull the original bit back to its resting place.
? How does it help?
Knowing a wave's frequency and wavelength gives its speed immediately, and knowing that waves add where they meet explains echoes, beats, musical notes, and interference.
?? How do we know?
Float a scrap of paper on rippled water or track air in a sound tube: the crests race across from one side to the other while the paper scrap only bobs in a small loop about the same spot.
?? What it isn't
It is not a stream of water or air travelling from source to receiver: wind and ocean currents move matter, whereas sound and sea swells move only a pattern of motion and energy.
?? Where does it stop working?
When a water wave runs into shallow water near a beach, the bottom slows the trough while the crest overtakes it and topples forward as surf; there the wave breaks and really does throw water up the sand.
:::

:::fig c9_wave_carries() | Ripples spreading from a dipped finger, seen from above and from the side. The crests move outward at a steady speed. The paper scrap, and each bit of water, goes round a small loop and returns; the dashed loop is its path. Wavelength is the distance from crest to crest; amplitude is the height of a crest above the still level.
:::live ripple

Three numbers describe any wave. The distance from one crest to the next is the ==wavelength==. The height of a crest above the still level is the ==amplitude==. And the time between one crest passing and the next is the period, exactly as for the swing; its inverse is the frequency. A crest moves one wavelength in one period, which gives the wave's speed: if swells pass the paper scrap every 5.0 s and the crests are 10.0 m apart, the swell is travelling at 10.0 ÷ 5.0 = 2.0 m/s. For every wave there is, from ripples to sound to light:

:::note Wavelength
The distance from one crest to the next (or trough to trough). Symbol λ, in metres.
:::

:::note Amplitude
The largest displacement from the resting position: for a water wave, the height of a crest above the still level.
:::

:::eq
wave speed = frequency × wavelength
:::

The energy a wave carries goes with the *square* of its amplitude: a swell twice as high carries four times the energy, and one three times as high carries nine times. It is also why the rings in the bucket fade as they spread: the energy of one ring is shared around a longer and longer circle, so the amplitude must fall.

:::recall 2
"The swell carries the water along, the way a thrown ball carries its push inside it." Chapter 2 had something to say about pushes carried inside things. What really travels in the swell?
???
Nothing carries a push inside it, and the swell carries no water. Each bit of water is pushed by its neighbour, moves round a small loop, and pushes the next; it is the pattern of pushes that moves on, together with the energy. Unlike the ball in Chapter 2, each bit of water is pulled back by its neighbours after every loop.
:::

Waves come in two kinds. In the bucket the water moves up and down while the ripple moves outward, across the direction of travel: a ==transverse== wave. A rope flicked at one end makes the same kind. In the other kind the material moves back and forth *along* the direction of travel, like a push running down a line of people standing close together: a ==longitudinal== wave. Sound is one of these. Two waves can also cross. Where they meet their displacements simply add, and then they carry on as if nothing had happened; the ripples from two fingers in the bucket pass straight through one another.

:::note Transverse wave
A wave in which the material moves across the direction the wave travels, like ripples on water or a flicked rope.
:::

:::note Longitudinal wave
A wave in which the material moves back and forth along the direction the wave travels, like a push running down a line of people. Sound is longitudinal.
:::

:::home Measure a ripple
In a bucket or a wide parat of still water, float a paper scrap and dip one finger at the edge at a steady rhythm, once a second. Watch the scrap stay put. Estimate the distance between crests with a ruler held above the water, and the time between crests from your dipping; divide one by the other for the speed. Does dipping harder change the spacing of the crests, or only their height?
:::

:::careful
> "Waves at the beach push the water up the sand, so waves do carry water."

At the shore, yes: when a wave runs into shallow water it breaks, and the water in it tumbles forward. That is a wave falling apart. Out at sea, where the wave is a wave, the water goes round in loops and stays.
:::

:::school
IGCSE puts all of this under *3.1 General properties of waves*, with the same words: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, and the two kinds, transverse and longitudinal. Learn to draw a wave and label the first two.
:::

:::check
Drop a stone into still water. The rings spread out and, as they spread, get lower. Why?
???
The stone gave the water a fixed amount of energy. As a ring spreads, that energy is shared around a circle whose circumference keeps growing, so each metre of the ring carries less. Energy goes with amplitude squared, so the amplitude falls as the ring grows.
:::

## 9.4 Sound is a push passed on

Tie a steel spoon to the middle of a metre of string. Wind the two ends of the string round your index fingers and press those fingertips gently into your ears. Now let the spoon swing and knock against the edge of a table. Through the string you hear a deep, clear bell, as if from inside your head. Let the string go slack and tap the spoon again: a faint tink. The spoon vibrated the same way both times. What changed was the road the vibration took to your ear.

:::predict
A friend taps one end of a long steel railing with a coin. You are at the other end, 100 m away, with one ear pressed against the rail and the other in the air. Which ear hears the tap first?
- The ear in the air
- The ear on the rail
- Both at once
???
The ear on the rail, by a clear margin. Sound travels through steel at about 5960 m/s and through warm air at about 349 m/s, so the rail delivers the tap in about 0.017 s and the air takes 0.29 s. The vibration travels about eighteen times faster in the steel.
:::

Here is what sound is. The spoon, struck, vibrates: its surface moves out and back many times a second. Each time it moves out it squeezes the layer of air next to it; each time it moves back it leaves that layer stretched. The squeezed layer pushes on the next, which pushes on the next, and a pattern of squeezed and stretched air, ==compressions== and rarefactions, runs outward at the speed of sound. No air travels from the spoon to your ear. Each layer shoves its neighbour and swings back, like the line of people in Section 9.3. Sound is a longitudinal wave of pushes passed on, and at your ear the pushes set your eardrum moving in step with the spoon.

:::note Compression
A region where the material a sound passes through is momentarily squeezed closer together. Between compressions come rarefactions, where it is stretched apart.
:::

:::fig c9_compressions() | A vibrating spoon squeezes and stretches the air beside it, and the pattern of squeezed (compression) and stretched (rarefaction) air runs outward. Each air particle only shoves back and forth about its place. Below, the same wave drawn as a graph of air pressure along the line.

Take the air away and there is nothing to pass the push on. A bell ringing inside a jar goes silent as the air is pumped out. You cannot do that at home: a bag with the air sucked out is nowhere near empty, so do not trust that test. Trust the spoon. The string carried the vibration better than air did because a tight string is stiff and passes a push along almost without loss, and that is the rule everywhere: stiffer material, faster sound; and, for a given stiffness, denser material, slower sound. Sound crosses water at about 1480 m/s and steel at 5960, eighteen times its speed in cold air. Lead, which is very dense, carries it at only 1960 m/s, slower than aluminium at 5120; so "denser is faster" is wrong and "stiffer is faster" is right.

In air the speed depends on the temperature: 331 m/s at 0 °C, about 343 m/s at 20 °C, and 349 m/s on a 30 °C Mumbai afternoon. A clap aimed at a building 50 m away comes back as an echo 0.29 s later, because the sound has to go there and back, 100 m, at 349 m/s.

:::evidence The speed of sound, measured in a glass tube | MIT, 2016
Newton worked out what the speed of sound in air should be, assuming the air's temperature stays fixed while it is squeezed, and got a number about 15% too low (with today's values for air, about 290 m/s). Laplace saw what was missing: a sound wave squeezes the air so quickly that the warmth made by the squeeze has no time to flow away, as Chapter 8 would lead you to expect, and warm squeezed air pushes back harder. His correction gives about 342 m/s. In an MIT lecture on waves the class sets up a standing sound wave in a glass tube, measures a wavelength of 34 cm at a known frequency, and gets 340 m/s, within 1% of Laplace's value.
:::

:::home The spoon bell
Tie a steel spoon to the middle of a metre of thread. Wrap the ends round your index fingers, press those fingertips lightly into your ears, lean forward and let the spoon knock the edge of a table. Then take your fingers out and knock it again. Try a fork, a steel ladle and a wooden spoon: which gives the deepest bell?
:::

:::careful
> "The air from the loudspeaker travels across the room to my ear."

Each layer of air moves back and forth a tiny distance about its own place and shoves the next layer. The push travels; the air stays. It is the paper scrap in the bucket again, a thousand times a second.
:::

:::school
IGCSE topic *3.4 Sound* asks for this: sound is a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions, it needs a medium, and an echo is a reflection. Cambridge Lower Secondary puts it as "no sound in a vacuum".
:::

:::check
On Diwali night you see one firework burst and hear its bang at the same instant. Another, further off, bursts in silence, and the bang arrives a moment later. Why?
???
Light reaches you almost instantly from both (Chapter 10 says how fast). Sound from the near one takes so little time that you cannot notice the gap. Sound from the far one, at about 349 m/s, takes about 2.9 s for every kilometre, so its bang arrives late. Count the seconds between flash and bang and you can tell how far away the firework was.
:::

## 9.5 Loud and high

A baby wails in the next seat of the local train; a whistle shrieks on the platform; a friend hums in your ear. Each sound has two things you can tell at once: how loud it is, and how high. Children, and plenty of adults, run the two together, and say that a high sound is a loud one. The microphone in your phone can take them apart.

:::predict
You sing one steady note into a phone app that draws the sound wave, and then sing the same note twice as loud. On the screen, the wave becomes:
- Taller, with the same spacing
- The same height, with the crests closer together
- Both taller and closer together
???
Taller, with the same spacing. Loudness is the size of the vibration, the wave's amplitude. The spacing of the crests is set by how often your vocal cords vibrate, which is the note, and you did not change the note.
:::

What you hear as ==pitch== is the frequency of the vibration reaching your ear: how many compressions arrive each second. Human ears work from about 20 Hz to about 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). Below 20 Hz is infrasound, above 20,000 Hz ==ultrasound==, and you hear neither, though dogs, bats and dolphins hear well into the ultrasonic range. The ear is remarkably fine about pitch: two notes only about 0.3% apart in frequency can be told apart.

:::note Pitch
How high or low a sound seems. It is the ear's reading of the frequency of the sound wave.
:::

Because ultrasound has such a high frequency, its wavelength (*λ* = *v* ÷ *f*) is tiny — under a millimetre in human tissue or steel at a few megahertz — so it can be aimed in a narrow beam and reflects cleanly off small boundaries that long, audible sound waves would simply wash around. Send out a short click of ultrasound and time the echo (*d* = ½*vt*, with the half because the pulse goes there and back), and you can measure hidden distances without cutting anything open. Ships and submarines map the seabed with ultrasonic ==sonar== (sound navigation and ranging). On land, engineers slide an ultrasonic probe over a steel railway axle or aircraft wing: a hidden internal crack reflects a small early echo before the main echo from the far wall arrives, revealing the flaw before the metal snaps. And in hospitals an ultrasound scanner presses a probe against the skin and builds a live image of an unborn baby or a beating heart from the echoes returning at different times from each tissue boundary — completely safely, without the ionising X-rays you will meet in Chapter 15.

:::note Ultrasound
Sound waves with frequencies above 20,000 Hz (20 kHz), the upper limit of human hearing. Their short wavelengths allow narrow beams for sonar depth-sounding, industrial flaw detection in metals, and non-invasive medical imaging via pulse-echo timing (*d* = ½*vt*).
:::

:::work
A research vessel in the Arabian Sea sends a short ultrasonic pulse straight down and detects the seabed echo 0.80 s later. Sound in warm sea water travels at about 1,480 m/s.
total round-trip distance = 1,480 m/s × 0.80 s = 1,184 m
depth of the seabed = ½ × 1,184 m = 592 m
:::

What you hear as loudness is mostly the amplitude of the vibration, and, as for any wave, the energy goes with the amplitude squared. The ear copes with an enormous range: the loudest sound you can stand delivers about a million million times the energy of the faintest you can hear. A scale running straight from one to a million million would be useless, so loudness is measured in ==decibels== (dB), on a scale in which every step of 10 dB means ten times the energy. The threshold of hearing is 0 dB; a whisper a metre away is about 20 dB; ordinary conversation, 60 dB; busy traffic, 70 dB, which is where a Mumbai road sits. The step from 40 dB to 70 dB is three tens, so it is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times the energy. Doubling the amplitude adds just 6 dB.

:::note Decibel
A unit for how loud a sound is. Every 10 dB more means ten times the sound energy arriving per second; 20 dB more means a hundred times.
:::

Down: a low note and a high one at the same loudness, so the crests crowd together at the same height. Loudness is amplitude; pitch is frequency; they are separate knobs.
:::live scope

The sensor lab for this section is your own voice: the live version of the figure above listens through the phone's microphone and draws the pressure against time, the way an oscilloscope does. Hum louder and the trace grows taller; hum higher and the crests crowd together. Nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the phone.

One wrinkle, which is why the paragraph above says "mostly": loudness depends a little on frequency as well, because the ear is less sensitive at the extremes of its range, so a very low or very high note has to carry more energy to sound as loud as a middle one.

:::try Investigation 9.5
Put a phone playing music face down on a light table or a cardboard box and rest your fingertips on the surface. Turn the volume up: you feel the surface vibrate harder, and the vibration you feel is the amplitude. Turn it down until you can only just feel it, then listen: is that also the point where you can only just hear it?
:::

:::careful
> "That whistle is so high it's deafening. High sounds are loud sounds."

Height and loudness are different properties of the same wave. A whistle can be high and faint; a drum can be low and deafening. The ear is more sensitive to some frequencies than others, so a modest amplitude at a frequency it likes can sound loud; the loudness still comes from the amplitude.
:::

:::school
Cambridge Lower Secondary states it as "loudness ↔ amplitude, pitch ↔ frequency", which is this section in six words. IGCSE (*3.4 Sound*) adds the audible range of 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, defines ultrasound as sound above 20 kHz, and asks for pulse-echo applications of ultrasound in sonar, medical scanning and non-destructive testing of materials (*d* = ½*vt*).
:::

:::check
A mayor announces that the new trains will add "only 30 dB" to the noise beside the line. Should the residents be reassured?
???
No. Each 10 dB is ten times the sound energy, so 30 dB is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 times. "Only 30 dB" on top of 40 dB is 70 dB, the level of busy traffic.
:::

## 9.6 Why a tabla sings

:::scene Calcutta, 1920

Strike an ordinary drum and you get a thud with a vague sense of height, and no note you could hum. Strike a tabla and a clear, singing note rings out. In 1920 C. V. Raman and Sivakali Kumar published a short paper in *Nature* explaining why. Most drums, they showed, give out overtones that are out of tune with one another, so the sound is a clash with no clear pitch. The tabla and the mridangam are different: their overtones fall in tune, at whole-number multiples of the lowest one, as a string's do, and the reason is the *syahi*, the black loaded patch on the drumhead, which tunes the drumhead's modes into line. To see why that is remarkable, start with a string.

:::scene Florence, about 1589

The old story says Pythagoras discovered the laws of harmony by hearing blacksmiths' hammers and weighing them. It was first written down about six centuries after his time, and the physics in it is wrong: the weight of a hammer does not set its note. The true version has Galileo's father in it. Vincenzo Galilei, in Florence, hung weights from strings to tighten them and measured what he heard. To raise a string's note by an octave, doubling its frequency, you must pull it with four times the weight. Four times the pull for twice the pitch: the pattern is a square root, found by measuring.

A string fixed at both ends cannot vibrate any way it likes. Its ends are held still, so the only patterns that fit are the ones with a whole number of half-wavelengths between the ends: one bump, two bumps, three. The one-bump pattern has the lowest frequency, the ==fundamental==, and gives the note you hear. The two-bump pattern vibrates at exactly twice that frequency, the three-bump at three times, and so on: the string's higher patterns, its ==overtones==, are all whole-number multiples of the fundamental, which is what makes them ==harmonics==. Pluck a string and it vibrates in many of these patterns at once, added together as in Section 9.3, and the mixture is what reaches your ear.

:::note Fundamental
The lowest frequency at which a string, pipe or drumhead can vibrate. It sets the note you hear.
:::

:::note Overtone
Any of the higher frequencies at which something can vibrate, above its fundamental.
:::

:::note Harmonic
An overtone that is a whole-number multiple of the fundamental: 2×, 3×, 4× and so on. Strings and open pipes have only harmonics; most drums do not.
:::

:::fig c9_harmonics() | Left: the first three patterns a string can vibrate in, at the fundamental frequency f, then 2f, then 3f. Middle: the ladder of overtones for a string (all whole-number multiples) and for an ideal flat drumhead (1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30 times the lowest: out of tune). Right: the tabla's syahi loads the middle of the drumhead and pulls its overtones onto the whole-number rungs.
:::live harmonics

The mix of overtones gives an instrument its character. A violin and a harmonium playing the same note have the same fundamental and different mixes, which is why you can tell them apart with your eyes shut; the word for it is ==timbre==. Air in a pipe has modes too. A pipe open at both ends behaves like a string, with every multiple present. A pipe closed at one end can only hold patterns with a still point at the closed end, which allows only the odd multiples: 1, 3, 5. A straight tube 30 cm long, closed at one end and blown across the open end on a warm day, sounds at about 290 Hz, with overtones near 870 and 1450 Hz; the same tube open at both ends would sound an octave higher, at about 580 Hz.

:::note Timbre
The quality that makes two instruments playing the same note sound different: the particular mix of overtones each one produces.
:::

:::predict
Blow across the mouth of an empty glass bottle and you get a low toot. Pour water into the bottle, so the air column above the water is shorter, and blow again. The note:
- Goes up
- Goes down
- Stays the same, because the bottle is the same bottle
???
It goes up. The note comes from the air inside, and a shorter column of air fits a shorter wavelength, which is a higher frequency. The water simply shortens the air.
:::

Now the drum. A drumhead is a flat circular skin held still all round its rim, and its modes are patterns across a surface rather than bumps along a line: one hump in the middle, then two halves rocking, then rings and quarters. Work out the frequencies of an ideal flat drumhead and they come out at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65 times the lowest. None of those is a whole number, so the overtones do not fit the fundamental, and when you strike the drum you hear a clash that soon dies into a thud. Raman and Kumar found that the tabla's syahi weighs down the middle of the skin and shifts its modes until the overtones sit at whole-number multiples of the lowest. The drum becomes, acoustically, a kind of string, and sings a note. Whole-number overtones are the sound of a note; out-of-tune overtones are the sound of a thud, and the live version of the figure above lets you build both from the same ingredients.

:::story tabla

:::home A bottle organ
Line up three identical glass bottles. Leave one empty, half-fill the second with water and nearly fill the third. Blow gently across the mouth of each (practise: a thin, steady stream of air just over the rim). Predict which gives the highest note before you blow. Then tap the bottles with a spoon instead: is the order the same? Blowing sounds the air inside; tapping sounds the glass and the water in it, which is a different vibration altogether.
:::

:::careful
> "All overtones are whole-number multiples of the fundamental."

That is true for an ideal string and for a pipe open at both ends. A pipe closed at one end gives only the odd multiples, and a plain drumhead's overtones are not whole-number multiples at all; that is why most drums have no clear note. The tabla is the exception, and the syahi is the reason.
:::

:::check
Two wind instruments are the same length. One is a pipe open at both ends; the other is closed at one end. Which can play the lower note?
???
The closed one. Its fundamental fits a quarter of a wavelength into the pipe, so its wavelength is four times the length; the open pipe's fundamental fits half a wavelength, so its wavelength is only twice the length. The closed pipe's fundamental is an octave lower, at half the frequency.
:::

## 9.7 The moving whistle

:::scene Utrecht, 1845

In 1842 Christian Doppler, in Prague, read a short paper to a handful of members of the Royal Bohemian Society. If a source of waves moves towards you, he argued, the waves should reach you more often and the pitch should rise; if it moves away, the pitch should fall. His main example was the colours of double stars, and it was wrong. Three years later a Dutch scientist, Christophorus Buys Ballot, tested it. On the railway line between Utrecht and Maarssen he had horn players stand on an open wagon pulled by a locomotive, sounding one steady note, while musicians with perfect pitch stood by the track and wrote down what they heard. The note rose as the wagon came towards them and fell as it went away. A well-known textbook credits Doppler himself with the train. It was Buys Ballot.

You have done his experiment on every station platform. Stand behind the yellow line as a fast local comes through with its horn sounding. The note holds steady while the train is far off, then, as it passes, drops, and holds a new, lower note as it recedes. The driver, meanwhile, hears none of this.

:::predict
The driver of the train hears the horn, which is mounted on the train with her. Compared with the horn's true note, she hears:
- A higher note, because the train is moving fast
- A lower note
- The true note
???
The true note. She and the horn move together, so each compression reaches her exactly as often as the horn makes it. The shift happens only between things moving towards or away from each other.
:::

Here is why the note changes. The horn makes compressions at a steady rate, say 300 a second, and each one spreads out through the air at the speed of sound from the point where it was made. But the horn does not stay at that point. By the time it makes the next compression it has moved forward, so the new compression starts a little closer to you than the last. Ahead of the train the compressions are bunched together: a shorter wavelength. Behind, each one starts further away than the last, so they are stretched out. The compressions still travel at the fixed speed of sound, so bunched-up ones arrive more often, which your ear reads as a higher note, and stretched-out ones arrive less often, a lower note. This is the ==Doppler effect==, and it is as real as a thermometer reading: a microphone measures the changed frequency, so it is no trick of the ear.

:::note Doppler effect
The change in the frequency of a wave heard or seen when the source and the observer are moving towards or away from each other. Approaching: higher. Receding: lower.
:::

:::fig c9_doppler() | Compressions spreading from a horn that is moving to the right. Each ring started where the horn was when it made that compression, so the rings are bunched ahead and spread out behind. The listener ahead receives them more often (a higher note) and the one behind less often (a lower note). The rings are drawn for a horn moving at about a third of the speed of sound, five times faster than a local train, so that the bunching shows.
:::live doppler

How big is the change? For a horn of frequency f on a train moving at speed v towards you, with sound travelling at c, the frequency you hear is f × c ÷ (c − v); moving away, it is f × c ÷ (c + v). A textbook example first: a 150 Hz horn at 35 m/s, with sound at 340 m/s, is heard at 167 Hz approaching and 136 Hz receding. The rise (17 Hz) is bigger than the fall (14 Hz): the two shifts are never quite symmetric. Now a Mumbai local. Nobody has published its horn's frequency or its speed through a station, so take a 300 Hz horn at 80 km/h, which is 22 m/s, on a 30 °C afternoon when sound travels at 349 m/s:

:::work
Approaching: 300 Hz × 349 ÷ (349 − 22) ≈ 320 Hz
Receding: 300 Hz × 349 ÷ (349 + 22) ≈ 282 Hz
The ratio 320 ÷ 282 ≈ 1.14, a drop of about 2.2 semitones: a bit more than a whole tone.
:::

That matches what your ear tells you on the platform: a drop of about a tone as the train passes. The drop is quick because, while the train is far off, almost all of its motion is straight towards you, and as it passes, that part shrinks to nothing and then reverses. A source that moves at the speed of sound catches up with its own compressions, which pile into one great pressure wall, the start of a sonic boom.

:::story doppler

:::try Investigation 9.7
With an adult, stand on a platform well behind the yellow line as a fast local passes with its horn sounding. Hum the note you hear as it approaches, then the note as it recedes, into a tuner app, and read off the two frequencies. Does the ratio come out near 1.14? Trains differ, so your number will be your own.
:::

:::careful
> "The horn gets louder as the train comes, and that is why it sounds higher."

Louder and higher are separate (Section 9.5). The pitch rises because the compressions arrive more often, and that depends on the train's speed, never on how loud the horn is.
:::

:::school
IGCSE leaves out the Doppler effect for sound. Under *6.2.3* it asks for *redshift*: the increase in the wavelength of light from galaxies that are moving away from us, which is the horn's falling note, for light. IB Diploma physics covers both as topic C.5. You will meet redshift again in Chapter 15.
:::

:::check
Two cars sound identical horns as they drive towards you, one at 20 m/s and one at 40 m/s. Which horn sounds higher?
???
The faster one. The faster the source moves towards you, the more each new compression starts ahead of the last, so the more bunched they are and the more often they arrive.
:::

## The echo off the building

:::case The echo off the building
A hot afternoon, 30 °C. You stand in the compound facing the building across the open ground and clap once, hard. The clap comes back to you from the wall. Your cousin says the wall "makes a new sound" when the clap hits it. You set the phyphox app's acoustic stopwatch going and clap again. It reads 0.29 s between the clap and its return.

Two questions. How far away is the wall? And is the echo a new sound?
???
**Distance.** The sound went to the wall and came back, so it travelled twice the distance in 0.29 s. At 30 °C sound travels at about 349 m/s, so the round trip is 349 × 0.29 ≈ 101 m, and the wall is about 50 m away.

**Not a new sound.** The compressions you made with your hands ran to the wall, pushed on it, and were pushed back: the same pattern of pushes, turned around. The wall adds nothing of its own, which is why an echo repeats what you said and never says anything else.
:::
