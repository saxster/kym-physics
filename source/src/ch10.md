Chapter 9 left you with an odd fact. A wave crosses the whole Arabian Sea, and the water in it stays, more or less, where it was. A sound crosses the room, and the air beside the tabla stays beside the tabla. What travels is energy and a pattern. The stuff stays behind.

Hold on to that, because the next two chapters are about the most familiar wave there is, and the hardest to think about clearly: light. You have been using it since you opened your eyes this morning, and you have probably never asked how. So here is the question to carry through this chapter. Tonight, with the lights off and the door shut, hold your hand up in front of your face. Can you see it? Most people say yes: a little, after a while, once your eyes get used to the dark. Keep your answer. We are going to test it, and then follow light from a lamp to your eye, into a mirror, through water, through a lens onto the back of your eyeball, and finally out to a moon of Jupiter that kept turning up late.

## 10.1 Does anything leave your eyes?

Take a pencil and draw this: you, a mango on the table, and arrows showing how you see it. Do it before you read on.

Most people draw at least one arrow that starts at the eye and points at the mango. In one study, students were asked to draw how a person sees a balloon, and 86 in every 100 drew arrows going out from the eye. Among adults asked straight out whether anything leaves the eye when you look, 69 in 100 said yes. If that is what you drew, you are in old and distinguished company. Euclid, the geometer, and Ptolemy, the astronomer, both believed that the eye sends out rays which reach out and touch what you see. The idea fits how looking feels. Looking is something you *do*. You point your eyes, you choose, you stare. A gaze has a direction, and a direction feels like something going out.

:::scene Cairo, c. 1011

Around the year 1011, in Cairo, a scholar called Ibn al-Haytham set out to test the idea instead of arguing about it. (The story goes that he was in confinement at the time, having pretended to be mad to escape the Caliph's anger over a failed scheme to tame the Nile. Historians doubt it.) He put two observations together. First: look at the Sun, or at a very bright lamp, and your eyes hurt. If seeing were something your eyes sent out, why should a bright object hurt them? Something must be arriving. Second: every point of a lit object sends out its own light, in every direction, and some of that light enters the eye. He wrote it all up in his *Book of Optics*, with experiments, including a trick we will use in the next section: a dark room with one small hole, in which the sunlit world outside paints itself on the far wall. He did not understand everything about how the eye works, and said so. But the arrows, he argued, point in.

So let us test it, as he would have.

:::predict
You are in a room with no window. The door is shut and every crack is sealed, so that no light at all gets in. You wait five minutes for your eyes to get used to the dark, then hold your hand up in front of your face. What do you see?
- Your hand, dimly. Eyes adjust.
- Only a faint outline where your hand is.
- Nothing at all, however long you wait.
???
Nothing at all. Eyes do adjust in the dark, and remarkably well. Your eye works across a range of brightness from the faintest thing it can detect to something ten billion (10¹⁰) times brighter, and the cells it uses in dim light get by on about a thousandth of the light the colour-seeing cells need. But adjusting means collecting the little light there is more carefully. If there is none, there is nothing to collect. A bedroom that seems pitch dark usually has a sliver of light under the door or round a curtain, and after a few minutes that sliver is enough to show shapes. A truly sealed room shows you nothing, and that is the answer to this chapter's big question.
:::

:::home The darkest room in the flat
Tell an adult what you are doing and where you will be. Find the darkest room, usually a bathroom with no window. Leave your phone outside: a phone is a lamp. Switch the light off, shut the door, and if light leaks under it, lay a towel along the gap. Count slowly to sixty to let your eyes adjust, then hold your hand in front of your face and wave it. Now open the door the width of a finger and watch. The moment a sliver of light comes in, your hand appears, because light from the crack is bouncing off your hand and into your eye. Shut the door and it goes again. Your hand did not change. The light did.
:::

Here is what happens when you see the mango. Light leaves a source: the Sun, a lamp, the screen of a phone. It travels across the room and hits the mango. The skin of a mango is rough on the scale of light, so the mango sends that light off in every direction at once: up, down, sideways, back towards the lamp. Some of it, a small share, happens to be heading for your eye. That share enters your eye, and that is seeing. Three things, always in the same order: a source, a thing, your eye, with light travelling from each to the next. Every arrow ends at the eye. None begins there.

The scattering-in-all-directions part has a name, ==diffuse reflection==, and it is why you can read this page from anywhere in the room. A page, a wall, a hand, a face: all of them are rough enough to send light everywhere. A mirror is the exception, which is why mirrors get a section of their own.

:::note Diffuse reflection
Light bouncing off a rough surface goes off in all directions. That is how you see a page or a wall from any angle, and it is why nothing is visible in a sealed dark room: there is no light to bounce.
:::

You can even watch light on its way. Fill a plant sprayer with water and spray a fine mist across the beam of a torch in a dark room. The beam appears, a bright bar in the air. Stop spraying and it vanishes, though the torch is still on and its light is still crossing the room. You see the beam only where droplets are sending some of its light into your eye. In clean air there is nothing to send it, and from the side the beam is invisible.

:::fig c10_seeing() | Two drawings of seeing a mango. Left: the one most people make, with rays going out from the eye. Right: what happens. Light from the lamp reaches the mango, the mango's rough skin sends it off in every direction, and a small share of it enters your eye. Every arrow ends at the eye.

:::careful
> "Looking is something I do. I point my eyes at things. Something must go out of them."

Nothing goes out. Your eye only collects. Pointing your eyes chooses *which* light to collect, the way turning a bucket in the rain chooses which drops to catch; the bucket sends nothing out. You already accept this for hearing. You turn your head towards a sound, and you never imagine that your ears send anything out to fetch it. Seeing is the same kind of thing, with light in place of sound. The test is the sealed room. If your eyes sent anything out, you would see your hand there. You don't.
:::

:::story seeing

:::check
Someone with an oily, shiny nose dabs face powder on it, and the shine is gone. What did the powder do to the light?
???
The thin film of oil was a smooth surface, a tiny mirror. It sent the lamp's light off in one direction, and from that direction you saw a bright highlight. Powder is rough. Light hitting it goes off in every direction, evenly, so no direction gets a highlight. Nothing changed about the light arriving; only what the nose did with it.
:::

## 10.2 Straight lines and shadows

Ibn al-Haytham's dark room is the oldest camera in the world, and you can build one from a shoebox. Shut yourself in a dark room with one small hole in the shutter, and the sunlit street outside appears on the opposite wall: dim, in colour, moving, and upside down. People walk along the wall with their feet in the air. The Latin name for such a room, ==camera obscura==, means "dark chamber", and it is where the word *camera* comes from.

Why upside down? Because light goes straight. Light from the top of a tree outside travels in a straight line through the hole. A straight line that comes in high at the hole carries on downwards, so it lands low on the wall. Light from the tree's roots comes in low and lands high. Every point of the scene outside sends light through the hole to exactly one point on the wall, and so the whole picture arrives turned over, top to bottom and left to right. Nothing "flips" the light. Straight lines through one small opening simply cross there.

:::note Camera obscura
A dark room or box with a small hole in one side. Light from outside passes straight through the hole and forms an upside-down picture on the opposite wall. A pinhole camera is a small one.
:::

:::fig c10_pinhole() | A pinhole camera. Rays from two marked window points cross at the hole and reach inverted positions on the screen. Main apparatus spacing and sizes are schematic. The separate height comparison uses one shared scale: with the screen 30 cm from the hole and the window 3 m away, a 1 m window makes a 10 cm image.

The size of the picture is pure geometry. Suppose the box is 30 cm deep and you point it at a window 1 m tall that is 3 m away. The light from the window's top edge and bottom edge crosses at the hole, making two triangles that share a point, and the small one is the same shape as the big one.

:::work
distance to the window: 3 m, so 300 cm
depth of the box: 30 cm, which is 300 ÷ 30 = one tenth of the distance
height of the picture = one tenth of the window's height
= 100 cm ÷ 10 = 10 cm, upside down
:::

In drawings we show the path light takes as a straight line with an arrowhead, and we call that line a ==ray==. A ray is a pencil line showing a direction; light does not come in thin threads. The drawing works because, within one material, and past things much larger than light's own wavelength, light goes straight. Light's wavelength (the crest-to-crest spacing you met for sound in Chapter 9) is tiny, about 400 to 700 nanometres, where a nanometre is a billionth of a metre. "Much larger" therefore covers everything you can see with your eyes. The exceptions wait for Chapter 11: at the edges of very small gaps, light does bend round corners, a little.

:::note Ray
A straight line, with an arrowhead, that shows which way light travels. It is a drawing tool. Rays work when light meets things much bigger than its wavelength, which is nearly everything.
:::

:::school
At school you will "draw and use ray diagrams". A ray isn't a thing that exists. It's a pencil line showing the direction light goes, and the rules for drawing it (straight, bouncing, bending) are the subject of this chapter.
:::

Before you build the camera, decide something about the hole.

:::predict
You make the pinhole bigger, three times as wide. What happens to the picture on the screen?
- Brighter, and just as sharp.
- Blurrier, and just as bright.
- Brighter and blurrier at the same time.
???
Brighter and blurrier. A bigger hole lets more light through, so the picture brightens. But now each point of the scene lights a small disc on the screen instead of a single point, because light from that point can get through anywhere in the hole. Neighbouring discs overlap, and the picture smears. A pinhole camera forces you to choose between bright and sharp. There is a way to have both: a curved piece of glass that gathers a wide beam and brings it back to a point. That is a lens, and Section 10.5 puts one in your eye.
:::

:::home A camera in a shoebox
Ask an adult to help with the cutting. You need a shoebox, kitchen foil, a pin, tracing paper or baking paper, and tape. Cut a hole about 3 cm across in the middle of one end of the box, tape foil over it, and prick the foil once with the pin. Cut away most of the opposite end and tape tracing paper over it as a screen. Put the lid on. In a dim room, point the pinhole at a bright window and look at the screen with a dark cloth over your head and the box, so no stray light reaches the paper. The window appears on it, upside down. Move the box nearer the window and the picture grows. Then, with the adult, make the pinhole a little bigger with a pencil tip, and see whether your prediction held. Never point the box, or your eyes, at the Sun.
:::

A shadow is the same geometry seen the other way round. Light that was travelling straight towards the wall is stopped by your body, so a patch of wall gets none, and the patch has your shape because the rays that missed you went straight on. With a small lamp the shadow's edge is sharp. In sunlight the edge is fuzzy, and the reason is that the Sun is not a point: it is a disc. Each point on that disc casts its own slightly shifted shadow of you, and round the edge there is a band where some of the Sun is hidden behind you and some is not. Hold that thought; in Chapter 11 we will ask whether light itself ever bends round an edge.

:::recall 2
A torch beam looks bright on a wall 1 m away and much fainter on a wall 10 m away. A friend says the light "runs out of push" on the way, the way a rolling ball slows to a stop. What is really happening?
???
Nothing slows the light. It arrives at full speed either way, and in empty space it would carry on for ever with nothing pushing it, just as the ball on Galileo's frictionless ramp would. What changes is that the beam spreads out as it travels, because its rays fan out in straight lines from the torch. The same light is shared across a bigger patch of wall, so each bit of wall gets less. The light is spread thinner; none of it has been used up.
:::

:::check
In a camera obscura the picture is upside down. A friend says "the hole flips the light over". Give the real reason.
???
The hole does nothing to the light. Light from each point outside travels in a straight line through the hole. Lines from high points pass through heading downwards and land low; lines from low points land high. Because all the lines cross at one small opening, the picture is turned over, top to bottom and also left to right.
:::

## 10.3 Mirrors

Stand in front of the bathroom mirror and ask a question you have probably never asked: where is your face? Not the real one; the one you are looking at. Most people say it is on the glass, like a picture stuck to the surface. Here is a test. Walk up to the mirror and touch it with a fingertip. Your fingertip's image meets your finger at the glass, which is what you would expect if the image were on the surface. Now look at your face. Your real face is an arm's length in front of the glass. Your face in the mirror is an arm's length *behind* it. The image of anything is as far behind the mirror as the thing is in front, so if you stand 1.0 m from a mirror, your image is 1.0 m behind the glass and 2.0 m from you.

The mirror can do this because it is smooth on the scale of light. A rough page sends light every way; a mirror sends each ray off in one definite direction, following one rule. Draw a line at right angles to the mirror's surface, which physicists call the ==normal==. The angle between the arriving ray and the normal is the angle of incidence; the angle between the departing ray and the normal is the angle of reflection; and they are equal. Light leaves at the angle it arrived, on the other side of the normal.

:::eq
angle of reflection = angle of incidence
:::

:::note Normal
A line drawn at right angles to a surface at the point where light hits it. Angles in optics are measured from the normal, never from the surface.
:::

Now follow the light from one point on your nose. It leaves in every direction (your nose is a diffuse reflector, as Section 10.1 said). Some of those rays hit the mirror, bounce by the rule, and enter your eye. Your eye knows only one thing about each ray: the direction it arrived from. So it does what it always does, and assumes the light came in a straight line. Trace the reflected rays straight back, through the glass, and they meet at a point behind the mirror: as far behind it as your nose is in front. That meeting point is where you see your nose. Nothing is there. The wall behind the mirror is there. The rays only *seem* to come from that point, and so it is called a ==virtual image==.

:::note Virtual image
A picture formed where light rays seem to come from, when traced back in straight lines, although no light actually passes through that place. The image in a flat mirror is virtual.
:::

:::five Virtual image
? What is it?
An image formed at the place where diverging light rays appear to come from when traced straight back, even though no light actually passes through that place.
? Why is it called this?
From medieval Latin *virtualis*, having the effect or appearance without the physical substance, used in optics since the seventeenth century to contrast with a *real* image where rays genuinely meet on a screen.
? What problem did it solve?
It explained why your face in a flat mirror looks as though it sits behind the glass at a definite distance, and why a magnifying glass makes letters look larger and farther back without projecting them onto a wall.
? Why does it work?
Your eyes and brain judge the position of any point by the angle at which two or more rays from it enter the pupil, always assuming the rays travelled in straight lines; a mirror redirects the rays so their straight-back extensions cross behind the glass.
? How does it help?
It lets you locate exactly where an image sits in any mirror or lens by drawing two rays and extending the reflected or refracted lines backwards with a ruler.
?? How do we know?
Focus a camera on a smudge on the mirror's glass and your reflected face is blurry; refocus to twice your distance from the mirror and your face becomes sharp, proving the rays enter the lens as if coming from behind the glass.
?? What it isn't
It is not an optical illusion in your head and it does not sit on the surface of the mirror; the diverging light rays entering your eye are completely physical.
?? Where does it stop working?
Because no light actually reaches the image's location behind the mirror, you can never catch a virtual image on a piece of paper put there; to put it on a screen or a retina, a second lens (such as your eye's) must bend the diverging rays back together into a real image.
:::

:::fig c10_mirror() | A point on a face, a flat mirror, and an eye. Two rays leave the point: one hits the mirror and reflects into the eye, with the angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence (both measured from the dashed normal); another meets the mirror head-on and comes straight back. Trace both reflected rays backwards (dashed) and they meet behind the glass, as far behind it as the face is in front. That is where the image appears.
:::live mirror

Virtual does not mean imaginary. The light entering your eye is real light; a camera pointed at a mirror collects the same rays and takes a perfectly good photograph of a virtual image. "Virtual" only describes where the light appears to come from.

:::predict
You can see yourself from head to waist in a small mirror on the wall. You step back, away from the mirror. Now you see
- more of yourself: the mirror shows you down to your knees.
- less of yourself.
- exactly the same amount.
???
Exactly the same. To see your feet, light from your feet has to reach your eyes after one bounce, and by the equal-angles rule it bounces off the mirror exactly halfway between the height of your feet and the height of your eyes. That halfway point does not move when you step back; only the angle changes. A mirror shows all of you if it is half your height, wherever you stand. A person 1.50 m tall needs a mirror 0.75 m tall, and it must hang with its top at eye level and its bottom halfway down to the floor. Stepping back shows you more of the *room*, never more of yourself.
:::

:::try Investigation 10.3
Put the object 20 cm in front of the mirror and find the image. Move the object to 40 cm and 10 cm. Record both distances each time. Then write one sentence about what the mirror does to distance, and one about what it does to left and right.
:::

Not every mirror is flat. Look at your face in the back of a steel spoon and it is small and the right way up. Turn the spoon round and look into the bowl: from arm's length you are upside down, and as you bring the spoon close your face flips upright and grows. The curve changes where the reflected rays go, and so where they seem to come from. The rule at each tiny patch of the spoon is still the same one: angle out equals angle in.

:::school
Your syllabus asks you to "define and use the terms normal, angle of incidence" and angle of reflection. Both angles are measured from the normal, the line at right angles to the mirror. Measure from the glass instead and every answer comes out wrong.
:::

:::careful
> "My reflection is on the surface of the mirror, like a picture stuck to it."

It is behind the mirror, as far behind as you are in front. Touch the glass and your fingertip meets its image, because your fingertip is *at* the glass. Your face is an arm's length away, so its image is an arm's length behind. If the image really sat on the surface, a camera focused sharply on a smudge on the glass would also show your face sharp. Try it with a phone: the camera has to refocus between the smudge and the face, because they are at different distances.
:::

:::check
Your friend stands 1.5 m in front of a flat mirror. How far from her is her image? She steps back another 0.5 m. How far is it now?
???
The image is as far behind the mirror as she is in front: 1.5 m behind, so 3.0 m from her. After stepping back she is 2.0 m from the mirror, her image is 2.0 m behind it, and the distance between them is 4.0 m. The image moved back by the same amount she did.
:::

## 10.4 Bending light

Stand at the side of a tank of water in an aquarium and look at a fish near the glass. Look up through the surface and you may see the fish a second time, in a different place. Wade into a swimming pool and look down: your legs are short and your feet are nearer the surface than they should be. Stand a pencil in a glass of water and look at it from the side: it looks broken at the waterline. In all three cases, light has changed direction on its way from the thing to your eye, and your eye, as always, has traced the light straight back to a place where the thing is not.

Before the explanation, commit to an answer.

:::predict
You are standing at the edge of a clear pool and see a shell on the bottom. You reach straight for where you see it. Where is the shell really?
- Exactly where you see it.
- Nearer the surface than it looks.
- Deeper than it looks.
???
Deeper than it looks. Looking straight down, the water makes everything look shallower by a factor of about three-quarters: a shell under 10 cm of water appears to be under about 7.5 cm, and a pool floor 2 m down looks about 1.5 m down. People hurt their necks diving into unfamiliar ponds for exactly this reason. The shell has not moved. Where it *appears* has.
:::

The cause is a change of speed. In air, light travels at very nearly 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. In water it travels at 2.25 × 10⁸ m/s, three-quarters as fast. When light crosses from air into water at a slant, one side of the beam reaches the water first and slows down first, while the other side is still moving fast in the air. The fast side swings round the slow side, the way a line of marching people turns if those at one end slow down, and the whole beam bends towards the normal. Leaving the water, the opposite happens: the side that gets out first speeds up first and the beam bends away from the normal. The path is the same in both directions. This bending is called ==refraction==, and it happens wherever light changes speed: air to water, air to glass, water to glass, cool air to hot air.

:::note Refraction
The change in a light ray's direction when it passes from one material into another, because its speed changes. Slowing down bends the ray towards the normal; speeding up bends it away.
:::

:::five Refraction
? What is it?
The bending of a wave when it crosses at an angle from one medium into another in which its speed is different.
? Why is it called this?
From Latin *refractus*, broken back, because a straight stick or pencil standing at a slant in water looks broken at the waterline.
? What problem did it solve?
It explained why pools look shallower than they are, why lenses can bring light to a focus, why swimming underwater blurs your vision, and why hot road tarmac shimmers with mirages.
? Why does it work?
When a wavefront meets a slower medium at a slant, the edge of the wave that enters first slows down first while the other edge keeps travelling faster outside, swinging the whole wave round towards the normal.
? How does it help?
From a material's refractive index (*n* = *c*/*v*) you can calculate how much any surface bends light, design spectacle and camera lenses, and trap light inside optical fibres by total internal reflection.
?? How do we know?
Shine a narrow beam of light from air into a flat-sided tank of water or glass block with a protractor underneath: the beam bends towards the normal on entering and bends back by the exact same angle on leaving.
?? What it isn't
The water does not pull on the light, and the underwater coin or fish has not floated upward; only the light's path has bent at the boundary.
?? Where does it stop working?
A ray meeting the boundary straight on along the normal slows down without bending at all, and inside a single medium whose refractive index varies slightly with wavelength, different colours bend by slightly different amounts (dispersion, which Chapter 11 uses to split white light).
:::

:::fig c10_refraction() | A coin under water, seen from above and to one side. Light from the coin bends at the surface: 32° to the normal in the water becomes 45° in the air. Your eye traces the arriving ray straight back (dashed) to a place higher and nearer than the coin. The coin has not moved; its image has.
:::live refraction

How much does it bend? For light going from air into water, a ray arriving at 45° to the normal continues at 32°; one arriving at 30° continues at 22°. The exact rule was found in 984 by Ibn Sahl, and is usually named after Snell, who found it again about six centuries later; at school you will meet it as a rule about sines. The number that fixes how strongly a material bends light is its ==refractive index==, written *n*: the speed of light in empty space divided by its speed in the material. For water *n* = 1.333, for ordinary glass about 1.52, for ice 1.31 and for diamond 2.42. It is always more than 1, because light never travels faster in any material than it does in empty space. The index also tells you the apparent depth when you look straight down: real depth divided by *n*, which is why 10 cm of water looks like 7.5 cm.

:::eq
**refractive index**: *n* = *c* ÷ *v*
:::

:::note Refractive index (*n*)
How much a material slows light: the speed of light in empty space divided by its speed in the material. Water is 1.333, glass about 1.52. A bigger index bends light more.
:::

Two more things follow from the same rule. Send light the other way, from water towards air, and it bends away from the normal: at a steep enough slant there is no direction in the air for it to take, and the surface acts as a perfect mirror from below. For water this happens beyond 48.6° from the normal. Swimmers see it as a silvery ceiling, and optical fibres, thin glass threads that carry light round corners, work by trapping light this way. And on a hot May afternoon the tarmac of a Mumbai road heats the air just above it, so that light from the sky, passing from cooler air into the thinner hot air near the ground, bends upwards into your eye. You see sky where the road should be, and it looks like a pool of water. That is a mirage, and it is refraction too.

:::try Investigation 10.4
Set the ray to arrive at 45° from the air and read the angle in the water. Then send the ray up from the water and increase its angle slowly. Find the angle at which it stops getting out. Write down both numbers.
:::

:::school
Your syllabus explains refraction "in terms of change of speed", and that is the right way. Some books say light bends when it enters a "denser medium". They mean a material that is slower for light, which is not the same as heavier: diamond slows light far more than water does, but the word to use is speed.
:::

:::careful
> "The water pulls the light down. And the coin floats up to where I see it."

Nothing pulls. Light slows down in water, and slowing one side of a beam before the other swings it round, which is all that bending is. And the coin stays exactly where it was. Only its image moves, because your eye traces the bent ray straight back to a place the coin is not. Reach for the image and your fingers close on water.
:::

:::check
Does a ray bend towards the normal or away from it when it goes (a) from air into water, (b) from water into glass, (c) from glass into air?
???
Towards the normal whenever the light slows down, away whenever it speeds up. Air (1.00) to water (1.333): slower, so towards. Water (1.333) to glass (1.52): slower again, so towards. Glass (1.52) to air (1.00): faster, so away from the normal.
:::

## 10.5 Lenses and the eye

Put a single drop of water on a sheet of clear plastic, lay it on a newspaper, and look down through the drop. The letters under it are bigger. A drop of water is a lens: a curved surface that bends light, and bends it more at the edges than in the middle, so that rays spread apart by the small letters are gathered together again before they reach your eye. Every lens in the world, from a magnifying glass to a telescope to the one in your head, is doing this one job: gathering light that is spreading out and bringing it back together.

Hold a magnifying glass in sunlight and it gathers the Sun's parallel rays to one bright, hot point, the ==focal point==. Never do this towards your eyes, or anyone's, and never look at the Sun through any glass. Do this instead: in a dim room, hold the magnifier a hand's width from a wall opposite a bright window and move it slowly back and forth. At one distance a small, sharp picture of the window appears on the wall, upside down, exactly like the pinhole camera's but far brighter, because the whole width of the lens is collecting light and delivering it to the picture. Now test an idea.

:::note Focal point
The point where a converging lens brings parallel rays together. The distance from the lens to it is the focal length. A fatter, more curved lens has a shorter focal length.
:::

:::predict
With the window's picture sharp on the wall, you cover the top half of the lens with a card. What happens to the picture?
- The top half of the picture disappears.
- The bottom half of the picture disappears.
- The whole picture stays, but fainter.
???
The whole picture stays, fainter. Light from every point of the window passes through every part of the lens, and every part bends it to the same spot on the wall. Covering half the lens removes half the light from each point of the picture, and none of the picture itself. The lens is a collector, and you have made it a smaller collector.
:::

Your eye is a camera obscura with a lens at the front and a living screen at the back. Light enters through the ==cornea==, the clear curved window at the very front, passes through the pupil (the hole in the coloured iris), then through the lens, and lands on the ==retina==, the layer of light-sensitive cells lining the back of the eyeball. There it forms a real picture: small, sharp, and upside down, just like the pinhole camera's. You have never seen the world the right way up. Your brain learned long ago to read the upside-down picture as the world, and it does so without telling you.

:::note Retina
The layer of light-sensitive cells at the back of the eye. The cornea and lens form a small upside-down image on it, and nerves carry the pattern to the brain.
:::

:::fig c10_eye() | The eye, from the side, forming an image of a distant tree. The cornea does about two-thirds of the bending, because air to cornea is the biggest change of speed on the way in; the lens fine-tunes the rest. The image on the retina is real and upside down. Schematic: the bends are drawn sharper than they are.
:::live eye3d

Here is a surprise. The lens does less than a third of the work. The biggest bend happens at the cornea, because that is where the biggest change of speed happens: light goes from air, where the index is 1.00, into the cornea, where it is 1.38. The lens (index about 1.41) sits in watery fluid (index 1.34), so the change of speed there is small. What the lens is for is adjustment. Look from your watch to the Moon and a ring of muscle round the lens relaxes, letting the lens flatten so that the picture of the far-off Moon lands sharply on the retina; look back at your watch and the muscle tightens, the lens fattens, and it bends the light from the nearby watch more. This is ==accommodation==, and the closest point your eye can focus on, about 25 cm for a typical eye, is where the lens is as fat as it can get. Opticians measure a lens's strength in dioptres: one dioptre is a focal length of one metre, two dioptres is half a metre, and so on. The eye's cornea and lens together are about 50 dioptres looking far away and about 54 looking at a book 25 cm away, a change of 8%. (That word *power* on a spectacle prescription means dioptres. It is not the power you met in Chapter 7, measured in watts. One word, two meanings.)

:::note Accommodation
The eye changing the shape of its lens to keep the picture sharp: fatter for near things, flatter for far ones. A ring of muscle round the lens does the work.
:::

The cornea's two-thirds explains a thing every swimmer knows. Open your eyes underwater and everything is a blur. The cornea's index is 1.38 and water's is 1.33, so the change of speed at the cornea, which was 0.38 in air, drops to 0.05 in water, and the cornea hardly bends the light at all. The lens cannot make up the difference. A swimming mask works by putting air back in front of your eyes.

:::try Investigation 10.5
Focus the eye on the far tree, then on the near book, and watch what the lens does. Then cover half the pupil. Predict first whether the picture on the retina loses its top, its bottom, or only some brightness.
:::

Many eyes bend light a little too much or too little. If your eye brings light to a sharp point *before* it reaches the retina, far things blur (short sight), and a lens that spreads the light slightly, a diverging lens, puts the point back on the retina. If your eye bends too little, near things blur (long sight), and a converging lens helps it. Glasses do nothing to the eye itself; they adjust the light before it arrives. And an eye that needs no glasses is better called ideal than normal, since so many eyes do.

:::careful
> "The lens in my eye does the focusing."

Mostly it is the cornea, the clear front window, which does about two-thirds of the bending because the jump in speed from air into it is the biggest on the way. The lens fine-tunes: it fattens for near things and flattens for far ones. Proof of the cornea's share: underwater, where the jump at the cornea almost vanishes, everything blurs, though your lens is working exactly as before.
:::

:::check
Why is everything blurry when you open your eyes underwater, and why does a swimming mask fix it?
???
Most of your eye's focusing happens where light passes from air (index 1.00) into the cornea (1.38). In water (1.33) the change in index at the cornea falls from 0.38 to 0.05, so the cornea hardly bends the light, and the lens alone cannot bring the picture to a point on the retina. A mask holds a layer of air against your face, so light again enters the cornea from air and the cornea gets its two-thirds back.
:::

## 10.6 How fast is light?

In a monsoon storm, the lightning comes first and the thunder after. Count the seconds between them: sound in warm air travels at about 340 m/s, so every three seconds is about a kilometre. The flash arrives so soon that it seems to arrive instantly. Does that prove light is extremely fast? Only that it is much faster than sound. In 1638 Galileo could say only that light was extremely fast, perhaps instantaneous. "Too fast to measure" is where things stood for nearly forty years, until an astronomer noticed that a moon was running late.

:::predict
Suppose the Sun switched off at this instant. How long before Mumbai goes dark?
- At the same instant.
- About eight minutes later.
- About a day later.
???
About eight minutes later. The Sun is 149,600,000 km away, and light covers 299,792 km every second, so the light reaching you now left the Sun 499 seconds ago, about 8.3 minutes. For eight more minutes after the switch-off, the sky over Mumbai would look exactly as it does now. You never see the Sun as it is; you see it as it was.
:::

:::scene Paris Observatory, 1676

Jupiter has moons, and one of the four Galileo had found in 1610, Io, slips into Jupiter's shadow on a schedule as regular as a railway timetable. The Danish astronomer Ole Rømer, working at the new observatory in Paris, timed these eclipses over months and found the timetable drifting. While the Earth, on its own orbit, was moving away from Jupiter, each eclipse came a little later than the last; while the Earth was moving towards Jupiter, each came a little earlier. Rømer saw what it meant. Light from Io was taking time to reach the Earth, and the farther away the Earth was, the longer it took. In September 1676 he announced that the eclipse due that November would come about ten minutes later than the tables predicted. It did.

:::evidence Io runs late | 1676
Rømer did not state a speed. He stated a time: in a letter the next year he put it at about 22 minutes for light to cross the full width of the Earth's orbit. His friend Christiaan Huygens did the arithmetic that turned the time into a speed, and got an answer well short of the true one, because Rømer's 22 minutes was too long. Light takes 998 seconds, about 16.6 minutes, to cross the Earth's orbit, so his estimate was a third too long, and the speed it gives, 2.27 × 10⁸ m/s, is about a quarter short. Textbooks often credit him with a number. The honest version is better: in 1676 a man with a telescope and a clock showed that light takes time, and estimated how much, from the timetable of a distant moon.
:::

:::fig c10_romer() | Why Io's eclipses run late. Light from Io has to cross the extra width of the Earth's orbit when the Earth is on the far side of the Sun: about 300 million km, which takes light 16.6 minutes. Rømer estimated 22. Not to scale: Jupiter is far more distant than shown.

:::story speed-of-light

:::scene Suresnes, 1849

For a hundred and seventy years the speed of light was known only from the sky. In 1849 Hippolyte Fizeau measured it across Paris. He set a lamp behind a spinning wheel with 720 teeth on the hill at Suresnes, and a mirror on Montmartre about 8.6 km away. Light flashed out through a gap between teeth, crossed to the mirror, and came back. If the wheel was spinning fast enough, the gap it had left through had moved on, and the returning light hit a tooth and was blocked. The round trip is 17 km, which light covers in 58 millionths of a second, so the wheel had to turn about a dozen times a second before the light was blocked. From the speed of his wheel Fizeau got about 315,000 km/s, 5% too high. Thirteen years later Léon Foucault did it with a spinning mirror and came within 0.6%, and Albert Michelson refined the method for nearly fifty years, until 1926.

Today the speed of light is not measured at all. Since 1983 it has been fixed by international agreement at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, and the metre is defined from it: a metre is the distance light travels in empty space in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The old question has been turned inside out. We no longer ask how fast light goes in metres; we define the metre by how far light goes.

:::note Speed of light (*c*)
299,792,458 m/s in empty space, exactly, by definition since 1983. About 3 × 10⁸ m/s. In water light travels at three-quarters of this.
:::

What does that speed feel like? Across a 5 m room, light takes 17 billionths of a second: too fast to notice, though not instant. To the Moon, 384,400 km away, it takes 1.28 s. When astronauts stood on the Moon, a question asked from Earth took 1.28 s to arrive and the answer 1.28 s to come back, so every reply came after a gap of about two and a half seconds. The same delay is still measured today: the Apollo crews left mirrors on the Moon, and laser pulses bounced off them time the round trip, which is how the Moon's distance is measured. To the Sun, as you predicted, 8.3 minutes. Light is the fastest thing there is, and even light needs time.

:::recall 9
Sunlight takes 8.3 minutes to reach your skin, and you feel its warmth. Does anything from the Sun's surface travel to you along with the light?
???
No material does. The Sun's hot gas stays at the Sun, just as the water of a wave stays in the sea while the wave crosses it. What arrives is energy, carried by the light wave, and you feel that energy as warmth. Light is the clearest case yet of Chapter 9's rule: a wave carries energy without carrying stuff, and this one does it across 150 million kilometres of empty space.
:::

:::careful
> "Light is instant. Flick the switch and the room is lit at the same moment."

Too fast to notice is not the same as instant. Light crosses a room in a few billionths of a second, crosses the Earth's orbit in 16.6 minutes and reaches us from the Sun 8.3 minutes after leaving it. The switch and the far wall are lit at very slightly different moments, and with a fast enough clock you could measure the gap. Fizeau did, in effect, with a wheel.
:::

:::check
Lightning flashes, and you count six seconds before the thunder. How far away did it strike? Does the time the light took matter in your sum?
???
Sound at about 340 m/s covers about 2,000 m in six seconds, so the strike was about 2 km away. The light from the flash covered that 2 km in about seven millionths of a second, far less than the time it took you to blink, so for this sum you can treat the flash as arriving the instant it happened. Compared with sound, light is as good as instant. Compared with Fizeau's wheel, it is not.
:::

## The disappearing coin

:::case The disappearing coin
A steel tumbler stands on the kitchen table, 8 cm deep and empty. You put a ₹1 coin on the bottom, against the far wall. You sit down and lower your head until the near rim of the tumbler just hides the coin: your line of sight skims the rim at 45° and reaches the bottom 8 cm beyond the near wall, which is exactly where the coin lies. You do not move your head at all. A friend slowly pours water into the tumbler, right up to the brim.

The coin comes back into view.

- The coin did not move: it is heavy and sits where you put it.
- Your head did not move.
- The tumbler did not move.

So what changed, and where did the coin appear?
???
The light did. Before, light from the coin had to travel in a straight line to your eye, and the rim was in the way. Now the light leaves the coin, travels up through the water, and bends at the surface as it comes out into the air, away from the normal. A ray that leaves the coin at 32° to the vertical comes out of the water at 45°, which is exactly the angle that skims the rim and reaches your eye.

The sum, line by line:

- Empty tumbler: the 45° sightline reaches the bottom 8 cm × tan 45° = 8.0 cm from the near wall.
- Full tumbler: the same sightline, bent to 32° in the water, reaches 8 cm × tan 32° = 5.0 cm from the near wall.
- So the strip of bottom you can now see has grown by 8.0 − 5.0 = 3.0 cm.

Any coin lying in that extra 3 cm strip of bottom appears. Your eye traces the arriving ray straight back along its 45° line, so the coin seems to lie higher up and nearer to you than it is, as though it had floated. It has not. Dip your finger in and you will find it exactly where you put it. The water did not lift the coin. It bent the light.
:::

It also said nothing about colour, though colour was in every scene: the sky behind the camera obscura's window was blue, the sunset over the sea is orange, and the sea itself is blue for reasons that took a voyage from England to Bombay to settle.
