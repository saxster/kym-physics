Chapter 10 followed light as a set of rays: lines that go straight, bounce off mirrors, bend in water and come to a point in a lens. The rays built a camera and explained your eye, and they left two questions untouched. Every ray in that chapter was *white* light, and nothing in it said what light actually is.

Stand on the sea wall at Marine Drive at about six in the evening and both questions are all around you. Overhead the sky is blue. Out over the Arabian Sea the Sun is going down in orange and gold. The sea in front of you is blue, and it is a different blue from the sky's. Three colours, three places, one Sun. Is it the same reason each time? By the end of this chapter you will know. You will also know something that Newton, who took white light apart, refused to believe: that light is a wave. The proof involves a card held in a sunbeam, a spot of light that a great mathematician said could not exist, and a letter posted at Bombay.

## 11.1 White is a mixture

:::scene Cambridge, 1666

Isaac Newton, twenty-three years old, darkened his room, made a small hole in the window shutter, and let a thin beam of sunlight cross the room to the far wall. Then he put a glass prism in the beam. The white spot on the wall stretched into a band of colours: red at one end, through yellow and green and blue, to violet at the other. People had seen a prism's colours for centuries. The obvious explanation, the one most people reach for, is that the glass *stains* the light: white light goes in pure, and the glass adds colours to it.

Newton did not trust the obvious explanation, so he designed a test for it. Six years later, in a letter to the Royal Society dated 6 February 1672, he called it his *experimentum crucis*, the decisive experiment. Before you read what he found, decide what you expect.

:::predict
Newton lets the band of colours fall on a board with a small hole in it, so that only the red light passes through. He puts a second prism in that red beam. What comes out of the second prism?
- A new band of colours. The second prism adds colours just as the first did.
- Light of a different colour: the red has been changed again.
- Red light, bent, and nothing else.
???
Red light, bent, and nothing else. The second prism bends the red beam but adds no colour to it and takes none away. Newton tried each colour in turn and got the same result. So the prism cannot be adding anything. The colours were in the sunlight all along, mixed together so that they looked white, and the prism only sorts them. White light is a mixture. That was the point of the letter.
:::

What sorts them is the thing you met in Section 10.4. Light slows down in glass or water, and the amount it slows depends slightly on its colour. In water the refractive index is 1.331 for red light and 1.342 for violet; in ordinary glass from 1.512 to 1.530. Violet is slowed most, so violet is bent most, and a beam of white light, which is every colour travelling together, fans out as it crosses the surface. The fan is narrow: a beam entering water at 45° comes out at 32.09° for red and 31.80° for violet, a spread of about a third of a degree. That is why a glass of water on a sunny windowsill throws only a thin smear of colour onto the floor, and why Newton needed a long dark room to stretch the colours apart. This sorting of white light by colour is ==dispersion==, and the band of colours it makes is a ==spectrum==.

:::note Dispersion
The spreading of white light into its colours, because a material slows each wavelength by a slightly different amount. Violet is slowed and bent most, red least.
:::

:::note Spectrum
The band of colours that white light spreads into, red to violet. It is continuous: the colours shade into each other with no lines between them. Naming seven of them is a habit; the light itself has no lines in it.
:::

:::fig c11_prism() | Newton's decisive experiment. White light enters the first prism and fans into a spectrum: red is bent least, violet most. A hole lets only the red through to a second prism, and the red comes out red, bent but otherwise unchanged. The colours were in the white light already. Schematic: the separation of the colours is enlarged for clarity.

What is it about red light and violet light that makes water treat them differently? The answer, which Newton never knew, is the one Chapter 9 gave you for sound: the spacing of the wave's crests. Red light has a wavelength of about 660 nanometres, violet about 410, and the whole visible range runs from about 400 to 700 nm, less than a thousandth of a millimetre. Sunlight is a fairly even mixture of all of them. It looks slightly yellow, but every colour is in it, which is why a lemon, a leaf and a blue door can all show their colours under the same Sun.

A rainbow is dispersion by millions of prisms at once. Sunlight enters a raindrop, is bent and sorted at the surface, bounces off the inside of the back of the drop, and is bent again on the way out, so the colours come back out towards the Sun's side of the sky. That is why you see a rainbow only with the Sun behind you. Each drop sends you a single colour, and together the drops paint the bow. Around 1300 the scholars al-Farisi and Theodoric of Freiberg each worked this out with a glass sphere of water as a giant raindrop, building on Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics*; al-Farisi put his sphere inside a camera obscura so he could follow the beams in the dark. On a Mumbai balcony you can make your own: with the Sun behind you, spray a fine mist from a plant sprayer and look into it. A small rainbow stands in the spray.

One more rainbow belongs here, because it is a trap. Tilt a CD in sunlight and it throws colours too, and it looks like the same trick. It is a different one. A CD has no prism in it; its colours come from thousands of fine grooves close together, and the order of colours comes out the other way round from a prism's. Section 11.5 explains why, and it needs the rest of this chapter first.

:::school
Your syllabus calls this topic "dispersion", and it asks for the order: red is bent least and violet most. Whether you say "deviated" or "bent", the colours leave the prism in the order red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet.
:::

:::careful
> "White light is the pure one. The prism adds colours to it."

The second prism settles it. If prisms added colour, the red light passing through the second prism would come out with new colours, and it comes out red. Each colour was in the white light already; "white" is what the whole mixture looks like to your eye. The prism only sorts the mixture, because it slows each colour by a slightly different amount.
:::

:::story prism

:::check
Why can you only ever see a rainbow with the Sun behind you?
???
The light that makes a rainbow enters a raindrop, is bent, reflects off the inside of the back of the drop, and is bent again on the way out. After that bounce it is travelling back towards the Sun's side of the sky. So the only drops that can send colour to your eye are the ones on the opposite side of the sky from the Sun, which puts the Sun behind you.
:::

## 11.2 Colour is in the light

:::recall 10.1
A friend says your eyes "see colour" the way a torch shines: the eye sends out something that colours what it looks at. Where is the colour of a ripe mango actually decided?
???
Nothing leaves the eye, as the sealed dark room showed. The mango's colour is decided by two things: which wavelengths of the arriving light the mango's skin sends back and which it absorbs, and how the three kinds of colour-sensing cell in your eye respond to the mix that reaches them. Change the light that arrives and the mango's colour changes, as this section shows.
:::

A ripe mango is yellow. A leaf is green. A red kurta is red, and most people would say it is red whatever light you shine on it: red is simply what it is. This section is about why that is only half true, and the test is one you can do tonight.

:::predict
In a dark room, you light a green leaf with nothing but a phone screen showing pure red. What colour does the leaf look?
- Green. A leaf is green.
- Red, like everything else in the room.
- Black, or very nearly.
???
Black, or very nearly. A leaf looks green in daylight because its surface sends back the green part of the light that reaches it and absorbs most of the rest. Red light has no green in it, so there is nothing for the leaf to send back, and it absorbs most of the red. Nothing much reaches your eye from the leaf, and nothing much is what black means. A phone screen's red is not perfectly pure, so expect "dark and strange" rather than perfectly black.
:::

Here is the rule. An object does two things to the light that reaches it: it absorbs some wavelengths and sends the rest back, in every direction, by the diffuse reflection of Section 10.1. The colour you see is the part that comes back. A banana in sunlight absorbs the blue and sends back the red and the green, and that mixture, red together with green, looks yellow to you. Under red light the same banana can send back only red, so it looks red. Under blue light it can send back nothing, and looks black. The banana has not changed. Its "true colour" is a description of what it does to white light. What you actually see depends on the light that arrives *and* on what the banana does with it.

:::fig c11_banana() | The same banana under three lights. Under white light it absorbs blue and sends back red and green, which together look yellow. Under red light only red can come back. Under blue light nothing comes back, and the banana looks black. The banana does the same thing each time; the light arriving is different.
:::live colourmix

:::home A banana by phone-light
In a room as dark as you can make it, open a plain, full-screen red image on a phone, or any app that fills the screen with red, and turn the brightness up. Put a banana, a green leaf and something blue on the table, and light them with the phone from a hand's width away. Say what colour each looks before you check with the room light. Then try a blue screen, and a white one. If you have a magnifying glass, or the water-drop lens from Section 10.5, look closely at the white screen: it is made of tiny red, green and blue dots, with no white dot anywhere.
:::

Now the eye's side of the deal. The retina has three kinds of colour-sensing cell, called cones, and each kind responds to a broad, overlapping range of wavelengths: roughly, one kind to 400–500 nm, one to 450–630 nm and one to 500–700 nm. Every colour you have ever seen is your brain's reading of just three signals: how strongly each kind of cone is firing. Pure yellow light, at about 580 nm, makes the "green" cones and the "red" cones fire together. So does a mixture of pure red light and pure green light, with no yellow in it at all, and your brain cannot tell the two apart. Both look yellow. That is why a screen needs only three colours of dot. Red, green and blue dots, lit in the right proportions, can make your cones fire the way any colour would, and all three together make white.

Mixing light this way is ==additive==: each colour you add puts more light into the mix, and adding them all gives white. Mixing paint is the opposite, and this is where the school primary colours come from. A paint works by *taking colours out* of the white light falling on it. Cyan paint absorbs red and sends back blue and green; yellow paint absorbs blue and sends back red and green. Mix the two and the light must survive both: red is removed by the cyan, blue by the yellow, and only green comes back, darker than either paint, because each paint subtracts. That is ==subtractive== mixing. The primary colours of paint (the printer's cyan, magenta and yellow, or the schoolroom's red, yellow and blue) are not the primary colours of light, which are red, green and blue.

:::note Additive and subtractive mixing
Mixing *light* adds: red + green + blue light gives white, and red + green gives yellow. Mixing *paint* subtracts: each paint removes some colours from the light, so cyan + yellow paint gives green, and mixing all the paints gives something near black.
:::

:::try Investigation 11.2
Before you touch the controls, write down what colour red light and blue light will make together, and what cyan and magenta paint will make. Then mix them and compare. Which of your two guesses was based on paintbox experience?
:::

There is one more trick in your head, and it is a good one. A white kurta looks white under the yellow light of an old bulb, under the blue light of an overcast sky and under a tube light, although the light coming off it is quite different each time. Your brain compares the kurta with everything around it and works out what the light must be, then corrects for it. This is ==colour constancy==, and it is why you hardly ever notice the colour of the light in a room. It fails when there is nothing to compare: light a scene with a single colour, as you did with the banana, and the correction has nothing to work with.

:::note Colour constancy
The brain's habit of judging an object's colour against its surroundings, so that a white shirt looks white under lamps of different colours. It fails under light of a single colour.
:::

:::school
Cambridge Lower Secondary calls this "adding and subtracting colours". Adding is for light (red, green and blue make white); subtracting is for paints and filters (each one removes colours). Keep the two lists apart, because the answers come out different.
:::

:::careful
> "A red shirt is red whatever the light. Colour is just what things are."

Colour is what a thing does to the light it is given. A red shirt absorbs most colours and sends back red, so in white light it looks red. Give it only blue light and there is no red to send back: it looks black. Give it red light and it looks much as it always does, because red is what it sends back anyway. Its "true" colour is really a statement about white light, and most of the time that is the light we have.
:::

:::check
A spot of red light and a spot of green light overlap on a white wall. What colour is the overlap, and why is it not the muddy brown you get from mixing red and green paint?
???
Yellow. Both lights reach your eye, so your "red" and "green" cones fire together, which is exactly what yellow light makes them do. Paints subtract: red paint removes green and blue, green paint removes red and blue, and together they remove nearly everything, so the mixture is dark and dull. Light adds; paint takes away.
:::

## 11.3 Blue sky, orange sunset

:::scene Marine Drive, this evening

The Sun is low over the Arabian Sea, orange and swollen. Overhead the sky is still blue. If you ask ten people why, you will hear that the sky is blue because it reflects the sea, that the sunset is orange because the Sun is cooling for the night, and that Mumbai's sunsets are orange because of the traffic. The first two are wrong and the third is unproven, and the real reason is the same for the blue overhead and the orange at the horizon.

Start with what light does to air. Light is a wave, and the wave shakes the tiny electric charges, the electrons, inside each molecule of air as it passes. A shaken charge sends out light of its own, in every direction, like a tiny radio antenna. So every molecule the sunlight passes becomes a faint new source of light, re-sending a little of what reached it in all directions. Physicists call this ==scattering==. Most of the sunlight passes straight through; only a tiny share is re-sent. But the air is deep, and that tiny share is the whole sky.

:::note Scattering
Light that is re-sent in all directions by the small things it passes: here, the molecules of air. Short wavelengths are scattered much more than long ones, so air scatters blue far more than red.
:::

:::five Rayleigh scattering
? What is it?
The redirection of light in all directions by particles much smaller than the wavelength of the light, which scatters short wavelengths (blue and violet) far more strongly than long ones (orange and red).
? Why is it called this?
Named after Lord Rayleigh, who worked out in 1871 that when small particles scatter light, the scattered intensity scales as one over the fourth power of the wavelength (1/λ⁴).
? What problem did it solve?
It explained two everyday mysteries with a single rule: why the daytime sky away from the Sun is blue, and why the Sun itself turns yellow, orange and red when seen through a long slant of air at sunset.
? Why does it work?
A passing light wave shakes the electrons in each air molecule, and an oscillating charge radiates light like a tiny antenna; higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength) oscillations radiate much more strongly, so blue at 450 nm is scattered nearly six times as strongly as red at 700 nm.
? How does it help?
It explains the colour of the sky, the redness of a totally eclipsed Moon lit by light bent through the Earth's rim of air, and why Raman could argue that water molecules themselves give the deep sea its blue.
?? How do we know?
Shine a white torch beam through a glass of water faintly clouded with a drop of milk: looked at from the side, the scattered light is bluish; looked at end-on through the glass, the remaining beam is yellow-orange.
?? What it isn't
Air is not blue like tinted glass (if it were, the noon Sun seen through it would look blue), and the sky is not blue because it reflects the sea.
?? Where does it stop working?
Once the scattering particles are as big as or bigger than the wavelength of light, such as water droplets in a cloud or fog, the 1/λ⁴ rule no longer holds; all visible wavelengths scatter about equally and the cloud looks white or grey.
:::

The share that is re-sent depends very steeply on the wavelength. For things much smaller than the wavelength, as air molecules are, the amount scattered goes as one over the wavelength to the fourth power: halve the wavelength and you get sixteen times the scattering. Compare blue light at 450 nm with red at 700 nm:

:::work
700 ÷ 450 = 1.56
1.56⁴ = 1.56 × 1.56 × 1.56 × 1.56 = 5.86
so blue light is scattered about six times as strongly as red
:::

Now look anywhere in the sky except at the Sun itself. The light reaching your eye from that patch of empty air cannot have come straight from the Sun, because the Sun is somewhere else. It is light that was re-sent your way by air molecules, and since air re-sends blue about six times as readily as red, that light is mostly blue. The sky is blue because you are looking at scattered sunlight, and air scatters blue best. Inland, a thousand kilometres from any sea, the sky is exactly as blue.

:::predict
Astronauts on the Moon stood in full sunlight, with no air above them at all. What colour was the daytime sky over their heads?
- Blue, like ours.
- A paler, whiter blue.
- Black, with the Sun blazing in it.
???
Black, with the Sun blazing in it. There is no air to scatter the sunlight sideways into your eyes, so the only direction light arrives from is the Sun itself. Everywhere else in the sky there is nothing to see, which is black. Blue sky needs air. It is the air you are looking at.
:::

At sunset the same mechanism works the other way. The Sun's light now reaches you through a far longer stretch of air, because it comes in at a slant. Treating the air as a flat layer, a sunbeam from 30° above the horizon crosses twice as much air as one from overhead, one from 10° about six times as much, and one from 5° about eleven times (the true numbers near the horizon are a little smaller, because the Earth curves). All along that path the air scatters the blue out of the beam, sideways, into other people's skies. By the time the light reaches you most of the blue is gone, and what is left of the direct beam is the colours air scatters least: orange and red. The Sun has not cooled by a single degree. The blue overhead and the orange at the horizon are the same six-to-one rule seen from two sides: the blue is what the air took out of the sunlight, and the orange is what it left in.

:::fig c11_sky() | One rule, two colours. At noon (left), sunlight crosses a short stretch of air, and the sky all round you is the blue it scattered sideways. At sunset (right), the beam crosses a much longer stretch, most of the blue is scattered away before it arrives, and the direct light that remains is orange. Not to scale, and the air layer is drawn far thicker than it is.
:::live skycolour

You might ask why the sky is not violet, since violet, at 400 nm, scatters even more than blue: 1.6 times as much. Two things seem to work against it, as far as physicists can tell: sunlight carries less violet than blue to begin with, and your eyes are far less sensitive to violet. The blue wins on both counts.

:::home A sky in a glass
Fill a clear glass with water and stir in a few drops of milk, enough to make the water very faintly cloudy; if it goes white, start again with more water. Take it into a dark room with a torch. Shine the torch at the glass from one side and look at the glass from another side: the water has a faint bluish tinge. Now look at the torch *through* the glass: the beam coming through is yellowish-orange. The milk droplets are playing the part of the air, scattering the shorter wavelengths sideways and letting the longer ones through. It is a model of the sky, not the sky itself: milk droplets are much bigger than air molecules, so the six-to-one rule does not hold exactly, but the two colours come out the right way round.
:::

One more thing is hidden in the scattered light, and the next section needs it. Light scattered sideways by air is partly polarised: the wave's sideways shaking has been given a preferred direction. With polarised sunglasses, look at blue sky about a quarter of the way round from the Sun and slowly rotate the glasses: the sky darkens and brightens as you turn. Ordinary tinted sunglasses do nothing of the kind. What polarisation is, and what it has to do with the sea, comes next.

:::try Investigation 11.3
Set the Sun straight overhead and read off the colour of the sky and of the Sun. Lower the Sun to 10° and then to 3°, and write down how the two colours change. Then switch the air off and say what you see.
:::

:::careful
> "The sky is blue because it reflects the sea."

Go to Nagpur, in the middle of the country and hundreds of kilometres from any sea, and the sky is just as blue. The sky's blue comes from the air itself scattering sunlight, and it arrives from every direction at once, including straight overhead, where no sea could be reflected. The sea, as it happens, has borrowed some of *its* blue from the sky, which is the mystery of the next section.
:::

:::check
A friend says the air is blue the way tinted glass is blue, so you see blue when you look through it. What is wrong with that picture?
???
Look *through* tinted blue glass at the Sun and the Sun looks blue. Look through the air at the Sun and it looks yellow-white at noon and orange at sunset: the air removes blue from the light that passes straight through it. The blue you see is in the *rest* of the sky, away from the Sun, where the removed blue has been re-sent towards you. That is scattering, and it is the opposite of tinting.
:::

## 11.4 Why the sea is blue

:::scene The Mediterranean, September 1921

The liner *Narkunda* is steaming east towards Bombay, and a young physicist from Calcutta is standing at the rail, looking at the sea through a small crystal. C. V. Raman is on his way home to India, and the Mediterranean is a deep, dark blue. He knows the standard explanation. Lord Rayleigh, the English physicist, had said that the sea is blue because it reflects the sky. Raman has with him a Nicol prism: a crystal cut so that it lets through only light whose sideways shaking lies in one direction. He is about to use it to take the sky out of the sea.

:::predict
Light reflected off the surface of water is polarised, as you are about to learn, so a Nicol prism held at the right angle can block the reflected sky almost completely. Raman holds the prism to his eye and turns it until the reflected sky is gone. What does the sea look like now?
- Grey or dark. Its blue was the sky's, and it has gone with the sky.
- Still blue. The water itself is sending blue light up to the eye.
???
Still blue. With the reflected sky removed, the sea's blue remained, so the colour must be coming from inside the water. Raman argued that the water was doing what the air does: its molecules scatter the sunlight that enters it, blue most of all, and send some of it back up. He wrote a letter to the journal *Nature* on board, and when the *Narkunda* docked at Bombay he posted it. The sea's blue is a cousin of the sky's, made in the water rather than above it.
:::

To see why the prism worked, you need one more fact about light as a wave. Sound, you learned in Chapter 9, is a push passed along the direction it travels. Light is the other kind of wave: its shaking is *sideways*, at right angles to the direction it travels, like the wave on a skipping rope. A sideways shake has a direction of its own, up-and-down or side-to-side or anything between, and that direction is the wave's ==polarisation==. Sunlight is a jumble of all of them. But some things sort the jumble. When light reflects off water or glass at a slant, the reflected part is mostly polarised side to side, parallel to the surface. That reflected light is the glare you see on the sea, on a wet road, on a windscreen.

:::note Polarisation
The direction of a light wave's sideways shaking. Sunlight shakes in every direction at once. Reflection off water sorts it: the glare off a surface shakes mostly parallel to the surface.
:::

A polarising filter, whether Raman's Nicol prism or the plastic in a pair of polarised sunglasses, lets through only the shaking that lines up with it. Polarised sunglasses are made to pass the up-and-down shaking and block side-to-side. So they block glare, which is side-to-side, while letting through most of the light from everything else, which is a jumble and loses only half. Turn the glasses through a right angle and the glare comes flooding back. Hold the filter at 45° to the light's shaking and half the polarised light gets through; at 90°, none.

:::fig c11_brewster() | Sunlight meeting the sea at Brewster's angle, 53° from the normal, which for someone on the Marine Drive wall means a line of sight about 37° below the horizon. At this angle the reflected glare is completely polarised, shaking parallel to the surface, and the reflected and refracted rays are at right angles to each other. A polarised lens, which passes only up-and-down shaking, removes that glare.

There is one angle at which the sorting is perfect. Light reflected off water at 53.1° from the normal is completely polarised, and at that angle the reflected ray and the ray going down into the water are at right angles to each other. That angle is called ==Brewster's angle==, and for the person on the sea wall it has a plain meaning: 53.1° from the vertical is 36.9° below the horizontal. Look at the sea about 37° below the horizon, roughly a third of the way from straight ahead to straight down, and that is where the glare is pure polarised light and a filter can remove all of it. Look at the water nearer or farther and the glare is only partly polarised, so the filter makes it bright and dim as you turn, without ever quite going black.

:::note Brewster's angle
The angle of incidence at which light reflected from a surface is completely polarised. For water it is 53.1° from the normal, and the reflected and refracted rays are then at right angles. It depends on the refractive index: tan θ = *n*.
:::

Now you can see what Raman did. From the ship's rail he was looking down at the sea at a steep slant, where the reflected sky was strongly polarised. His Nicol prism, turned to block side-to-side shaking, removed it. Whatever was left was light from inside the water, and it was blue. Only about 2% of sunlight falling straight down onto water reflects off the surface; the rest goes in, and what the water does with it decides the colour. Raman argued that water molecules scatter it, blue most, as the air does. The fuller modern account adds a second effect working the same way: water very faintly absorbs red light, so the deeper the light goes before coming back, the bluer what returns. Either way, the sea's blue is made in the sea.

:::evidence A crystal at the ship's rail | 1921
Raman's test is a model of how to settle an argument. Rayleigh's explanation, reflected sky, predicted that the sea's blue would vanish when the reflected sky was blocked. The Nicol prism blocked it. The blue stayed. One observation, made with a crystal the size of a thumb, ruled the explanation out. Raman wrote up what he had seen in a letter to *Nature* on the voyage and posted it when the ship reached Bombay. Seven years later the question of what water does to light led him and his colleague K. S. Krishnan to the discovery in Section 11.6.
:::

:::story raman

Richard Feynman, the American physicist, taught for a time in Brazil and told afterwards of a class there. His students could recite the definition of Brewster's angle word-perfect. He asked them to look at the bay outside the window through a piece of polarising film, turn it, and say what they saw. They could not connect the two. They knew the words and had never once looked at the thing. Next time you are on the sea wall with polarised sunglasses, look at the glare about 37° down, tilt your head slowly, and watch the sentence come true.

:::recall 9
Chapter 9's sound waves were pushes passed along the direction of travel. Could a sound wave in air be polarised the way light can?
???
No. Polarisation is the direction of a wave's *sideways* shaking, and sound in air has none: the air moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels, with no sideways motion to point one way or another. Light does shake sideways, which is why it can be polarised, and why a polarising filter can tell glare from the light of the water beneath it.
:::

:::careful
> "Polarised sunglasses are just dark glasses with a fancy name."

Dark glasses dim everything equally. Polarised glasses block one direction of shaking, so they dim glare off water far more than they dim the water itself, and turning them changes what you see. Test a pair on a phone or laptop screen before you trust them: the screen's light is polarised, so through genuinely polarised lenses the screen goes dark at some angle as you rotate them. Ordinary tinted lenses do nothing of the kind.
:::

:::check
Looking at the glare on a pond through polarised sunglasses, you turn your head sideways through 90°, and the glare gets much brighter. Why?
???
The glare is light reflected off the water, and it shakes mostly side to side, parallel to the surface. Upright, the glasses pass only up-and-down shaking, so they block most of the glare. Turned through 90°, they pass side-to-side shaking instead, which is exactly the glare's direction, so now it comes through.
:::

## 11.5 Light is a wave

:::scene London, 24 November 1803

Thomas Young let a narrow sunbeam into a dark room. Into the beam he held a thin card, about a thirtieth of an inch wide (under a millimetre), edge-on, so that the light passed on both sides of it. On the wall behind, inside the card's shadow, where it should have been darkest, he saw fine stripes: bright, dark, bright, dark. The light that had passed on the two sides of the card had spread into the shadow from both edges, met there, and *interfered*.

You know what that word means from Chapter 9. Drop two stones into still water and watch where the two sets of ripples cross. Where a crest from one meets a crest from the other, the water is lifted twice as high. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel, and the water is flat. The pattern of doubled and flattened places is interference, and nothing but a wave can do it. Two balls do not add up to nothing; two cricketers' throws do not cancel. Young's stripes were bright where light met light in step, crest with crest, and dark where it met out of step, crest with trough. Dark stripes made by *adding* light together are the surest sign of a wave there is.

:::fig c11_young() | Two sources of light in step (the two edges of Young's card, or the two slits of the later experiment). Where crests meet crests the light is bright; where a crest meets a trough it is dark. The stripes on the screen are interference, and only a wave can make them. Schematic: in a real experiment the stripes are about a millimetre apart.
:::live interference

Why had no one seen it before? Partly because the stripes are small. In the tidy version of Young's experiment that textbooks draw, light passes through two narrow slits half a millimetre apart, and on a screen a metre away the bright stripes come about a millimetre apart. You need a small, bright source, a dark room and patience, and Young had all three. And partly because Newton, who had taken white light apart so brilliantly, held that light was a stream of tiny particles, and decades after his death his authority still counted. Young's stripes said otherwise, and he was not thanked for it.

:::note Interference
What happens when two waves meet. In step, crest on crest, they add and the wave is bigger; out of step, crest on trough, they cancel. The pattern of bright and dark stripes it makes in light is the proof that light is a wave.
:::

:::five Interference
? What is it?
The combining of two or more overlapping waves, adding together to make a larger wave where they meet in step (crest on crest) and cancelling out where they meet out of step (crest on trough).
? Why is it called this?
Thomas Young introduced the word *interference* into optics in 1801, from the everyday sense of two things crossing and acting on each other, to name the way two overlapping light waves reinforce or cancel.
? What problem did it solve?
It settled the long argument over whether light has wave properties: streams of simple particles do not cancel each other to make darkness, whereas two overlapping beams of light produce alternating bright and dark stripes.
? Why does it work?
A wave's displacement can be positive (a crest) or negative (a trough); where two waves cross, the medium responds to the sum of both displacements at each instant.
? How does it help?
It explains the bright and dark fringes behind narrow slits, the swirling colours of soap bubbles and oil films on wet roads, the rainbow thrown by a CD's tracks, and the bronze-green sheen of a peacock's feather.
?? How do we know?
Hold a sub-millimetre card edge-on in a narrow sunbeam in a dark room, as Young did in 1803, or shine a laser through two narrow slits: the centre of the shadow is crossed by fine bright and dark stripes that vanish the moment you block either side.
?? What it isn't
Destructive interference does not destroy energy: the energy missing from the dark stripes is shifted into the bright stripes beside them, keeping the total energy unchanged.
?? Where does it stop working?
Two ordinary torches shining on a wall show no interference stripes because their waves shift out of step with each other billions of times a second, blurring the bright and dark fringes into smooth illumination before your eye can register them.
:::

The stripes also answer a question left over from Section 10.2. If light goes in straight lines, how did it get into the shadow at all? Every point on a wave's crest acts as a small new source of waves spreading in all directions (an idea from Christiaan Huygens, who did Rømer's arithmetic). Behind a wide opening the little waves add up to a wave going straight on; at an edge they spill round the corner. This spilling is ==diffraction==, and every wave does it. Sound bends round the corner of a building because its wavelength, tens of centimetres to a few metres, is about the size of a doorway. Light's wavelength is half a millionth of a metre, so a doorway a metre wide is two million wavelengths across and the spilling at its edges is far too small to notice. That is why rays work for mirrors and lenses, and why they fail at the edge of a card less than a millimetre wide.

:::note Diffraction
Waves spreading round an edge or through a small gap. The effect is large when the gap is about the size of the wavelength, and tiny when the gap is much bigger, which is why light usually seems to go straight.
:::

:::scene Paris, 1818

The decisive scene came fifteen years later. The French Academy offered a prize for the best account of diffraction, and a young engineer, Augustin Fresnel, submitted a complete wave theory. On the jury sat Siméon Poisson, a formidable mathematician and a particle man. Poisson worked through Fresnel's mathematics looking for a flaw and found what he took to be a fatal one. Fresnel's waves predicted that behind a small round disc, lit by a tiny bright source, the very centre of the shadow should hold a bright spot of light. Absurd, said Poisson. Before you read what happened, decide.

:::predict
A small round disc is lit by a tiny, bright source of light. At the exact centre of its shadow, the light is
- as dark as the shadow gets, since the disc is directly in the way.
- about the same as the rest of the shadow.
- a bright spot.
???
A bright spot. François Arago set up the experiment and looked, and there it was: a point of light at the centre of the shadow, exactly where the wave theory said the light spilling round the whole edge of the disc arrives in step. Fresnel won the prize in 1819. Poisson was never convinced, and the spot now carries his name as well as Arago's, which is a fair reward for an honest wrong prediction. Others had seen the spot long before, without understanding what they were looking at.
:::

Once you know light is a wave, colours appear in places that have none of their own. A soap bubble is a film of water so thin that light reflecting off its outer surface and light reflecting off its inner surface interfere. For a film 100 nm thick, yellow-orange light at 600 nm comes back in step and bright, and other colours do not; where the film is thicker or thinner, other colours win, and the colours swirl because the film is draining. Just before the bubble pops a patch goes black: there the film is so thin that the two reflections cancel exactly. The same thing colours oil on a wet Mumbai road after rain. It also explains the CD. Its surface carries thousands of fine, evenly spaced tracks, and light bouncing off neighbouring tracks interferes: for each colour there is a direction in which the reflections arrive in step, that direction depends on wavelength, and so the colours spread out, with red thrown farthest, the opposite order to a prism's. A peacock's feather works the same way: it has no green pigment, and its green and bronze come from fine structure sorting light by interference.

:::try Investigation 11.5
Set the two sources close together and note how far apart the stripes are. Move the sources apart and watch the stripes. Then shorten the wavelength. Write one rule for what makes the stripes wider.
:::

:::school
Interference and diffraction appear in Cambridge Lower Secondary Stage 9 and again in IB, and IGCSE light stays mostly with rays. Keep both pictures and know which to use: rays for mirrors and lenses, waves for stripes and for the colours of films and bubbles.
:::

:::careful
> "Light always travels in straight lines, so it can't bend round anything."

It bends round every edge it meets, by an amount set by its wavelength, which is so small that for anything you can hold the bend is invisible. Hold a card less than a millimetre wide in a sunbeam, as Young did, and the bending shows up as stripes in the shadow. Rays are a drawing that works when the gaps are huge compared with the wavelength. They are not a law.
:::

:::story light-wave

:::check
Your shadow in sunlight has fuzzy edges. Is that light diffracting round you?
???
Mostly not. The Sun is a disc, not a point, so each point on it casts a slightly shifted shadow of you, and the fuzzy band at the edge is where some of the Sun is hidden and some is not (Section 10.2). Diffraction round something as big as a person does happen, but the spreading is a tiny fraction of a millimetre, far too small to see at that scale. Diffraction shows itself at edges less than a millimetre across.
:::

## 11.6 The colour that changed

:::scene Calcutta, February 1928

Everything scattered so far came back the colour it went in. The blue of the sky is sunlight's own blue, re-sent; the blue of the sea is the same. In the seven years since the *Narkunda*, Raman and his students at the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science in Calcutta had gone on studying how liquids scatter light, and he now had a young colleague, K. S. Krishnan. In February 1928 they shone light of a single colour into clear liquids and looked carefully at what came out sideways.

:::predict
Light of one pure colour is shone into a clear liquid, and you look at the light scattered out sideways. Its colour is
- all exactly the colour that went in.
- all a different colour.
- almost all the colour that went in, with a tiny fraction of a different colour.
???
Almost all the same, with a tiny fraction shifted to a different colour. Krishnan's diary records the work day by day from 5 February. On 9 February Raman said it should be called the "Raman–Krishnan effect". On 28 February 1928 they announced it: when light of one colour enters a liquid, a small fraction of the scattered light comes out a different colour, and the amount of the change depends on the liquid. India now keeps 28 February as National Science Day.
:::

The shifted light is faint, a tiny fraction of what is scattered, which is why it needed a careful experiment and why nobody can see it at home. But it carries a message. The size of the shift depends on the material, so the shifted colours are a fingerprint: shine light of one colour into an unknown liquid, measure the colours that come back, and you can tell what it is made of. In the modern account the molecules take a small fixed bite of energy out of the light, or occasionally add one, and Chapter 15 shows why a light wave's colour and its energy go together. In 1930 Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics, "for his work on the scattering of light and for the discovery of the effect named after him". The ==Raman effect== is used in laboratories around the world to identify substances by the colours they scatter, and Krishnan, who kept the diary, deserves to be named beside him.

:::note Raman effect
When light of one colour is scattered by a material, a small fraction of it comes out as light of a different colour, and the shift depends on the material. Discovered by C. V. Raman and K. S. Krishnan in Calcutta in February 1928.
:::

:::fig c11_raman() | The colour that changed, drawn as a spectrum. Light of one colour enters a liquid (left). Of the light scattered sideways, almost all comes out the same colour (the tall line); a small fraction comes out shifted (the small lines beside it), by an amount that depends on the liquid. Schematic: the shifted lines are drawn far taller than they are.

Raman's effect belongs to a much bigger picture, and this is the place to open it. Light is a wave, and in 1865 James Clerk Maxwell worked out what kind: a travelling shake of electric and magnetic influence, the fields you will meet in Chapter 13. He calculated how fast such a shake should travel, and the answer was the speed of light that Fizeau had measured. Light is an ==electromagnetic wave==, and once you know that, you know it has relatives. Any wavelength is possible, and the visible colours, from about 400 to 700 nm, are one narrow band. Longer than red lies the infrared your TV remote sends to the television (a phone camera can see it; Chapter 15 shows you how), then the millimetre waves J. C. Bose made in Calcutta in the 1890s, then the microwaves and the metre-long radio waves that the giant dishes of the GMRT near Pune collect from the sky. Shorter than violet lie ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All of them travel at exactly the same speed in empty space, 299,792,458 m/s, and for all of them the speed is the wavelength times the number of waves per second. A wave of yellow light, 600 nm long, passes a point about five hundred trillion times a second.

:::note Electromagnetic wave
A travelling wave of electric and magnetic fields. Light is one; so are radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All travel at the speed of light in empty space, and differ only in wavelength.
:::

The whole family is laid out in [The Ladder of Light](#explore-spectrum), at the front of this book, as a ladder of wavelengths with a real source on every rung and the stories that belong to each. Open it now and find the visible band. It is a very small part of the ladder, and everything in these two chapters happened on it.

:::school
Your syllabus topic is "Electromagnetic spectrum". You need the order of the family by wavelength (radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma), and the fact that all of them travel at the same speed in a vacuum. The Ladder of Light is that order, with real sources on it.
:::

:::careful
> "Raman discovered why the sky is blue."

That was ordinary scattering, which keeps the colour: the blue in the sky was already in the sunlight. Raman and Krishnan found the rare scattering that *changes* colour. And "the light scattered by a liquid changes colour" is wrong too: almost all of it keeps its colour, and a tiny fraction shifts. The smallness of the fraction is why it took a careful experiment, and why the discovery was worth a Nobel Prize.
:::

:::check
Put these in order of wavelength, longest first: X-rays, radio waves, visible light, infrared.
???
Radio waves (metres and more), infrared (longer than red light), visible light (about 400 to 700 nm), X-rays (far shorter than violet). All four travel at the same speed in empty space; only the wavelength, and with it the number of waves per second, is different.
:::

## The sunglasses test

:::case The sunglasses test
Take a pair of polarised sunglasses to Marine Drive an hour before sunset, with an adult, and first check the glasses on a phone screen: through genuinely polarised lenses the screen goes dark at some angle as you turn them. Then, from the sea wall, do three things and write down what you see.

1. Look at the glare on the water about 37° below the horizon, roughly a third of the way from straight ahead to straight down, and slowly tilt your head towards one shoulder. The glare brightens as you tilt, and fades again as you straighten up.
2. Look at the sea's own colour, the blue in the water between the patches of glare, while tilting. It stays blue.
3. Look at the blue sky about a quarter of the way round from the Sun and tilt again. It darkens and brightens a little as you turn. Never look at the Sun itself, with or without sunglasses.

Three patches of light. One is strongly polarised, one is partly polarised, and one hardly at all. Which is which, and what does the one that stays blue tell you about where the sea's colour comes from?
???
The glare off the water (1) is strongly polarised: it is sunlight reflected off the surface, shaking parallel to the surface, and at Brewster's angle, 53° from the normal and so about 37° below the horizon, it is completely polarised. Upright lenses, which pass only up-and-down shaking, block it; tilt your head and the lenses start to pass side-to-side shaking, and the glare returns.

The sky (3) is partly polarised, because it is sunlight scattered sideways by the air, and scattering sorts the shaking only partly, so the sky dims and brightens without going black.

The sea's own blue (2) is hardly polarised at all, so tilting changes nothing. That is Raman's result from the rail of the *Narkunda*: with the polarised reflected sky removed, the blue is still there, so it is coming from inside the water, where sunlight is scattered, blue most of all, and sent back up. The sky is blue because the air scatters sunlight, the sunset is orange for the same reason seen along a longer path, and the sea is blue partly because it reflects that sky and mostly because the water scatters light itself. The same rule, three times, and a different share of it in each.
:::
