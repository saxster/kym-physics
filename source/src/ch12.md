Volume III was about things that travel without any stuff travelling. A wave crossed the Arabian Sea and the water stayed where it was. Sound turned out to be a push passed from particle to particle. Light turned out to behave as a wave too, one that crosses the emptiness between the Sun and your face with nothing you could point to doing the waving. That last fact was left hanging, because to pick it up you need two new characters: electric charge, and the magnet. This volume is theirs. By its end you will know what light is made of, and the answer begins in the least likely place: a plastic comb and a torch battery.

Here is the puzzle to carry with you. Open a torch. Inside are a battery, a bulb or LED, a switch, and two paths of metal, one from each end of the battery. Ask most people what the battery does and they say it sends electricity along the wire to the bulb, and the bulb uses it up to make light. That is a tidy story, and if it were the whole story one wire would be enough: battery, wire, bulb, done. Yet every circuit anyone has ever built needs a path back to the battery. Take the return path away and the bulb goes dark at once. Something is wrong with the tidy story, and finding out what is the work of this chapter.

## 12.1 Rubbing moves charge

:::scene Mumbai, a dry evening in winter

Comb your hair quickly with a plastic comb, then turn on a tap until a thin, smooth stream falls into the basin. Bring the comb close to the stream without touching it. The water leans towards the comb. Move the comb away and the stream straightens. Rub a balloon on your hair and it does the same, and it will also lift scraps of paper off a table and cling to the wall for a minute. You met a version of this in *The Invisible World*, where a rubbed balloon sorted pepper from salt. Now it is time to ask what the rubbing did.

Say your first thought out loud. Most people's is: *the rubbing made electricity*. That was also the best idea anyone had for a long time, and it has one thing right. Rubbing is involved. But watch what two strips of sticky tape do, because they show that whatever rubbing does, it does to *two* things at once.

:::predict
Stick two strips of tape, each about four centimetres long, side by side on a table, then peel both off quickly and hold them dangling near each other. They swing *apart*. Now stick one strip on top of another, press them together, peel the pair off the table, and pull them apart. Hold the two strips near each other. What happens?
- They swing apart, as before.
- They swing towards each other.
- Nothing: pulling them apart cancels the effect.
???
They swing towards each other. The side-by-side strips were treated the same way, so they ended up alike, and alike pushes apart. The stacked pair was pulled off each other, and whichever strip took something from the other, the two ended up *opposite*, and opposite pulls together. Rubbing does not make one kind of stuff. It leaves two things different from each other.
:::

Here is what is really going on, and you already know the parts from chemistry. Every atom holds protons, which carry positive charge, and electrons, which carry an equal negative charge. An ordinary object has exactly as many of one as the other, so the two kinds cancel and the object is neutral. When two different materials are pressed together, the one that holds its electrons more tightly takes a few from the other. Rubbing presses the surfaces into closer contact and so moves more electrons across. That is all rubbing does. The comb ends up with a few extra electrons and is negative. Your hair, which lost exactly those electrons, is left with a few protons unmatched and is positive. In a solid, only the electrons move; the protons stay locked in their atoms. And nothing was made. Count every charge in the comb and the hair before and after, and the totals are the same. Electrons moved from one place to another, and that is the whole event.

:::fig c12_rubbing() | Before and after rubbing, with the charges counted. Before, the comb and the hair each hold equal numbers of positive and negative charges, so each is neutral. After, three electrons have crossed to the comb. The comb is negative by three and the hair positive by three, and the total is exactly what it was. The numbers are tiny for the drawing's sake; the real count is enormous.

This property, the one that makes the comb and the hair pull on each other, is ==electric charge==. It comes in those two kinds, called positive and negative by a convention that goes back to Benjamin Franklin, and the rule you have just watched is the whole of its behaviour at this level: like charges repel, unlike charges attract. The unit of charge is the coulomb (C), and it is a big unit. One coulomb is the charge of about six billion billion electrons: 6.24 × 10¹⁸ of them. A rubbed comb carries a minute fraction of that.

:::note Electric charge
The property of matter that makes objects attract or repel each other electrically. It comes in two kinds, positive (protons) and negative (electrons). Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Measured in coulombs (C); one coulomb is about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons' worth.
:::

Why does the comb keep its charge while a metal spoon rubbed the same way shows nothing? In some materials, metals above all, a few electrons from each atom are free to wander through the whole piece; in others, such as plastic, rubber and dry hair, every electron stays with its atom. The first kind are ==conductors==, the second ==insulators==. Rub a spoon and the extra electrons run away through the metal and through your hand. Rub a comb and they stay where you put them.

:::note Conductor and insulator
A conductor (a metal, salty water, you) lets charge move through it easily. An insulator (plastic, rubber, glass, dry air) does not. There is no perfect example of either.
:::

That leaves the water. The stream was never charged, so why did it lean? Because the comb's negative charge pushes on the charges *inside* the water: it shoves the water's electrons slightly away and pulls its positive charges slightly towards itself. The nearer charges are the opposite kind, and nearer wins, so the net effect is a pull. This slight separation of charge inside a neutral thing is called polarisation, and it is why a charged balloon lifts paper, dust and hair that have no charge of their own. It also explains why the trick fades in July. Everyone who has tried it knows that static tricks work badly in Mumbai's monsoon. The books this chapter is built on say only this much about why: in damp air the extra charge leaks away faster, so a rubbed comb loses its charge before you have reached the tap. What damp air does to carry charge off, they do not say, and this book will not pretend to.

:::try Investigation 12.1
Do the tape experiment above on a dry evening, then do it again on a humid one, and time how long a strip keeps swinging away from its twin. Then try the comb on the tap. Write down what you observe before you write down why. The "why" column is allowed to say "don't know yet".
:::

:::recall 3
The comb pulls the stream of water sideways. In Chapter 3 every push had a partner. Does the water pull on the comb?
???
Yes, with exactly the same size of force, the other way. You do not notice it because you are holding the comb, and the pull is tiny next to the grip of your fingers. Hang the comb from a thread instead and bring something charged near it, and you will see it swing. Attraction is never one-sided.
:::

:::careful
> "Rubbing makes electricity in the comb."

Rubbing makes nothing. It moves electrons from one material to the other, and it leaves exactly as much positive charge behind as negative charge it carried off. The comb and your hair together have the same total charge they had before, which is none. "Static electricity" is also not a special kind of electricity. It is the same charge you will meet flowing round a circuit in the next section, except that here it is sitting still.
:::

:::story static

:::check
A rubbed balloon ends up with a charge of −3 units, where one unit is a very large number of electrons. It was rubbed on a woollen scarf. What charge does the scarf have, and how do you know without measuring it?
???
+3 units. Both started neutral, and electrons only moved between the two of them. Every electron the balloon gained is one the scarf lost, so the scarf is short of exactly three units of negative charge, which leaves it positive by three. The total of the pair is still zero.
:::

## 12.2 A loop, not a pipe

:::scene Mumbai, your table

For the rest of this chapter you need a small kit from an electronics shop: a 9 V battery (the rectangular kind with two snap terminals), a resistor marked 330 Ω, two red LEDs, one white LED, and four leads with crocodile clips on their ends. Everything in this chapter runs on that 9 V battery, which is safe to handle. Nothing here goes anywhere near a wall socket, and Section 12.6 explains why.

Here is the first experiment, and you must predict before you do it. Clip one lead to the battery's positive terminal and the other end of the lead to one leg of a red LED. That is a wire from the battery to the "bulb", which is exactly what the tidy story from the start of the chapter says you need.

:::predict
One lead joins the battery's + terminal to one leg of the LED. Nothing else is connected. What does the LED do?
- It lights.
- It glows faintly, since only half the circuit is there.
- Nothing at all.
???
Nothing at all, however long you wait. Researchers who asked students to draw a working circuit in 1980 found many drew exactly this: one wire, battery to bulb. It is the most natural picture there is, and it never works. Now add the resistor and a second lead so that the path runs battery + → resistor → LED → battery −. The LED lights the instant the last clip touches. If it stays dark, turn the LED round: it lets current through one way only.
:::

:::fig c12_loop() | Left: one wire from the battery to the LED. Nothing happens, because charge has nowhere to go. Right: a complete loop, battery to resistor to LED and back. The arrows mark the current, which is the same size at every point on the loop, including inside the battery. The arrowheads show the conventional direction, from + round to −.

A working circuit is a closed path, a loop with no gaps, and the battery is part of the loop, not the start of it. What flows round that loop is charge, and the rate at which it flows is the ==electric current==: the amount of charge passing a point each second, measured in amperes (A). One ampere is one coulomb per second. Your LED carries about 20 mA, two hundredths of a coulomb each second. A lightning stroke, for comparison, can move 10²⁰ electrons in two thousandths of a second, which works out at 8,000 A.

:::note Electric current
The rate at which charge flows past a point: coulombs per second, measured in amperes (A). 1 A = 1 C/s. Current is the same all the way round a simple loop.
:::

Why must the loop be closed? Because the battery does not hold a tank of charge to pour out. It holds chemicals. Its chemistry does one job: it moves charge from one of its terminals to the other, inside the battery, so that the two terminals are left different, one with a surplus and one with a shortage. Join them by a path and charge flows round from surplus to shortage, and the chemistry keeps replacing what leaves, pushing more charge across inside. Break the path anywhere and the chemistry stops too: a lead-acid cell's reaction, one textbook says plainly, "will not take place unless there is a complete circuit". The battery is a pump in a loop, and a pump with its pipe cut off has nothing to pump.

Try a kitchen model. Pack a drinking straw full of dried chana, so the peas touch end to end. Push one more pea in at one end, and a pea pops out at the far end at the same instant. You did not have to wait for *your* pea to travel the length of the straw: the straw was already full, and a push at one end is a push at the other. A copper wire is like that straw. It is already full of free electrons, all the way round the loop, before you connect anything. The battery does not fill it; the battery pushes on electrons that are already there. Section 12.3 shows how slowly the individual electrons actually move, and how quickly the push arrives.

The straw picture, like all pictures, stops somewhere, and it is worth saying where. Peas push each other by bumping. Electrons push each other by electric repulsion, without touching. And the wire is solid metal, not a hollow tube: cut it and nothing spills out, because the electrons belong to the metal and stay in it.

:::home Build the loop
Lay out the kit. Clip a lead from the battery's + terminal to one end of the 330 Ω resistor. Clip a second lead from the other end of the resistor to the longer leg of a red LED, and a third from the LED's other leg back to the battery's − terminal. It lights. Unclip any one clip and it goes dark; clip it back and it lights at once, from whichever point you broke the loop. Now take the resistor out and reconnect the loop with it: same brightness, same instant switch-on. Swap the two battery leads over so the current runs the other way: dark. An LED is a one-way device, and that is a clue for the chapter's case. Two rules, always: keep the resistor in the loop (Section 12.4 explains what it is for), and never join the battery's two terminals to each other with a lead and nothing else. That makes a path with almost no resistance, and the lead and the battery heat up fast.
:::

Before electrons were discovered, people had to pick a direction for the current arrows, and they chose "out of + and round to −". Electrons, it later turned out, flow the other way, from − to +. Both arrows are still used, and both are correct, because an arrow on a diagram is a convention, not a claim about which particles move. Circuit diagrams, your school book and this book all draw conventional current, from + to −.

:::school
Your Stage 7 book may describe electricity as a flow of electrons, and that is fine. In IGCSE you write *current*, which is charge flowing per second, and you will be asked for both arrows: conventional current from positive to negative, and the flow of free electrons from negative to positive. They were decided at different times, and both are right.
:::

:::careful
> "Electricity flows through the wire like water through a hollow pipe, and the battery fills it up."

The wire is solid metal and it is already full of movable electrons before the battery is connected. Nothing pours in. The battery supplies a push, and the push sets electrons moving all round the loop at once, like peas in a full straw. Cut a wire and nothing leaks out, and a battery is never "full of electricity": it is full of chemicals that can move charge round a loop that someone else has to close.
:::

:::check
Two coulombs of charge pass a point in a wire every ten seconds. What is the current there, and what is the current at a point on the other side of the same loop?
???
0.2 A at the first point: two coulombs divided by ten seconds. And 0.2 A at the other point too. Round a single loop, whatever passes one point must pass every other, or charge would be piling up somewhere, and piled-up charge pushes back until the flow evens out.
:::

## 12.3 What gets used up?

:::scene Mumbai, the first of the month

The electricity bill arrives on the phone. "Units consumed: 312." Somebody paid for something, and the natural thought is that the something came down the wire and the flat used it up. The torch tells the same story: the bulb glows, the battery dies, so the bulb must be eating what the battery sends. The sentence "the current gets used up on the way round" feels so obviously true that one group of physics teachers wrote, after years of asking, that "it is clear to most pupils that something must get used up". Something does. The question is what, and the two red LEDs settle it.

:::predict
Put both red LEDs in the loop, one after the other: battery + → resistor → first LED → second LED → battery −. The current leaves the battery, passes the first LED and then the second. Which LED is brighter?
- The first, because it gets the current fresh from the battery.
- The second, because the first has slowed the current down and the second has a clearer run.
- They are equally bright.
???
Equally bright. Now swap the two LEDs over and look again. Nothing changes. If the first LED were using up some of the current, the second would always be the dimmer, whichever LED you put there. It never is.
:::

:::try Investigation 12.3
Build the two-LED loop above and do the swap. Then leave it running for a minute and touch the resistor lightly. It is warm. Note where the warmth is: not in the leads, not in the battery, but in the resistor, and a little in the LEDs. Something is being delivered there. Section 12.3 names it.
:::

The current is the same at every point of a single loop, inside the battery included. There is a reason it must be. Suppose more charge arrived at the first LED each second than left it. Charge would then pile up at the LED, and piled-up charge pushes back on the charge behind it, slowing the arrivals until arrivals and departures match. That evening-out takes a tiny fraction of a second, and after it the flow is the same everywhere, like a bicycle chain, where every link moves at the same speed because the chain is one loop. Current is not consumed. Count the charge leaving the battery's + terminal in a minute and the charge arriving at its − terminal in that minute, and the two are equal.

So what *is* delivered at the LED, and what did you pay for on the bill? Energy. The battery's chemistry does work on the charge it pushes round the loop, and the charge carries that energy to wherever the loop makes it hard to get through. At the LED some of the energy leaves as light and some as heat; at the resistor, all of it leaves as heat, which is the warmth under your finger. The charge itself comes back to the battery for more. The unit "consumed" on your bill is a kilowatt-hour of energy, and Chapter 7 followed where energy goes: it is never destroyed, only delivered and spread out as light, motion and warmth through the flat. The charge in the wires is not consumed at all.

:::recall 7
The bill says "units consumed". In Chapter 7 energy was never used up. Reconcile the two.
???
A "unit" on the bill is a kilowatt-hour of energy, and what you buy is the *delivery* of that energy into the flat, where it ends up as light, motion, sound and, finally, warmth spread into the air. None of it is destroyed; it is spread out and is no longer useful. The charge that carried it is not consumed either. It stays in the wires, going back and forth fifty times a second.
:::

Here is a model that keeps all of this straight; it is ours, and you can build it with friends. Stand in a circle, a rope loop running loosely through everyone's hands. One person is the battery: she pulls the rope round, hand over hand. The rope is the charge, and it moves at the same speed through every pair of hands. Now one person grips a little: he is the LED. His hands warm up, because the rope rubs through them. Notice three things. The rope is not used up; every bit of it comes back round to the battery. The gripper's hands warm the moment the rope starts moving, wherever in the circle he stands. And the battery feels the grip at once, through the rope, and has to pull harder to keep it moving. That last point is the energy: the puller does the work, and the gripper's hands are where it is delivered.

:::fig c12_potential_hill() | The loop unrolled, with the push drawn as height. The battery lifts the charge by 9 volts. Along the leads the height barely changes. The resistor is a long slope down of 7 volts and the red LED a steeper drop of 2 volts, which brings the charge back to where it started. Below, the current at every point is drawn with the same arrow, because it is the same everywhere. Heights are to scale; the drops always add up to the rise.

The height in that figure has a name. The push a battery gives, and the push that is "spent" across each component, is measured in volts, and a volt is a joule per coulomb. Your 9 V battery gives each coulomb of charge 9 joules of energy to deliver round the loop. A red LED takes about 2 of those joules from each coulomb; the resistor takes the other 7. This energy-per-coulomb is ==voltage==, and Section 12.4 takes it further. For now it is enough to see that voltage is the quantity that *does* get divided up round a loop, while current does not. Power is energy delivered per second, and it is voltage times current. With 21 mA flowing, the battery delivers 9 V × 0.021 A, about 0.19 W: roughly 0.04 W as light and warmth at the LED and 0.15 W as warmth in the resistor.

:::note Voltage
The energy transferred by each coulomb of charge as it passes between two points, in joules per coulomb, which are volts (V). A 9 V battery gives each coulomb 9 J. Across a component, the voltage is the energy each coulomb delivers there. Also called potential difference.
:::

One more thing the rope gets right. When the battery starts pulling, the whole rope starts moving at once; no piece of rope had to travel from her hands to the gripper's first. Electrons are like that, only more so. In a copper lead half a millimetre thick carrying your 21 mA, the electrons creep along at about eight millionths of a metre per second. At that pace an electron takes about 34 hours to drift round a one-metre loop. Yet the LED lights the instant the last clip touches, because the wire was already full and the push travels along it at about 10⁸ m/s, a million million times faster than the drift. Every electron in the loop starts creeping almost at the same moment, and 1.3 × 10¹⁷ of them pass any point each second. (Each one, by the way, is also jiggling about at random very fast; the creep is the slow average of all that jiggling.)

:::careful
> "The first LED uses up some of the current, so the second gets less."

The current is the same at every point round the loop. What each LED takes is a share of the *energy* each coulomb carries, which shows up as a share of the battery's volts: 2 V across each red LED out of the 9. Swap the LEDs and nothing changes, because neither is first in any way that matters. Current goes round; energy is delivered.
:::

:::check
In the loop with the resistor and one red LED, where is the current largest: as it leaves the battery's + terminal, as it passes through the LED, or as it returns to the − terminal?
???
Nowhere. It is the same at all three points. If it were larger leaving than returning, charge would be accumulating somewhere in the loop every second, and that pile would push back until the flow evened out. What differs between the three places is the energy each coulomb still carries: most as it leaves, less after the resistor, least after the LED.
:::

## 12.4 Push and resistance

:::scene Mumbai, a taxi on a cold morning

Watch a car start on a cold morning with its headlights on. As the driver turns the key the lights dim for a second, then recover once the engine catches. A torch with tired cells does something similar: the light is yellow and dull, and fresh cells make it white again, though both sets of cells say 1.5 V on the side. There is a wrong idea hiding in both scenes, and it is a popular one: *a battery always gives out the same current*. If that were so, the headlights would not care about the starter motor and old cells would be as bright as new ones. Here is the test, with your kit.

:::predict
You have two red LEDs in the loop with the resistor and both are lit. You add a third red LED, in line with the others. What happens?
- All three stay as bright as before: the battery gives the same current, so each LED gets it.
- All three are dimmer.
- The first two stay bright and the third is dim, because it is last in line.
???
All three are dimmer, and equally so, because the same (smaller) current runs through each. The battery did not keep the current fixed. It kept something else fixed, roughly: its *push*. With more in the loop to push through, less gets through.
:::

:::work
The battery's push is 9 V. Each red LED needs about 2 V of it, and whatever is left pushes current through the 330 Ω resistor.
One LED: 9 − 2 = 7 V left, 7 ÷ 330 ≈ 0.021 A, about 21 mA.
Two LEDs: 9 − 4 = 5 V left, 5 ÷ 330 ≈ 15 mA.
Three LEDs: 9 − 6 = 3 V left, about 9 mA.
Four LEDs: 1 V left, about 3 mA, a dim glow.
Five LEDs would need 10 V before anything flowed. They stay dark.
:::

So a battery sets a push, and the current depends on what you connect. Every part of a loop opposes the flow of charge to some degree, and the measure of how much is its ==resistance==. Georg Ohm found, by careful measurement, that for a metal wire at a steady temperature the current is proportional to the voltage across it: double the push and you double the flow. The ratio of the two is the resistance, R = V/I, measured in ohms (Ω). One ohm lets one ampere through for each volt. A car headlight running on 12 V and carrying 2.5 A has a resistance of 12 ÷ 2.5 = 4.8 Ω. A long thin wire has more resistance than a short thick one, in proportion to its length and in inverse proportion to its cross-section, which is why the thick cables to a geyser are thick. This rule, V = IR, is Ohm's law. It is a measured fact about many materials at ordinary temperatures, not a law of nature, and some things disobey it.

:::note Resistance
How strongly a component opposes current: R = V/I, in ohms (Ω). A component of 1 Ω passes 1 A for each volt across it. For metals at a steady temperature R is roughly constant (Ohm's law). For an LED it is not.
:::

The LED is one of the disobedient ones. It lets almost nothing through until the voltage across it reaches about 2 V (red) or about 3 V (white and blue), and then the current rises so steeply that the LED, left to itself, will take far more than the 30 mA its datasheet allows and destroy itself. That is what the resistor is for. The LED cannot limit its own current, so the 330 Ω does it: it takes whatever voltage is left over and lets through only 21 mA. A red LED and a white one need different voltages because they are made of different materials, and in Chapter 15 that difference turns out to be the colour of the light.

:::fig c12_iv_graph() | Current against voltage for the 330 Ω resistor and for a red LED. The resistor's line is straight: double the volts, double the milliamps, and 9 V gives 27 mA. The LED passes almost nothing until about 2 V and then climbs steeply. Its curve is drawn schematically from a datasheet's single point (about 2.0 V at 20 mA); the dashed line is the 30 mA the LED survives. Without a resistor, 9 V would drive the LED far off the top of the graph.

Now the headlights. A battery's push is not quite fixed either. Inside every real battery is some resistance of its own, called its internal resistance, in line with the push. When only a small current flows, almost all the push reaches the terminals. When a large current flows, as it does while a starter motor turns the engine, a good part of the push is used inside the battery, and the voltage at the terminals sags. One textbook's example: a 12 V car battery with 0.1 Ω inside gives 11.9 V to a 10 Ω load but only 10.0 V to a 0.5 Ω one, and a worn battery whose internal resistance has risen to 0.5 Ω gives just 6.0 V to that heavy load. Headlights on 6 V are dim. As a cell ages its internal resistance climbs, which is why old cells read 1.5 V on a meter with nothing connected and yet make a dull torch: the push is there, but it cannot deliver. A battery tester, the textbook adds, really tests the internal resistance.

:::school
Where this book says "the battery's push", IGCSE says the battery's *e.m.f.*, and where it says the volts across a component, IGCSE says the *p.d.*, the potential difference. Both are in volts. "E.m.f." once stood for electromotive force, but it is not a force at all; it is energy per coulomb, and the name is a historical leftover.
:::

:::careful
> "A battery always pushes out the same current."

A battery sets a push, roughly, and the current depends on everything in the loop. Add resistance and the current falls; add LEDs and each gets less; connect almost nothing and the current is almost nothing. Even the push is not perfectly fixed: a heavy current, or an old cell's internal resistance, makes it sag.
:::

:::check
A friend's torch is dim with old cells. A meter across each old cell, with the torch off, reads 1.5 V, the same as a new cell. "So the cells are fine," says the friend, "the bulb must be going." What is wrong with that reasoning?
???
A meter draws almost no current, so it reads the cell's push with nothing asked of it. The torch bulb draws a real current, and an old cell's internal resistance uses up a large share of the push inside the cell, leaving the bulb with less. Test the cells under load: swap in new ones, and if the torch is bright again the bulb was fine all along.
:::

## 12.5 Series and parallel

:::scene Mumbai, a flat at night

The kitchen tube light flickers and dies. The fan keeps turning, the fridge hums, and the hall light is still on. Nobody thinks that is remarkable, but it should be, after what you have just learned. If everything in the flat were on one loop, like your three LEDs, a dead light would be a gap in the loop and the whole flat would go dark. It does not, so the flat's wiring is arranged some other way. There are exactly two ways to put two things in a circuit, and your kit can do both. Predict first, with a prediction that MIT asks its first-year students.

:::predict
One red LED (with its own 330 Ω resistor) is lit on the 9 V battery. You add a second red LED, with its own 330 Ω resistor, so that each LED-and-resistor pair has one end on the battery's + terminal and the other on its − terminal: two separate branches side by side. Compared with before, the first LED is now:
- Dimmer, because the two LEDs share the battery.
- Just as bright.
- Brighter.
???
Just as bright, or so close you cannot tell. Each branch has the full 9 V across it and carries its own 21 mA regardless of the other, so the battery now supplies 42 mA and is doing twice the work. (A real battery sags a little at the higher current; a perfect one would not sag at all.) Unclip one LED and the other does not even flicker.
:::

Things connected one after another along a single path are in ==series==. The same current passes through each of them, because there is only one way round, and their resistances add up, because the charge has to get through all of them in turn. The push divides between them: the volts across each part add up to the battery's volts. Here is a textbook example with round numbers. Three resistors of 1.0, 6.0 and 13 Ω in series on 12 V make 20 Ω in all, so the current is 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 A, and the volts across the three are 0.6, 3.6 and 7.8, which add up to 12. Your three red LEDs were a series circuit, and so was the flat in the imaginary one-loop version, where the dead tube light would have broken the only path.

Things connected side by side, each with its two ends joined to the same two points, are in ==parallel==. Each branch has the full voltage across it, because its ends are the battery's ends. The currents in the branches add up to the current at the battery, because charge arriving at the fork has to go one way or the other and none is lost. And adding a branch *lowers* the total resistance, since there are now more paths through: three identical resistors in parallel let three times the current through, so together they behave as one third of one. Think of the ticket gates at Churchgate station. Open a second gate and twice as many people get through each minute, at the same pace each.

:::fig c12_series_parallel() | Two red LEDs on one 9 V battery, two ways. In series (left) the current is the same, about 15 mA, at every point, and the two LEDs share the push: 2 V each, with 5 V left for the resistor. In parallel (right) each branch has the full 9 V across it and carries its own 21 mA, and the battery supplies both, 42 mA. Unclip the top LED in each circuit and the drawing tells you what the other does.

Now your flat. Every light, fan and socket in a home is wired in parallel across the supply, so each gets the full 230 V and each works on its own. "Generally, loads are connected in parallel across the power supply," as the MIT course notes put it, and that is why the dead tube light took only its own branch out of action. It is also why switching on the geyser does not dim the hall light noticeably, and why plugging in one more thing draws one more current from the supply rather than sharing out a fixed one.

:::home Two LEDs, two ways
Build the series circuit: + → resistor → LED → LED → −. Both glow at the same, slightly dull level. Unclip one LED: the other goes dark, because the only path is broken. Now build the parallel circuit, which needs the second resistor from the shop or a second 330 Ω: two branches, each resistor-then-LED, both starting at + and ending at −. Both LEDs glow at full brightness. Unclip one: the other carries on exactly as it was. Last, in the series circuit, swap the order of an LED and the resistor. Nothing changes, because order along a single path makes no difference to a current that is the same everywhere.
:::

Now the brightness puzzle that MIT's question finishes with. Put two identical bulbs in series on a battery instead of one, and the current halves, because the resistance has doubled. Each bulb then gets a quarter of the power it had alone, since power goes as the current squared, so each is much dimmer. Put the same two bulbs in parallel and each is as bright as one alone, but the battery is supplying twice the current and will run down in half the time. The energy has to come from somewhere, and it comes from the battery's chemistry.

:::careful
> "Bulbs in parallel share the battery, so each one is dimmer."

In parallel each branch has the whole voltage across it and draws its own current, so each bulb is as bright as it would be alone. What is shared is the battery's *effort*: it supplies the sum of the branch currents and runs down faster. The sharing-out of brightness happens in series, where the push is divided between the bulbs.
:::

:::check
Three identical resistors, each of resistance R, are connected in parallel. Without any arithmetic, what is their combined resistance? And how would you connect the three to get the largest possible total?
???
R/3. Each branch passes the same current it would alone, so three branches pass three times the current for the same push, which is what a resistor of one third the value would do. For the largest total, connect them in series: 3R, since the charge has to pass through all three in turn.
:::

## 12.6 Safe at home

:::scene Mumbai, the cupboard by the front door

Ask an adult to open the cover of the flat's distribution board with you. Inside is a row of switches, each one guarding a few circuits of the flat, and usually one larger switch with a small test button. Look and do not touch. Everything in this section is to be understood, not tried. The battery on your table pushes with 9 V. The sockets in the wall push with about 230 V, swinging back and forth 50 times a second, and that is a different world.

Electricity has two ways to hurt. The first is heat. Remember the one rule of your kit: never join the battery's terminals with a bare lead. A lead has almost no resistance, so a huge current flows and the lead and the battery get hot. Such an accidental low-resistance path across a supply is called a ==short circuit==, and at 230 V the current in one can set a cable on fire. The guard against it is simple and old: put something in series with each circuit that opens if the current grows too large. A fuse is a strip of metal with a low melting point that melts and breaks the path. A circuit breaker is a switch that trips itself and can be reset, which is what each small switch on the board is. In Indian homes these are usually called MCBs, miniature circuit breakers. They protect the wiring in the walls from overheating.

:::note Short circuit
An unintended path of very low resistance across a supply. It carries a very large current and heats up dangerously. A fuse or circuit breaker in series opens the circuit when the current is too high.
:::

The second way is shock, which is current through a person. Current, not voltage, is what does the harm: about 1 mA is just felt, around 5 mA is the most that is considered harmless, 10 to 20 mA makes the muscles clamp so that you cannot let go, and 100 to 300 mA can stop the heart's rhythm. But voltage is what *drives* current through you, by Ohm's law, and your resistance decides how much. Dry skin has a resistance of roughly 200,000 Ω; skin that is soaking wet, about 10,000 Ω. (Those figures come from an American textbook and the human body is the same everywhere, so this book applies them to 230 V.) Dry, 230 V would push about 1 mA through you, a tingle. Wet, about 23 mA, which is beyond the point where you can let go. That is the whole reason wet hands and switches never mix, and the reason bathrooms are wired with such care.

:::fig c12_earth_wire() | A metal-cased appliance, with its live wire frayed so that it touches the case. Left: the case is wired to earth. The fault current runs down the earth wire, the current in the live and neutral wires no longer match, and the residual current device in the board switches off within a fraction of a second, before anyone touches it. Right: no earth wire. The case sits live, waiting, and the first person to touch it becomes the path to the ground.

Two devices protect people rather than wiring, and both use ideas from this chapter. The first is earthing. The metal case of a fridge, a geyser or a mixer is connected by a third wire to the earth, which is such a large body that it can take or give charge without its own state changing noticeably. If a live wire inside frays and touches the case, current flows at once down the earth wire instead of waiting for a hand. The second device is the large switch on the board, a residual current device (RCD). It uses the fact you proved in Section 12.3: in a healthy circuit the current going out along the live wire equals the current coming back along the neutral, exactly. The RCD compares the two. If they differ by 30 mA or more, charge is leaking out of the loop somewhere, perhaps through a person, and it cuts the supply. India's safety regulations require every home's RCD to trip at a leak of 30 mA or less. The current that cannot be used up turns out to be the current that keeps you safe.

:::school
IGCSE has a topic called *Electrical safety* (4.4). The hazards and devices in it are the ones in this section: damaged insulation and overheating, fuses and circuit breakers in series, and earthing. Where IGCSE says "circuit breaker", the electrician who comes to the flat says "MCB". Same thing.
:::

Three facts about the supply itself. Its voltage in Maharashtra is declared as 230 volts, with 240 written beside it in some documents, and the rules allow it to wander between about 195 and 253. It is alternating: the push reverses direction a hundred times a second, fifty full swings, with the voltage reaching a peak of about 325 V on each swing; "230" is an average that gives the same heating as a steady 230 V would. And it is never, under any circumstances, for experiments. No wire goes near a socket, no probe, no finger, no coin. The whole of this chapter can be done with a 9 V battery, and that is where it stays.

:::careful
> "It's the current that kills, not the voltage, so voltage doesn't matter."

Current does the damage, but voltage is what pushes the current through you, and your body's resistance is the only thing in the way. The same 230 V that tingles through dry skin drives twenty times the current through wet skin. Both numbers matter, together, by Ohm's law.
:::

:::careful
> "The MCB protects me from shocks."

A circuit breaker or fuse opens when the current is far too large, which protects the wiring from overheating. A current of 30 mA through a person is far below anything a breaker notices. What protects people is earthing, which gives a fault current somewhere to go, and the RCD, which notices when 30 mA has gone missing from the loop and cuts off.
:::

:::check
Why must a fuse be connected in series with the circuit it protects, and not in parallel with it?
???
In series, every bit of the circuit's current passes through the fuse, so when the current grows dangerously large the fuse melts and breaks the only path. In parallel, the fuse would carry only some of the current, and melting it would leave the circuit's own path intact and still carrying the fault current. A guard has to stand in the doorway, not beside it.
:::

## The LED that wouldn't light

:::case The LED that wouldn't light
A friend follows an online video and builds a circuit on the kitchen table: two AA cells in a holder, one white LED, and a lead from each end of the holder to each leg of the LED. Nothing. He swaps the two leads over: still nothing. He presses the clips harder: nothing. Then he takes a red LED from your kit, adds your 68 Ω resistor, and connects it the same way. It lights at once. "The white LED is broken," he says, and reaches for the bin.

Clues from the datasheets:

- An AA cell has a nominal voltage of 1.5 V. Two in a row give 3.0 V.
- A red LED starts to pass current at about 1.8 to 2.4 V, typically 2.0 V.
- A white LED starts to pass current at about 2.8 to 3.8 V, typically 3.3 V.
- An LED passes current in one direction only.

Is the white LED broken? Why did it stay dark while the red one lit, and how would you light it safely?
???
It is almost certainly not broken. A white LED needs about 3.3 V across it before it passes much current, and two AA cells supply 3.0 V. There is not enough push. The red LED needs only about 2 V, so 3.0 V is plenty for it, with the 68 Ω resistor taking up the extra volt and holding the current to a safe 15 mA or so. Some white LEDs sit at the low end of their range and will glow faintly on 3.0 V; this one did not.

The other two classic reasons for a dark LED were ruled out by the friend himself. He swapped the leads, so it was not the wrong way round, and the red LED lit on the same clips, so the loop was complete.

To light the white LED, give it more push and keep the resistor: on your 9 V battery with the 330 Ω resistor, 9 − 3.3 = 5.7 V is left for the resistor, and 5.7 ÷ 330 is about 17 mA, comfortably under the 30 mA the LED can take. Never connect any LED straight across a battery without a resistor, even the red one on two AA cells: once an LED starts to conduct, nothing but the resistor stops the current from climbing far past what it survives.
:::
