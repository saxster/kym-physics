Chapter 12 ended with two things given a name instead of an explanation. The push that lit your LED reached the far end of the wire at 10⁸ m/s, and the comb pulled a stream of water across empty air. In both cases something reached across a gap with nothing visible in between, and the word for it is *field*. This chapter is about the other field you can feel with your hands, the one that holds a note to the fridge door, and about the discovery that it and the electric one are two faces of a single thing, which turns out to be light.

Here is the puzzle. Tip the coins out of a purse and run a fridge magnet over them. The ₹1 and ₹2 coins jump to it. So does one ₹5 coin. Another ₹5 coin, the same size, the same silvery look, the same "5" on its face, does not move at all. A steel spoon from the drawer may ignore the magnet too, while the steel fridge door does not. What decides which metals a magnet grabs, and why would two coins of the same value fall on different sides of the line?

## 13.1 Which metals stick?

:::scene Mumbai, the kitchen table

Gather what you can: every coin in the house, a steel spoon or two, a scrap of aluminium foil, a brass key, a steel safety pin, and the strongest fridge magnet you own. Before anything touches the magnet, write down what you expect. Most people expect that *magnets attract metals*. Children of three believe it, and most adults never find out otherwise, because the metals we meet most often, nails, pins and tins, happen to be the ones that stick.

:::predict
You hold the fridge magnet over, in turn: a ₹1 coin, a ₹2 coin, a ₹5 coin dated 2007, a ₹5 coin dated 2011, a steel spoon, the aluminium foil and the brass key. Which stick?
- All of them: they are all metal.
- All the coins and the spoon, which are steel; not the foil or the key.
- The ₹1, the ₹2 and the 2007 ₹5. Not the 2011 ₹5, the foil or the key. The spoon depends on the spoon.
???
The third, as far as the Reserve Bank's own notices let us say. The ₹1, ₹2 and 2007 ₹5 are made of a steel that magnets grab. The ₹5 coins issued from 2010 are nickel brass, a copper alloy with no iron in it, expected not to stick, though no source this chapter rests on has tested one, so your magnet is the judge. Foil and brass never stick. And a spoon can go either way, which is the strangest result on the table and the one this section must explain.
:::

Only a few metals respond strongly to a magnet: iron, nickel and cobalt, alloys of them with the right internal arrangement, and a rare one called gadolinium. Everything else responds so feebly that only sensitive instruments notice. These few are ==ferromagnetic==, after *ferrum*, the Latin for iron. A magnet lifts a plain iron nail that was never a magnet because it makes the nail into one, briefly: the end nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole, and opposite poles attract. Take the magnet away and most nails forget.

:::note Ferromagnetic
Strongly attracted by a magnet, and able to be made into one: iron, nickel, cobalt, gadolinium, and alloys such as ordinary steel. Most metals (copper, aluminium, brass, gold) are not.
:::

Why these metals and not others? Inside a ferromagnetic metal the atoms behave like tiny magnets, and they do not act alone: they line up with their neighbours in small regions called ==domains==, each a little magnet in its own right. In iron that has never met a magnet the domains point every which way and cancel, so the piece shows nothing. Bring a magnet near and the domains that point its way grow at the expense of the others, and some swing round, so the piece becomes a magnet with its near end the opposite pole to the one approaching. Heat and hard blows scramble the order; above 770 °C iron cannot be magnetised at all. Copper, aluminium and brass have no such tiny magnets to line up, so a fridge magnet passes over foil and keys as if they were paper.

:::fig c13_domains() | Inside one piece of iron. Each irregular region is a magnetic domain; its arrow shows the average magnetic moment. Before magnetisation, different directions largely cancel. Near a magnet, favourably oriented regions grow and moments can turn, giving the piece a net moment. Domain boundaries and changes are schematic and hugely enlarged.

:::note Domain
A small region inside a ferromagnetic metal in which the atoms' tiny magnets all point the same way. A magnet nearby makes the domains that point along it grow, and the piece becomes a magnet.
:::

Now the coins and the spoon. "Steel" is mostly iron, and "stainless steel" is iron with a good deal of chromium, 17 per cent in the coins, often with nickel as well. But what decides whether a steel is ferromagnetic is how its atoms are stacked, not simply whether iron is in it. In one family of stainless steels, called ferritic, the stacking lets the tiny magnets line up, and the metal is strongly magnetic. In another, called austenitic, which usually contains nickel and is what many spoons, pans and sinks are made of, it does not, and a magnet slides over it as if it were brass. Bending or pressing an austenitic steel hard can change the stacking in places and make them faintly magnetic, so a spoon's deeply pressed bowl may hold a magnet weakly where its handle does not. That is why the spoon "depends on the spoon", and why the only honest instruction is to test it.

The Reserve Bank publishes what its coins are made of. The 50 paise, ₹1 and ₹2 coins of the 2011 series are ferritic stainless steel, 83 per cent iron and 17 per cent chromium. The ₹5 coin of that series is nickel brass: 75 per cent copper, 20 per cent zinc, 5 per cent nickel, no iron at all. The ₹5 coins issued in 2007 were ferritic stainless steel, the same 83–17 recipe as the small coins. So two ₹5 coins from one purse can be different metals, and only one has the tiny magnets to line up. The 5 per cent of nickel in the other does not rescue it: nickel is ferromagnetic as a metal, but a little nickel dissolved in copper is a different material, and the only way to know how a material behaves is to test the material, not its ingredients.

:::home Sort your family's coins with a magnet
Every Indian coin has its year stamped on it. Lay out all the coins you can borrow, sorted by value and then by year, and beside each write your prediction: sticks or not. Then test each with the fridge magnet and record what happened. Expect the recent ₹1 and ₹2 coins to stick, and the ₹5 coins to split by year. Coins from 2019 onwards are a newer series whose metals this book has not checked, so treat them as a real experiment. Then do the same for the spoons, bowl and handle separately, the foil, the key and the pin. Two cautions: keep magnets away from phones and bank cards, and keep small strong magnets away from small children, who can swallow them.
:::

:::recall 12
In Chapter 12 a rubbed comb lifted scraps of paper. Will the fridge magnet lift the same scraps? And will the comb lift the ₹1 coin?
???
Neither. These are two different forces. The comb's charge pulls feebly on anything at all by separating the charges inside it, enough for paper scraps but never for a coin. The magnet pulls strongly, but only on the few ferromagnetic materials. A charge sitting still has no magnetic effect whatever, as Section 13.3 shows with a compass.
:::

:::careful
> "Steel is magnetic, so anything made of steel will stick to a magnet."

Steel is a family of metals, and whether a magnet grabs one depends on how its atoms are stacked, not on the label. The ferritic stainless steel of Indian coins is strongly magnetic; the austenitic steel of many spoons and sinks is not, except faintly where it has been pressed or bent. The same goes for "it has nickel in it": behaviour belongs to the material, not to its ingredients.
:::

:::check
A plain iron nail, which has never been a magnet, is picked up just as readily by the north end of a bar magnet as by its south end. Why is it never pushed away?
???
Whichever pole approaches, it magnetises the nail with the *opposite* pole nearest: a north end makes the near end of the nail a south pole, a south end makes it a north pole. Either way the nearest poles are unlike, and unlike attract. Repulsion needs two things that are already magnets, which is why two fridge magnets can push each other apart and a magnet and a nail never do.
:::

## 13.2 The Earth is a magnet

:::scene London, 1600

Sailors had steered by compass for centuries without anyone knowing why the needle pointed north, and they had a stock of beliefs about it, one being that garlic would spoil it. William Gilbert, in London, tested such claims and dismissed the garlic as "fable and falsehood". Then he did something better: he ground a lump of lodestone, a naturally magnetic rock, into a sphere, called it a *terrella*, a little Earth, and laid small needles on it. Over the sphere's equator a needle lay flat; towards its poles it tilted down, more steeply the nearer it got, just as compasses at sea were known to tilt as ships sailed north. Gilbert's conclusion, published in 1600, was that the Earth itself is a great magnet.

:::try Investigation 13.2
Make a compass. Stroke a steel sewing needle about twenty times with one end of your magnet, always the same way, lifting the magnet clear between strokes. Float the needle on a small leaf in a bowl of water away from steel, and wait: it turns and settles. Check it against a phone's compass app, then bring the fridge magnet near and watch which way it swings.
:::

A freely turning magnet settles with one end towards the north, and that end is *called* its north pole, which is where a trap lies.

:::predict
The north end of a compass needle points north. Unlike poles attract. So what kind of magnetic pole is near the Earth's geographic North Pole?
- A north magnetic pole, because the needle points towards it.
- A south magnetic pole.
- Neither: the Earth only steers the needle, it is not a magnet.
???
A south magnetic pole. The needle's north end points north because something *unlike* it is pulling, and unlike means south. Textbooks admit the muddle: the geographic term "North Pole" has come to be used, incorrectly, for the magnetic pole near it.
:::

Two facts about poles separate them from the charges of Chapter 12. First, they always come in pairs. Cut a magnet in half and you do not get a lone north and a lone south; you get two complete magnets, each with both. Nobody has ever found a single pole on its own, while a single charge, an electron, is the most ordinary thing in the world. Second, poles are not charges. A north pole does not attract a comb's negative charge or repel its positive; it attracts south poles and ferromagnetic materials, nothing else. "Like repels, unlike attracts" sounds the same for both, but the two kinds of like are different things.

To describe what a magnet does to the space around it, physicists use a ==magnetic field==. At every point near a magnet a small compass needle turns to point a certain way; the field there is that direction, and it is stronger where the needle is pulled round more firmly. Join the directions up and you get field lines, which leave the magnet's north end, loop through the air to its south end, and continue through the magnet to close the loop. The lines are a drawing, a record of what a compass would do at each place. Nothing flows along them, and iron filings lie along them for the same reason the needle does: each filing becomes a tiny magnet and turns.

:::note Magnetic field
What a magnet (or a current) sets up in the space around it: at every point, a direction (the way a compass's north end points there) and a strength. Measured in tesla (T); the Earth's is about 40 to 50 millionths of a tesla.
:::

:::five Magnetic field
? What is it?
The condition a magnet or an electric current sets up in the space around it, giving every point a magnetic direction (the way a compass needle's north end points there) and a strength, measured in tesla (T).
? Why is it called this?
*Magnet* comes from Magnesia, the district where lodestones were found in antiquity; Michael Faraday introduced *magnetic field* in the 1840s to describe the region of space around a magnet or wire where magnetic forces act.
? What problem did it solve?
A magnet swings a compass needle and pulls on steel right through empty air or a wooden tabletop without touching them; the field replaces mysterious action at a distance with a measurable property at the exact spot where the compass sits.
? Why does it work?
Moving charges in a wire or aligned electron magnets in iron modify the space around them so that any other magnet or moving charge placed at a point experiences a torque or sideways force determined by the field right at that point.
? How does it help?
Mapping the field's direction and strength lets you navigate with a compass in the Earth's 44 μT field, calculate the sideways force inside an electric motor, and see why electric trams ruined the Colaba Observatory's readings a kilometre away.
?? How do we know?
Move a small compass or a phone's magnetometer step by step around a bar magnet or a current-carrying wire: at every point it settles along a definite direction and reports a strength that weakens predictably with distance.
?? What it isn't
Field lines are a drawing, not physical threads in space: the magnetic field exists smoothly in the gaps between the drawn lines, and iron filings only form separate threads because the magnetised filings stick end to end.
?? Where does it stop working?
A magnetic field exerts zero force on an electric charge that is sitting still relative to it; it pushes only on ferromagnetic materials, other magnets, and charges that are moving.
:::

Gilbert's terrella showed that the Earth acts like a magnet, and modern measurements fill in the numbers. The field is weak: a textbook's round figure is 5 × 10⁻⁵ T, fifty microtesla. At Mumbai, from the world magnetic model used by navigation systems, it is about 43.6 μT, 87 per cent of the round figure, and it does not point along the ground. Only 38.4 μT of it is horizontal; the rest points down into the earth, so the whole field dips about 28° below the horizontal. A compass needle, pivoted to swing only sideways, shows the horizontal part and hides the dip, which is what Gilbert's needle on the terrella revealed. In Mumbai the horizontal part points within a fraction of a degree of true north. What makes the Earth's field is a question this chapter's sources do not answer, and the honest thing is to leave it as one.

:::fig c13_earth_field() | The Earth's magnetic field, drawn as a bar magnet's whose *south* magnetic pole lies near the geographic North Pole. At Mumbai, 19° north, the field dips 28° below the horizontal: of its 43.6 microtesla, 38.4 are horizontal and point almost exactly at true north and 20.7 point down into the ground. A compass shows only the horizontal part.
:::live magnetometer

A phone has a magnetometer, a small sensor that measures the magnetic field in three directions, and the free phyphox app can show its reading. Expect a total around 40 to 45 μT in Mumbai, and expect it to wobble: phone sensors are not calibrated like an observatory's, and any steel nearby adds to the reading. Treat it as rough, then notice how it leaps when the fridge magnet comes within a hand's breadth.

That word, observatory, matters for the end of this chapter. From 1841 a magnetic observatory at Colaba, at the southern tip of Bombay, kept watch on the Earth's field, and from 1846 it recorded it every hour. Its superintendent Charles Chambers published its results in London; Nanabhoy Moos, the first Indian to direct it, compiled sixty years of them into volumes. By 1904 it had a problem, and the problem is the chapter's case.

:::story earth-magnet

:::careful
> "The Earth's North Pole is a north magnetic pole; that's why the compass points to it."

A compass's north end is attracted there, and attraction is between *unlike* poles, so the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole is a south pole. The names clash because the needle's ends were named after the directions they point, long before anyone understood magnets. Keep the two meanings of "north" apart and the muddle disappears.
:::

:::check
A compass in Mumbai points north. Does the Earth's magnetic field at Mumbai point north?
???
Mostly it points *down*. The field at Mumbai dips 28° below the horizontal, so a needle free to swing in any direction would tilt its north end into the ground. An ordinary compass is held level and shows only the horizontal part, which does point north. Gilbert saw the dip on his little Earth four centuries ago.
:::

## 13.3 Electricity makes magnetism

:::scene Copenhagen, 21 April 1820

Hans Christian Ørsted was lecturing, with a battery and wires on the bench and a compass lying nearby. When he connected the wire and a current flowed, the compass needle twitched, so slightly that the audience did not notice. Ørsted did, and in the following weeks he tested it: reverse the current, and the needle twitched the other way. In July he published four pages in Latin, and the news crossed Europe. For the first time something electric had moved something magnetic: the first evidence, a textbook says, that the movement of charges had "any connection with magnets" at all.

Before this section explains the twitch, notice what does not twitch. A charged comb held near a compass does nothing to the needle as a magnet: charge sitting still has no magnetic effect. Charge *moving* does, and that one word is the hinge of the chapter.

:::predict
A wire runs north–south directly over a compass, and when a current flows the needle swings east. You reverse the current along the same wire. Now the needle:
- Swings east again, since the current is the same size.
- Swings west.
- Does not move: a current either side of the compass cancels out.
???
Swings west. Ørsted saw exactly this, and it told him the direction of the current matters, which no earlier idea about electricity had suggested. Whatever the current makes around itself has a direction that follows the current's own.
:::

What the current makes is a magnetic field, and its shape is unlike a bar magnet's. Around a long straight wire the field lines are circles centred on the wire. The rule for which way round: point your right thumb along the current (conventional current, + to −) and your fingers curl the way the field goes. The field is strongest close to the wire and weakens in inverse proportion to the distance: twice as far, half as strong. A current of 1 A makes 20 μT at 1 cm, 10 μT at 2 cm, and so on. Set that against Mumbai's horizontal field of 38 μT. A wire carrying 1 A, laid north–south a centimetre above a compass, adds 20 μT sideways at right angles to the Earth's field, and the needle turns to follow the sum: about 27° from north. To match the Earth's horizontal field outright at that distance would take 1.9 A. These are small currents, and Ørsted's were not large either.

:::fig c13_wire_field() | The magnetic field of a straight wire. Left: the field lines are circles round the wire, closer together where the field is stronger; with the current flowing up, the field runs anticlockwise seen from above, the way a right hand's fingers curl when the thumb points along the current. Right: a compass 1 cm under a north–south wire carrying 1 A. The wire's 20 microtesla, at right angles to Mumbai's 38, swing the needle about 27°.
:::live field3d

:::home Ørsted's twitch
Ask an adult to be with you. You need one AA cell, about 30 cm of thin insulated wire with a centimetre of bare copper at each end, and a compass: a pocket one, or the compass app on a phone laid flat. Let the needle settle, then lay the wire directly over the compass, running north–south along the needle, about a centimetre above it. Touch the two bare ends to the two ends of the cell for one second, no longer, and watch the needle swing sideways. Let it settle, then touch again with the wire the other way round: it swings the other way. Three cautions. A wire straight across a cell is a short circuit: some amperes flow, and wire and cell warm quickly, which is why each touch lasts a second, with a pause between. Use one AA cell only, never the 9 V battery and never anything else. And with a phone, move the wire about if the needle is slow to respond: the sensor sits somewhere inside.
:::

Wind the wire into a coil of many turns and the circles of field from each turn add up inside it, and the coil's field looks exactly like a bar magnet's, with a north end and a south end; iron filings lie along it in the same pattern. Put an iron core inside and the core's domains line up too, so the field grows many times larger: that is an ==electromagnet==, a magnet you can switch off, the thing that lifts scrap steel in a yard. Within months of Ørsted's twitch, André-Marie Ampère in Paris had worked out the mathematics and proposed something bolder: that *all* magnetism comes from moving charge, including a lodestone's or a fridge magnet's, whose field is the summed effect of its electrons. Modern physics agrees, with a caution: electrons do not literally orbit or spin like tiny tops, and the textbook that draws them that way says so. They behave like tiny magnets, and a ferromagnetic metal is one in which those tiny magnets can be made to agree.

:::note Electromagnet
A coil of wire carrying a current, usually around an iron core, which acts as a magnet while the current flows and stops when it stops. Its field has the same shape as a bar magnet's.
:::

:::evidence Ørsted's lecture compass and Ampère's two wires | 1820
When Ørsted's four-page Latin pamphlet reached Paris in September 1820, François Arago repeated the compass twitch before the French Academy of Sciences on 11 September. Sitting in the audience was André-Marie Ampère. Within a single week Ampère asked a sharp question: if a wire carrying a current acts on a magnet, and a magnet is really just hidden currents inside iron, shouldn't two current-carrying wires push or pull directly on *each other*, with no iron or lodestone anywhere in the room? By 18 September 1820 he had built a delicate frame holding two parallel straight wires so one could swing freely beside the other. When both carried currents in the same direction, they pulled together; when one current was reversed, they pushed apart, with a force proportional to both currents and inversely proportional to the distance between them. Today we even define the unit of electric current and magnetic field from that link between electricity and magnetism: two experiments in 1820 turned two separate chapters of physics into one.
:::

:::story oersted

:::careful
> "A charged balloon should swing a compass needle; charge and magnetism are the same thing."

A charge sitting still has no magnetic effect at all, however much of it there is. Only *moving* charge makes a magnetic field: a current in a wire, or the electrons inside a magnet. Charge and magnetism are connected, which is this chapter's discovery, but they are not the same thing, and the field lines round a wire record what a compass would do; nothing streams out along them.
:::

:::check
Your wire over the compass carries 1 A and swings the needle 27°. What two separate changes would each make the swing bigger, and roughly how?
???
Double the current: the wire's field doubles to 40 μT at the same distance, and the needle swings to about 46°, since 40 against 38 is nearly an even match. Or halve the distance: at half a centimetre the field is also doubled. The field grows in proportion to the current and in inverse proportion to the distance.
:::

## 13.4 The first motor

:::scene Royal Institution, London, 3 September 1821

Seventeen months after the twitch in Copenhagen, Michael Faraday, a bookbinder's apprentice turned laboratory assistant, stood a magnet upright in a dish of mercury and let a wire hang down into the mercury beside it, free to swing. When a current flowed, the wire began to circle the magnet, round and round, for as long as the current lasted. It was the first electric motor, and it ran on nothing but a current, a magnet and a force nobody had named yet. Do not copy the apparatus: mercury is poisonous.

The natural way to describe what Faraday saw is that the magnet pushed the electricity round the dish, as if stirring a fluid. Keep hold of Chapter 12: there is no electric fluid, only charge moving in a solid wire. So what did the magnet push? First, a question that catches most people out.

:::predict
Two long straight wires hang side by side a few centimetres apart, carrying the same current in the same direction, so each sits in the other's magnetic field. Do the wires:
- Push apart, like two like charges.
- Pull together.
- Do nothing to each other, since neither is a magnet.
???
Pull together. Currents in the same direction attract and currents in opposite directions repel, the reverse of the rule for charges. Each wire feels a force because it carries a current *through the other's field*, and when you follow the rule below carefully the direction comes out as a pull. It is the force Faraday's wire felt from his magnet.
:::

The rule is this. A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field feels a force at right angles to both the current and the field: not along the wire, not along the field, but sideways to both. Reverse the current and the force reverses; reverse the field and it reverses again. The force acts on the moving charges, and since they are in the wire, the wire is pushed; the magnet, by Chapter 3's rule, is pushed back equally. Nothing is stirred. For a wire of length *L* sitting at right angles to a magnetic field of strength *B* and carrying a current *I*, the sideways force is simply the product of the three: *F* = *BIL*. It is small for everyday currents: with inputs that are guesses, 1 A along 2 cm of wire in a field of 0.1 T near a small magnet gives 0.1 T × 1 A × 0.02 m = 0.002 N, the weight of a fifth of a gram, which is why a toy motor uses fine wire and many turns.

:::eq
**magnetic force on a current**: *F* = *B* × *I* × *L*
:::

:::fig c13_motor_force() | The force on a current in a magnetic field. Left: the field runs left to right, the current up the page, and the force points into the page (the cross), at right angles to both. Right: a loop seen end-on between a magnet's poles. Current comes towards you on the left (dot) and goes away on the right (cross), so one side is pushed up and the other down. A split-ring commutator and its brushes reverse the current every half-turn to keep the loop turning the same way.

Now bend the wire into a rectangular loop and hang it between the poles of a magnet. Current runs one way along one long side and the other way along the opposite side, so one side is pushed up and the other down, and the loop turns. Left alone it would turn until it lay flat across the field and stop, the two forces pulling straight against each other. The trick that keeps it going is a split metal ring on the axle, the commutator. It turns against sliding contacts called brushes, swapping the coil's connections every half-turn and reversing its current. The turning effect then keeps the same direction. That is an ==electric motor==. A mixer-grinder, a ceiling fan, a local train: each is a coil in a field, pushed sideways by this one force, turning electrical energy into motion. Double the turns in the coil and you double the turning effect, which is why real motors are wound with hundreds.

:::note Electric motor
A coil carrying a current in a magnetic field. The sideways force on the current turns the coil; a commutator and brushes reverse the current every half-turn so that it keeps turning. It converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.
:::

:::home The simplest motor (adult-led)
This one is for an adult to build with you, and it runs for seconds at a time. You need one AA cell, a small round neodymium magnet (a strong one, sold for crafts) and 15 cm of stiff bare copper wire. Stand the cell on the magnet, which clings to its flat negative end. Bend the wire into a shape that balances on the cell's positive tip and hangs down both sides so that its ends just brush the magnet's edge. When both ends touch, the wire spins: Faraday's dish without the mercury. Current runs down the wire, through the magnet and back into the cell, and the magnet's field pushes the current-carrying wire sideways, all the way round. Run it for ten or twenty seconds, then lift it off: the wire is a near short circuit, and wire and cell warm up. Neodymium magnets snap together hard enough to pinch skin, must never be near small children, and spoil phones and cards.
:::

:::school
IGCSE 4.5 calls this the "force on a conductor" and the "d.c. motor", and asks you to find the direction of the force using **Fleming's left-hand rule**: hold the thumb, first finger and second finger of your *left* hand mutually at right angles; point the **F**irst finger along the **F**ield (N to S) and the se**C**ond finger along the conventional **C**urrent (+ to −), and your **Th**umb points in the direction of the **Th**rust (force or motion). Keep your right hand for the next section, where **Fleming's right-hand rule** gives the direction of the induced current in a generator when a wire is moved by an outside force across a field.
:::

:::careful
> "The magnet pushes the electricity round the wire."

There is nothing in the wire to stir. The field pushes sideways on moving charges, and because the charges are inside the wire, the wire moves. Switch off the current and the force vanishes; reverse it and the force reverses. A motor converts energy, electrical to motion; the same current comes out of it as went in, as Chapter 12 insists.
:::

:::check
A small motor's coil has 50 turns. The maker rewinds it with 100 turns of the same wire carrying the same current. What happens to its turning effect, and why?
???
It doubles. Each turn is a loop pushed by the same sideways force, so a hundred loops are pushed round twice as hard as fifty. The turning effect grows in proportion to the number of turns, and to the current and the field.
:::

## 13.5 Magnetism makes electricity

:::scene Royal Institution, London, 29 August 1831

Ten years on from the spinning wire, Faraday was still chasing the obvious question: if a current makes a magnet, should a magnet not make a current? He wound two separate coils on opposite sides of an iron ring, connected one to a battery through a switch and the other to a compass needle, and closed the switch. The needle jumped, then settled to zero while the current flowed steadily. He opened the switch and the needle jumped the other way, then settled. A steady current in the first coil did nothing to the second; only the *change*, at switching on or off, made anything happen. Eleven years after Ørsted, the connection ran both ways.

The result deserves a prediction. Faraday soon simplified it to a bar magnet and a coil of wire wired to a meter.

:::predict
You push the magnet into the coil and the meter needle kicks to the right. Now you hold the magnet perfectly still inside the coil. The meter:
- Shows a steady reading to the right while the magnet stays in.
- Falls back to zero.
- Kicks to the left.
???
Falls back to zero. A magnet sitting still inside a coil, however strong, produces nothing. Pull it out and the needle kicks to the left; push it in faster and the kick is bigger. Current appears only while the magnetic field through the coil is *changing*.
:::

This is ==electromagnetic induction==, and its rule is short. Whenever the magnetic field threading a loop of wire changes, a push appears round the loop, a voltage such as a battery gives, and if the loop is closed a current flows. How the change is made does not matter: move a magnet, move the coil, switch a nearby current on or off, or turn the loop so that the field passes through it at a different angle. Faster change gives a bigger push; more turns give proportionally more, so a coil of two hundred turns gives two hundred times the push of one. The induced current always runs the way that opposes the change that made it: a magnet pushed towards a coil induces a current whose own field pushes the magnet back, and a magnet pulled away induces one that pulls it back. One MIT course sums it up cleanly: the system always responds in the way that opposes the change, and shows it with a magnet dropped towards a thick copper ring: it slows as it approaches, held up by the current it is inducing.

:::note Electromagnetic induction
A changing magnetic field through a loop of wire produces a voltage round the loop, and a current if the loop is closed. No change, no current. The induced current always opposes the change that made it.
:::

:::five Electromagnetic induction
? What is it?
The production of a voltage round a loop of wire whenever the magnetic field threading through the loop changes.
? Why is it called this?
Faraday discovered it in August 1831 and called it *induction*, from Latin *inducere*, to lead in, because a changing magnetic field calls forth a voltage in a nearby coil without any wire touching between them.
? What problem did it solve?
Ørsted had shown in 1820 that an electric current makes magnetism, and for eleven years physicists failed to make a stationary magnet produce a current; Faraday saw that the secret was *change*, not steady strength.
? Why does it work?
A changing magnetic field creates an electric field that curls in a loop around the changing region; any charges in a wire placed along that loop are pushed round by that electric field, and the induced current always runs in the direction that opposes the change.
? How does it help?
Every power-station generator in the world spins magnets and coils past each other to generate alternating current by induction, and every grid transformer steps voltages up or down using a changing field in an iron core.
?? How do we know?
Connect a coil of wire to a sensitive meter and push a magnet in: the needle kicks one way while the magnet moves, drops dead to zero while the magnet sits still inside, and kicks the opposite way when you pull the magnet out.
?? What it isn't
A magnet sitting still beside a coil never generates electricity, and a generator does not create energy out of magnetism; it converts the mechanical work of steam, water or muscle that turns the shaft against magnetic drag.
?? Where does it stop working?
A transformer relies entirely on a *changing* magnetic field in its core, so if you connect its primary coil to a steady DC battery, it gives a single brief kick at switch-on and zero voltage thereafter.
:::

:::fig c13_induction() | A magnet and a coil wired to a meter. Left: the magnet moves in and the needle kicks one way. Middle: the magnet is held still inside the coil and the needle reads zero, however strong the magnet. Right: the magnet moves out and the needle kicks the other way. Faster motion, bigger kick.
:::live induction

:::five Lenz's law
? What is it?
The rule, stated by Heinrich Lenz in 1834, that any induced current always flows in the direction whose own magnetic field opposes the change that caused it.
? Why is it called this?
Named after the Baltic German physicist Emil (Heinrich) Lenz, who formulated it three years after Faraday's discovery of induction to give a single universal rule for the sign of every induced current.
? What problem did it solve?
Faraday's law tells you how *big* the induced voltage is from the rate of change of the magnetic field, whereas Lenz's law tells you *which way* the induced current flows without having to guess.
? Why does it work?
It is conservation of energy (Chapter 7) applied to electromagnetism: if pushing a north pole into a coil induced a south pole on the near face, the coil would suck the magnet in faster and faster, creating both kinetic and electrical energy out of nothing. Instead, the near face becomes a north pole that pushes back, so your hand must do mechanical work equal to the electrical energy generated.
? How does it help?
It explains why a generator becomes harder to crank the moment you switch on a heavy load, and how eddy-current brakes slow high-speed trains and rollercoaster cars smoothly without brake pads touching a wheel.
?? How do we know?
Drop a strong neodymium magnet down a vertical copper tube: although copper is not ferromagnetic and does not attract a stationary magnet, the falling magnet induces swirling currents in the copper that repel it from below and attract it from above, making it drift down like a feather in treacle.
?? What it isn't
It does not mean the coil permanently repels magnets; pull the same north pole *out* of the coil and the induced current reverses immediately to make a south pole on the near face that tries to hold the magnet back.
?? Where does it stop working?
If the loop of wire is open (switched off), an induced voltage still appears across its ends, but because no current can flow, there is no opposing magnetic force and the coil turns or the magnet falls freely.
:::

Every power station in the world runs on the result. Spin a coil between the poles of a magnet, or a magnet past a coil, and the field through the coil changes continuously, so a push appears round it continuously: one way as a side of the coil sweeps past the north pole, the other way past the south. How do you take that current out of a spinning coil without twisting the wires into a knot? In an alternating-current (AC) generator (an alternator), each end of the coil is permanently joined to its own continuous smooth brass ring on the axle, a ==slip ring==, with a carbon brush pressing against each ring; as the coil turns, each brush stays with its own side of the coil, so the output voltage naturally swings positive and negative every half-turn (50 times a second in India). Compare that with the DC motor of Section 13.4, which used a single *split-ring commutator* whose two halves swap brushes every half-turn so current keeps acting in one direction. Such a machine is a ==generator==, built almost exactly like a motor: turn a motor's shaft and it becomes one. What a generator does *not* do is make energy. Something has to turn the coil against the magnetic drag of Lenz's law, and the work done turning it is what becomes electrical energy: steam in a thermal station, falling water in a hydro station, wind on a wind farm. The company that generates a large share of Mumbai's electricity runs about 930 MW of thermal plant and 447 MW of hydro at Bhira, Bhivpuri and Khopoli in the Western Ghats.

:::note Generator
A coil turned in a magnetic field, or a magnet turned past a coil. The changing field through the coil induces an alternating voltage, taken out through continuous slip rings and brushes. The work done to turn it against Lenz's-law magnetic drag is what becomes electrical energy; nothing is created.
:::

Faraday's iron ring was also the first ==transformer==. A changing current in one coil (the primary, with *N*_p turns) makes a changing field in the ring, which induces a voltage in the other coil (the secondary, with *N*_s turns); wind the second coil with more turns than the first and the voltage comes out larger (a step-up transformer), with fewer and it comes out smaller (step-down), in exact proportion to the turns ratio: *V*_s ÷ *V*_p = *N*_s ÷ *N*_p. In an ideal transformer that wastes no energy as heat, the electrical power going into the primary equals the power coming out of the secondary (*V*_p *I*_p = *V*_s *I*_s), so stepping the voltage *up* ten times steps the current *down* to a tenth. It works only with a current that keeps changing, which is why the grid runs on alternating current. Electricity crosses the country at up to 400,000 V and is stepped down near your home to 230 V, because the heating loss in a long transmission cable of resistance *R* is *P*_loss = *I*²*R*: send the same power at a thousand times the voltage and the current is a thousandth, so the cable wastes a millionth as much energy heating the sky. A phone charger is more than a transformer: it steps 230 V AC down to about 5 V and uses diodes (Section 12.4) to rectify it into the steady DC a phone battery needs.

:::note Transformer
Two coils on one iron core. A changing current in the primary coil (*N*_p turns) induces a voltage in the secondary coil (*N*_s turns) in proportion to the turns (*V*_s ÷ *V*_p = *N*_s ÷ *N*_p), while keeping power *V*_p *I*_p = *V*_s *I*_s conserved in an ideal transformer. It works only with alternating current.
:::

:::eq
**transformer turns ratio**: *V*_s ÷ *V*_p = *N*_s ÷ *N*_p
**ideal transformer power**: *V*_p × *I*_p = *V*_s × *I*_s
**transmission line heating loss**: *P*_loss = *I*² × *R*_line
:::

:::work
A power station must send 46 MW (46,000,000 W) along transmission cables whose total resistance is *R*_line = 2.0 Ω.
At 230,000 V: current *I* = 46,000,000 W ÷ 230,000 V = 200 A; line loss *I*²*R* = (200 A)² × 2.0 Ω = 80,000 W (80 kW, under 0.2% wasted).
At 23,000 V (ten times lower voltage): current *I* = 2,000 A; line loss *I*²*R* = (2,000 A)² × 2.0 Ω = 8,000,000 W (8 MW, a hundred times more wasted).
:::

:::careful
> "A magnet next to a wire makes electricity."

A magnet sitting beside a wire, or inside a coil, makes nothing at all, however strong it is. Only a *changing* field does: move the magnet, move the coil, switch a current on or off, or spin one past the other. And a generator does not make energy; it converts the work done to turn it. Stop turning and the lights go out.
:::

:::check
A coil connected to a meter gives a small kick when a magnet is pushed in. Without changing the magnet or the speed, name two ways to make the kick larger, and say what would happen if you pushed the magnet in and left it there.
???
Wind more turns onto the coil (double the turns, double the push), or move the magnet faster so that the field through the coil changes more quickly. Pushing the magnet in and leaving it gives one kick as it enters, then nothing: with the field no longer changing, the induced voltage is zero until something moves.

## 13.6 Light is electromagnetic

:::scene London, 1865

By the 1860s the rules were all in hand. Charges make electric fields. Magnetic field lines have no ends, because poles come in pairs. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field: Faraday's induction. A current makes a magnetic field: Ørsted's twitch, to which James Clerk Maxwell, a Scottish physicist working in London, added one piece of his own: a *changing electric field* makes a magnetic field too, with no current anywhere. He wrote the four rules as equations and asked what they allowed in empty space. The answer was a ripple. A changing electric field makes a changing magnetic field, which makes a changing electric field, and the pair travels on together, each making the other, at a speed fixed by two numbers already measured with coils and capacitors. Put them in and the speed comes out at 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s: the speed of light, as Fizeau had measured it across Paris. Maxwell wrote: "We can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena." Light was a wave of electric and magnetic fields. The "medium" he assumed was later found unnecessary; the waves cross truly empty space.

:::recall 10
In Chapter 10 most people drew arrows leaving the eye to touch what it sees. Now that you know what light is, describe what actually happens when you see a lamp across the room.
???
Charges in the lamp's hot filament or glowing LED shake back and forth, and the shaking sends ripples of electric and magnetic field in every direction at 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Some cross the room and enter your eye, where they are absorbed. Nothing leaves your eye. With the lamp off, no ripples are made and you see nothing, however hard you look.
:::

:::predict
A radio in the kitchen plays music that left a transmitter across the city. Sunlight comes through the window. Which is true?
- They are different things: radio is a kind of electricity in the air, light is a kind of wave.
- They are the same kind of wave, differing only in how fast they wiggle.
- Radio is a wave; light is a stream of tiny particles, so they cannot be compared.
???
The same kind of wave, differing only in frequency. Radio waves wiggle a few hundred million times a second and are metres long; visible light wiggles about a million times faster and is less than a thousandth of a millimetre long. Both are ripples of electric and magnetic field travelling at one speed. (Chapter 15 has a surprise about the third option, which does not undo this one.)
:::

What makes such a wave? Shake a charge. A charge sitting still has an electric field; a charge moving steadily, a current, has a magnetic field as well; but only a charge that is speeding up, slowing down or changing direction sends out a ripple that carries on without it. Steady currents do not radiate. The current in a radio antenna reverses millions of times a second, so its charges are forever changing direction and the antenna pours out waves; the charges in a hot filament or a glowing LED jiggle far faster, and what they pour out is light.

:::fig c13_em_wave() | Left: an electromagnetic wave. The electric field (vertical) and the magnetic field (horizontal) wiggle at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, and keep each other going. Right: the ladder of light, one kind of wave at many frequencies, with real rungs from a 50 Hz mains wave six thousand kilometres long, through GMRT's metre waves, Bose's 5 mm, a remote's 940 nm infrared and visible light at 400 to 750 nm, to ultraviolet and X-rays. The steps are powers of ten.

Maxwell died before anyone made his waves on purpose. In Karlsruhe, from 1887, Heinrich Hertz did it with a spark: a current jumping a gap shook its charges violently, and a second loop of wire across the room, with its own tiny gap, sparked in answer. He measured their speed, which was the speed of light, and showed that they reflected, refracted and interfered as light does. The story goes that when students asked what use the waves were, he said none whatever; there is no record of it, so take it as a story.

:::scene Calcutta, 1895

At Presidency College in Calcutta, Jagadish Chandra Bose built apparatus for very short waves, about 5 mm long, a frequency of about 60 GHz. In a public demonstration that year, reportedly chaired by the Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal, his invisible waves passed through walls to a receiver some twenty metres away, where they rang a bell and set off gunpowder. Accounts differ on how many walls and on the date, so hold the details loosely; the achievement is not in doubt, and in January 1897 Bose lectured on it at the Royal Institution in London, Faraday's old home, at Lord Rayleigh's invitation.

Set all the waves on one ladder and the family resemblance appears. At the long-wavelength end, India's mains, swinging at 50 Hz, counts as a wave six thousand kilometres long. The giant metrewave radio telescope near Pune collects waves about a metre long, around 300 MHz. Bose's were 5 mm. A typical television remote talks to the set with infrared of about 940 nm, which your eye cannot see and a phone camera can. Visible light runs from about 750 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet): 400 to 750 million million wiggles a second. Beyond violet lie ultraviolet, down to about 10 nm, then X-rays and gamma rays. One wave, one speed, a ladder of frequencies, and every rung crosses empty space, which is how the Sun's light and warmth reach Mumbai across a hundred and fifty million kilometres of nothing.

:::story maxwell

:::story bose-waves

:::careful
> "Radio waves come from the current flowing in the antenna."

A steady current makes a steady magnetic field, and nothing travels away from it. Waves come only from charges that are *accelerating*: speeding up, slowing down or reversing. An antenna radiates because its current reverses millions of times a second. Shake a charge and you make a wave; let it drift steadily and you do not.
:::

:::check
In an electromagnetic wave travelling east, the electric field wiggles up and down. In which direction does the magnetic field wiggle, and in which direction does neither of them point?
???
North and south: the magnetic field is at right angles to the electric field and to the direction of travel. Neither field ever points east, along the wave's motion. Both wiggle sideways to it, which is what "transverse" meant in Maxwell's sentence.
:::

## The trams and the magnetometer

:::case The trams and the magnetometer
The Colaba observatory had recorded the Earth's magnetic field at the southern tip of Bombay every hour since 1846. Around 1904 its directors began a second observatory at Alibag, down the coast across the harbour, ran both at once for two years to match one record against the other, and then moved the magnetometers out of Bombay for good. The reason was that the city was about to electrify its trams: on 7 May 1907 the first electric tram ran from the Municipal Office to Crawford Market.

Some clues, with the guessed inputs marked:

- A tram line is fed with direct current, out along an overhead wire and back along the rails. For a line with several trams running, a few hundred amperes is a reasonable guess; take 500 A.
- Section 13.3's rule: a straight current makes 20 μT at 1 cm for each ampere, falling off in proportion to distance.
- Mumbai's horizontal field is about 38,400 nT. The observatory's purpose was to track the slow, small changes in it, hour to hour and year to year.

Why would trams a few hundred metres away spoil the readings, and why move the whole observatory out of the city instead of shielding the instruments?
???
A tram line is a long straight current, and every long straight current is a magnet, exactly as Ørsted's wire was. Scale Section 13.3's number: 500 A at 200 m gives 20 μT × 500 ÷ 20,000, which is 0.5 μT, or 500 nT; at a kilometre it is still 100 nT. Against Mumbai's 38,400 nT that is about one part in eighty, which sounds small until you remember what the observatory was for: tracking changes far smaller than that, hour by hour. Worse, a tram's current is not steady. It rises as trams start, falls as they stop and vanishes at night, so the stray field would come and go with the timetable, forging "storms" in the record that were really the 5:30 service to Crawford Market.

Shielding is no answer, because a magnetometer's whole job is to feel the Earth's field, and anything that kept the trams' field out would keep the Earth's out too. The only cure was distance. Alibag is far enough from any tram for the stray field to fade to nothing, and the Earth's field there is almost the same as at Colaba: about 43.4 μT, dipping 27°. Running both observatories from 1904 to 1906 let the directors, Moos among them, tie the new record to the old. The 500 A and the 200 m are guesses; the physics that drove a scientific institution out of a city is the rule you tested with one AA cell and a compass.
:::
