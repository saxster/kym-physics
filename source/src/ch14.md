Chapter 13 ended with light itself: a ripple in the same fields that a magnet and a coil of wire make, pouring out of the Sun and caught, at its long-wavelength end, by the dishes of GMRT in the hills beyond Pune. This chapter turns round and looks at what that light shows us when we look up. A Sun that climbs and sinks. A Moon that changes shape every night. A sea that covers the causeway at Haji Ali and uncovers it again, twice a day. These are the oldest questions in physics, older than Aristotle, and the answers to every one of them come from three bodies moving in simple ways.

Here is the puzzle to carry. In early January the Earth is nearer the Sun than at any other time of the year: about five million kilometres nearer than it will be in early July. The sunlight falling on the whole planet is about seven per cent stronger in January than in July. And January is Mumbai's coolest month. At the same moment, in Australia, it is high summer, under the same Sun at the same distance. Whatever makes the seasons, it cannot be how far away the Sun is. By the end of this chapter you will know what it is, and you will have measured it on your kitchen table with a torch.

## 14.1 Day and night

:::scene Kusumapura, near today's Patna, 499 CE

Every clear night the stars wheel slowly across the sky from east to west. The Sun does the same by day, and so does the Moon. For almost everyone who has ever lived, the explanation was obvious: the sky turns. You can see it turning. Around 499 CE, in Kusumapura on the Ganges, a mathematician named Aryabhata, then about twenty-three, wrote down a different idea in his *Aryabhatiya*. The sky, he said, stands still. It is the Earth that turns, once a day. He gave a picture to go with the claim: a person in a boat drifting down a river sees the trees on the bank slide backwards, although the trees are not moving. In the same way, people on the turning Earth see the fixed stars slide west.

Your first objection is probably the one everyone has had for fifteen hundred years. If Mumbai is being carried round the Earth's axis, it is moving fast: a point at Mumbai's latitude covers about 1,576 kilometres every hour, faster than any aeroplane you have flown in. Surely you would feel that. But think back to Chapter 2. You feel *changes* in motion: the lurch when the local brakes, the push in your back when it pulls away. Steady motion, with nothing to jolt you, feels exactly like standing still. Aryabhata's boat makes the same point with water and trees.

:::predict
You are sitting in a local at Dadar, waiting to leave. Through the window, the train on the next track begins to slide slowly backwards. Looking only at that train, can you tell which of the two has started to move?
- Yes: you can see which train is moving
- No: the view through the window is the same either way
- Only if you feel a jolt or look at the platform
???
The view through the window is the same either way, so the second answer is right, and the third tells you how to settle it: find something else to check against. Astronomers were in exactly this position for more than a thousand years. Watching the stars rise and set cannot tell you whether the sky turns or the Earth does; the two pictures predict the same view from the deck. Deciding between them needed a new kind of evidence, and it came from a pendulum.
:::

:::fig c14_boat() | Aryabhata's argument. Left: from a drifting boat, the bank seems to slide backwards. Right: from the turning Earth, the stars seem to slide west at 15° an hour. In both pictures the thing that seems to move is standing still, and the mover feels nothing.

:::evidence Foucault's pendulum | 1851
By the 1600s most astronomers had accepted that the Earth turns, but nobody had *shown* it. In 1851, in Paris, Léon Foucault hung a bob of about 28 kilograms from a wire about 67 metres long under the dome of the Panthéon and set it swinging. A pendulum keeps swinging in the same plane; nothing pushes it sideways. Yet hour by hour the line of its swing crept round the floor. The pendulum had not turned. The floor had, and the building, and Paris, carrying the audience with them. The newspapers asked their readers: have you seen the Earth turn? In Paris the swing comes round about 11° every hour. In Mumbai, much nearer the equator, it would creep round less than 5° an hour, taking about three days to come full circle, and on the equator itself it would not turn at all.
:::

:::story foucault

So what turns is the Earth, once round its ==axis== in about 24 hours. Divide 360° by 24 and you get 15° an hour, which is why the stars seem to wheel at 15° an hour, and why the Sun seems to. Mumbai does not see the Sun rise; Mumbai turns eastward into the sunlight, and twelve hours or so later turns out of it again. How fast you are carried round depends on where you stand. A point on the equator makes the biggest circle. Mumbai sits 19.08° north of the equator, its ==latitude==, and makes a somewhat smaller one: about 1,576 km/h, if you take the Earth as a ball 6,371 km in radius turning once in 24 hours.

:::note Axis (of rotation)
The line through the North and South Poles about which the Earth turns, once in about 24 hours. It leans 23.44° from upright on the Earth's orbit, which matters in the next section.
:::

:::note Latitude
How many degrees you are north or south of the equator. Mumbai is at 19.08° N; the equator is 0°; the poles are 90°.
:::

:::story earth-turns

:::careful
> "You can see the Sun go round the Earth. It rises in the east and sets in the west, every day."

You can see the Sun *seem* to go round, and that is a different thing. The view would be exactly the same if the sky stood still and the ground under you turned east, and the Foucault pendulum shows that it is the ground that turns. Aryabhata saw the trap in 499 CE: what you watch from a moving platform is the world sliding the other way. Keep what you observe (the Sun crosses the sky) apart from the model that explains it (the Earth turns), as Chapter 0 asked you to.
:::

:::school
IGCSE says the Earth "rotates on its axis, which is tilted, once in approximately 24 hours". Hold on to that *approximately*; 15° an hour is close enough for every question in this book.
:::

:::check
Astronomers accepted that the Earth turns long before Foucault. What is wrong with saying "we know the Earth turns because we see the Sun and stars go round us every day"?
???
Seeing the Sun and stars go round is exactly what you would see whether the sky turned or the Earth did; Aryabhata's boat shows why. The observation cannot choose between the two models. Foucault's pendulum can, because a swinging pendulum keeps its plane, so when the line of its swing turns relative to the floor, the floor must be turning.
:::

## 14.2 Why seasons happen

:::scene Mumbai, early January

A January morning in Mumbai is the best the city's weather gets: the air is dry, the nights are almost cool, and January is, on average, the coolest month of the year. It is also the time when the Earth is nearest the Sun. Our orbit is not a perfect circle. In early January we pass about 147.1 million kilometres from the Sun; in early July, about 152.1 million. Light spreads out with distance by the same inverse-square rule that Chapter 5 found for gravity, so the Sun shines on the whole Earth about 7% more strongly in January than in July. That is real, and it is measurable. And it arrives in Mumbai's coolest month.

:::predict
The Earth is about 5 million kilometres closer to the Sun in early January than in early July. What does this do to Mumbai's January?
- It makes January the warmest month
- It makes January a little warmer than it would otherwise be, but something much bigger is making it cool
- It makes no difference at all; sunlight does not weaken with distance
???
The second. The extra 7% of sunlight is real, and January is still the coolest month, so whatever sets the seasons must be far stronger than a 7% change and must be working the other way. A film called *A Private Universe* once put this question to newly graduated Harvard students and some of their professors. Most of those asked got it wrong, and the commonest wrong answer, there and in every survey since, is the one most people reach for first: that summer is when we are nearer the Sun.
:::

The answer is in the angle of the light, and you can see it with a torch.

:::home The torch and the graph paper
Lay a sheet of graph paper on the table in a dim room and hold a phone torch about 20 cm straight above it. Count, roughly, how many squares the bright patch covers. Now keep the torch at the same height and tilt it so the beam comes in at a slant, as the winter Sun does. The patch stretches into a long oval and covers many more squares, and each square gets less light: tilt the beam 60° from straight down and the patch covers about twice the area, so every square gets about half the light. Nothing about the torch changed. The same light was spread over more ground.
:::

That is the first of two things the Earth's tilt does. The Earth's axis is not upright on its orbit. It leans over by 23.44°, its ==axial tilt==, and it keeps pointing the same way in space all year round, towards the same patch of stars, as a spinning top keeps its lean while it travels across the floor. So in June the northern half of the Earth leans towards the Sun, and the Sun stands high in its sky at noon; in December the northern half leans away, and the noon Sun stands low. Low means slanting, and slanting means spread out, as on your graph paper. The southern half does the opposite at the same time, which is why Australia's summer is our winter.

:::note Axial tilt
The lean of the Earth's axis away from upright on its orbit: 23.44°. The axis points the same way in space all year, so each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year and away for the other half.
:::

:::fig c14_tilt_orbit() | One year, seen from a slant. The axis leans the same way at every point in the orbit. In June the north leans towards the Sun; in December it leans away. The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January and farthest in early July: the distance changes by only 3.4%, and the leaning has nothing to do with it. Not to scale: the Sun and Earth are hugely enlarged, and the orbit is very nearly a circle.

Put numbers on Mumbai. At noon on the June ==solstice==, the day the Sun stands highest, the Sun's rays fall straight down at the Tropic of Cancer, 23.44° north. Mumbai is at 19.08° north, a little *south* of that line, so the noon Sun is not quite overhead: it stands 85.6° above the horizon, leaning 4.4° to the north. At the ==equinoxes== in March and September, when the Sun is overhead at the equator, Mumbai's noon Sun is 70.9° high. At the December solstice the Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, 23.44° south, and from Mumbai it climbs only to 47.5°. Light coming in at 47.5° is spread over about 35% more ground than light coming in at 85.6°, so each square metre of Mumbai gets about a third less sunlight at noon in December than in June, before a single cloud is counted.

:::note Solstice and equinox
The two solstices (around 21 June and 21 December) are the days the noon Sun is highest or lowest for the year; the Sun is then overhead at one of the tropics. At the two equinoxes, in March and September, the Sun is overhead at the equator and day and night are about 12 hours long everywhere.
:::

The second thing the tilt does is change the length of the day. When the north leans towards the Sun, Mumbai spends more of each turn of the Earth in the lit half: about 13.2 hours of daylight at the June solstice against about 10.9 at the December one, a difference of a little over two hours. (A few extra minutes come from the air bending the Sun's light over the horizon, which is why the Sun is up a few minutes longer than 12 hours even at the equinoxes.) More hours of a higher Sun: that is summer. Fewer hours of a lower Sun: that is winter. The distance to the Sun plays no part in either.

:::fig c14_sun_height() | Mumbai's noon Sun at the June solstice (85.6°, leaning slightly north), at the equinoxes (70.9°) and at the December solstice (47.5°). The same width of sunbeam lights 1.00 m of ground in June and 1.36 m in December, so each square metre receives about a third less. The days are about 13.2 and 10.9 hours long.

Two more things, because this book must not fool you. First, the longest day is not the hottest. Land, sea and air take time to warm, so where summers are dry the warmest weeks come after the June solstice, as the hottest part of an afternoon comes after noon. In Mumbai the heating is cut short instead: the May afternoon in Chapter 8 was 34.3 °C and the July one 29.4 °C, because by July the monsoon has arrived. Second, Mumbai is not Europe. The city lies inside the tropics, and between about mid-May and late July the noon Sun passes *north* of overhead; on two days a year, around mid-May and again in late July, a vertical pole in Mumbai casts almost no shadow at noon. The swing from winter to summer is real here, and January's cool mornings prove it, but it is gentle. Near the equator, people do not divide the year by temperature at all; they divide it by rain, into a wet season and a dry one. Mumbai's year is divided the same way. On a normal year the monsoon reaches the city around 11 June, and the rain changes life more than the height of the Sun ever does. The tilt still rules: the monsoon comes in the months when the north leans towards the Sun, and Chapter 8 told you how much of its cause is still being argued about.

:::story seasons

:::careful
> "Summer is when the Earth is closer to the Sun."

The Earth is closest in early January, when Mumbai is coolest and Australia is hottest. A 7% change in sunlight, pointing the wrong way, cannot make a season. What makes a season is the tilt: how high the Sun climbs, and for how many hours it is up. A subtler version of the mistake says the tilted half is a little nearer the Sun and so warmer. The lean brings it a few thousand kilometres closer at most, against 150 million, and the whole Earth is five million kilometres nearer in January anyway.
:::

:::school
IGCSE says the Earth "orbits the Sun once in approximately 365 days" and uses that to "explain the periodic nature of the seasons". The orbit explains why the seasons *repeat* every year. The reason there are seasons at all is the tilt of the axis, which the syllabus mentions without connecting it.
:::

???
The Sun is overhead 23.44° south of the equator, and Mumbai is 19.08° north of it, so they are 23.44 + 19.08 = 42.52° apart; the Sun's height is 90 − 42.52 = 47.48°, about 47.5°. A Sun at 47.5° spreads the same light over about 35% more ground than a Sun at 85.6°, and shines for about two hours less each day; the extra 7% of sunlight from being closer is smaller than either effect, and it arrives in the coolest month.

## 14.3 Phases of the Moon

:::scene Marine Drive, just after sunset

The Sun has gone down into the Arabian Sea, and low in the west, not far from where it set, hangs a thin bright crescent with its horns pointing away from the sunset. Over the next two weeks the crescent fattens night by night into a half, then a swollen oval, then a full round Moon that rises in the east just as the Sun sets in the west. Then it thins again. The whole cycle takes about 29.5 days, which is where the word *month* comes from.

Ask around what makes the dark part of the crescent dark and a good many people, about four in ten in survey after survey, will tell you it is the Earth's shadow. It is a reasonable guess. Shadows are round, the Moon is round, and the Earth is right there with it. Hold the guess up against what you just saw.

:::predict
The crescent is hanging low in the west, near where the Sun went down. The Earth's shadow points directly away from the Sun. Where is the Earth's shadow right now, compared with the Moon?
- Falling on the Moon, making the dark part
- Pointing the opposite way, out towards the east, nowhere near the Moon
- Too short to reach the Moon at all
???
It points the opposite way. The Sun is below the western horizon, so the Earth's shadow stretches out to the east, into the night sky on the other side. The Moon is in the west, almost in the Sun's direction. No shadow of ours can be on it. (The third answer is wrong too: the Earth's shadow is about 1.4 million km long and the Moon is only 384,000 km away. It does reach, on the rare nights when the line-up is exact; the next section is about those.) The dark part of the crescent is the Moon's own night side, seen from an angle.
:::

The Moon makes no light of its own. It is a ball of rock, and we see it the way we see everything in Chapter 10, by light bouncing off it into our eyes: in this case sunlight. The Sun lights exactly half of the ball at any moment, the half facing it, and the other half is in night. What changes through the month is where the Moon is in its orbit round the Earth, and so how much of its sunlit half is turned towards us. That is all a ==phase== is: the fraction of the lit half we can see.

:::note Phase (of the Moon)
The shape the Moon shows us, set by how much of its sunlit half faces the Earth. Half the Moon is always lit; the phase is our view of that half from a changing angle.
:::

:::home The orange and the lamp
Darken a room so that one lamp or torch is the only light; a bare bulb across the room is ideal. Stand about two metres from it, hold an orange at arm's length a little above your head, and turn slowly on the spot, watching the orange. With your back to the lamp the orange is full: its whole lit side faces you. Turn a quarter and it is half lit, like a quarter Moon. Turn until the orange is almost between you and the lamp and only a thin crescent of light shows along its edge. The lit half of the orange always faces the lamp; your view of it is what changes. Nothing cast a shadow on the orange at any point, and nothing needs to.
:::

In the picture below the Sun is far off to the right, so the right-hand half of the Moon is lit wherever it is in its orbit. When the Moon is between us and the Sun, we face its night side and see nothing: a ==new Moon==. A few days later it has moved on and a sliver of the lit half comes into view, the ==waxing== crescent you saw over Marine Drive. A week after new, the Moon is a quarter of the way round, and we see exactly half of the lit half: first quarter, which despite its name looks like a half Moon. Then the swollen gibbous shape, then full, when the Moon is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun and the whole lit half faces us. Then the same shapes in reverse, ==waning==, through third quarter and a thin morning crescent back to new.

:::note New Moon, waxing and waning
New Moon: the Moon lies in the Sun's direction and shows us its night side. Waxing: the lit part grows, from new through first quarter to full. Waning: it shrinks, from full through third quarter back to new. The whole cycle takes about 29.5 days.
:::

:::fig c14_phases() | The Moon at eight points in its orbit, with sunlight coming from the right, and below, the shape we see from Earth at each point. The lit half always faces the Sun; what changes is how much of it faces us. Not to scale: the real Moon is about 30 Earth-widths away, far too distant for the Earth's shadow to fall on it except at a full Moon lined up exactly, which is an eclipse.

Because the Moon moves on about 12° each day along its orbit, it rises about 50 minutes later each day: a Moon that rose at sunset on Monday rises after dark on Tuesday. The full Moon rises as the Sun sets and stands highest at midnight. The first-quarter Moon rises around noon, which is why you can sometimes see a half Moon in the afternoon sky.

One more surprise. The Moon turns on its own axis exactly once for every trip round the Earth, so the same face is always towards us. Have a parent stand in the middle of a room while you walk slowly round them, always facing them: by the time you get back you will have turned once round, and they will never have seen your back. That is the Moon. The side we never see is the ==far side==. People call it the dark side, and it is lit for half of every month, in the days around new Moon, when the near side is dark.

:::note Far side (of the Moon)
The half of the Moon that always faces away from Earth, because the Moon turns once on its axis for each orbit. It gets sunlight for half of every month, so "dark side" is the wrong name.
:::

:::story moon-phases

:::recall 10
"We see the Moon because it shines." Chapter 10 found that we see a thing when light from it enters our eyes. Where does the Moon's light come from, and why does that explain the phases?
???
The Moon makes no light; sunlight falls on it and some bounces off towards us, so we see the Moon the way we see a white wall in daylight. Because the light comes from the Sun, exactly half the Moon is lit at any time, the half facing the Sun. The phases are the changing fraction of that lit half we happen to be looking at as the Moon goes round us. A Moon that glowed by itself would look full every night.
:::

:::careful
> "The dark part of the Moon is the Earth's shadow falling on it."

The Earth's shadow always points directly away from the Sun. The crescent Moon hangs near the Sun in the sky, at sunset or sunrise, where our shadow cannot reach. The dark part is the Moon's own night side, which the Sun is not shining on, seen from an angle. The Earth's shadow does reach the Moon on rare occasions: only at full Moon, only when the line-up is exact, and then it is called an eclipse, which is the next section.
:::

:::school
IGCSE asks for "approximately one month for the Moon to orbit the Earth" and the "cycle of phases". Two months hide in that *approximately*: the Moon goes once round relative to the stars in 27.3 days, but the phases take 29.5 days to repeat, because the Earth has moved on round the Sun meanwhile and the Moon must catch up. If a book says "the dark side of the Moon", read "far side".
:::

## 14.4 Eclipses

Here is a coincidence so neat that it looks designed. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon: about 1,391,000 km across against 3,475 km. It is also about 400 times farther away: 149.6 million km against 384,400 km. Divide one by the other and both discs come out about half a degree wide in our sky, so nearly the same size that the Moon can just cover the Sun.

:::scene Kusumapura, 499 CE, again

Aryabhata, who gave us the boat, also wrote down what an eclipse is. The stories said a demon swallowed the Sun or the Moon. He said it was a shadow: the Moon's shadow falling on the Earth, or the Earth's shadow falling on the Moon. Every eclipse since has agreed with him.

A shadow in sunlight has two parts. Behind the Moon there is a cone of complete darkness, the ==umbra==, where the whole Sun is hidden, and around it a wider, lighter region, the ==penumbra==, where only part of the Sun is covered. Hold your hand up to a window and look at its shadow on the floor: the sharp dark centre and the fuzzy grey edge are the same two parts. The Earth's umbra stretches about 1.4 million km out into space behind it, far beyond the Moon's distance.

:::note Umbra and penumbra
The umbra is the cone of full shadow behind a body, where the Sun is completely hidden. The penumbra is the lighter region round it, where the Sun is only partly covered.
:::

A ==solar eclipse== happens when the Moon's shadow falls on the Earth. The Moon must then be between the Earth and the Sun, which is the position of a new Moon, so a solar eclipse can only happen at new Moon. A ==lunar eclipse== happens when the Moon passes into the Earth's shadow; the Earth must then be between the Sun and the Moon, so a lunar eclipse can only happen at full Moon. There is a new Moon every month, and a full one. Yet eclipses are rare.

:::note Solar and lunar eclipse
Solar eclipse: the Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth, at new Moon. Lunar eclipse: the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, at full Moon. Neither is a phase; both are shadows.
:::

:::predict
There is a new Moon every month, when the Moon lies in the Sun's direction. Why isn't there a solar eclipse every month?
- There is, but each one is seen only from a small part of the Earth
- The Moon's orbit is tilted, so its shadow usually passes above or below the Earth
- The Moon is too small to cover the Sun most of the time
???
The second. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the plane of the Earth's orbit round the Sun. At the far point of that tilt the Moon sits about 34,000 km above or below the plane, and the Earth is only 6,371 km in radius, so in most months the Moon's shadow sails past, above or below us. Only twice a year, when the Sun lines up with the two points where the Moon's orbit crosses the plane, can a shadow strike. Those are the eclipse seasons, and every eclipse falls in one of them.
:::

:::fig c14_eclipse_tilt() | Why eclipses are rare. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the plane in which the Earth goes round the Sun (drawn steeper here so that you can see it). In most months (left) the new Moon passes above or below that plane, up to about 34,000 km off it, and its shadow misses an Earth only 6,371 km in radius. Twice a year (right) the Sun lies in line with the crossing points of the orbit, the new Moon passes through the plane, and its shadow reaches the ground. Distances are not to scale.

Because the tilt is fixed in space while the Earth travels round the Sun, the line-up comes round on a schedule, and the schedule can be worked out far ahead. Astronomers predict eclipses centuries in advance; that reliability, more than anything, is what finished the demons. Two more details follow from the geometry. The Moon's distance varies, from about 363,000 km to about 405,000 km, so sometimes it looks a little too small to cover the Sun entirely and a ring of Sun is left showing round it: an annular eclipse. And the Earth's umbra at the Moon's distance is much wider than the Moon, so a lunar eclipse lasts far longer than a solar one, and can be watched, safely, by half the world at once.

Watch one and you will see the eclipsed Moon turn a deep copper red rather than vanishing. The Earth's air bends a little sunlight into the shadow, and the air lets the reds through while scattering the blues away: it is exactly the physics of the Marine Drive sunset in Chapter 11, seen from the Moon's side.

Which brings us to the Sun. Never look at it, eclipsed or not; even a brief look can damage your eyes, and sunglasses, smoked glass and camera filters do not make it safe. The easy way to watch a solar eclipse is to make a hole about a millimetre across in a card and let the Sun shine through it onto a second card held a metre or so behind: a small bright image of the Sun appears, and during an eclipse you will see the Moon's bite in it. You are using Ibn al-Haytham's dark room from Chapter 10. Totality, the few minutes when the Moon covers the whole Sun, is the one time it is safe to look up, and it is seen only from the narrow track the tip of the umbra draws across the ground. For Mumbai, in most years, the pinhole is the whole method.

:::careful
> "The Sun and the Moon are the same size. You can see they are: they match in the sky."

They match in *apparent* size, which is a different thing from actual size. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, and the two 400s cancel, so both look about half a degree across. A coin held at arm's length can cover a building in just the same way. Apparent size is a size divided by a distance, and it tells you nothing about either on its own.
:::

:::school
Eclipses are not in the IGCSE syllabus at all; Cambridge Lower Secondary covers them in Stage 7, alongside tides. One phrase to carry into any exam: the Sun and Moon have similar *apparent* sizes. Their actual sizes differ by a factor of about 400.
:::

During a total lunar eclipse the Moon does not go black. It glows a dim copper red. Where is that light coming from?
???
From the Sun, by way of the Earth's air. Sunlight grazing past the edge of the Earth is bent slightly inward by the atmosphere, into the shadow, and on the way through the air the blue light is scattered away while the red gets through, as at sunset (Chapter 11). What reaches the eclipsed Moon is the red light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at that moment, and we see it bounced back.
:::

## 14.5 Tides at Haji Ali

:::scene Haji Ali, Mumbai

A narrow causeway runs out from the shore at Worli to the white dargah of Haji Ali on its rock in the sea. Twice a day the sea rises over the causeway and cuts the dargah off; twice a day it falls and the path reappears, wet and crowded. The timing shifts, later each day by close to an hour, and the people who sell flowers at the shore end know the schedule better than any clock. The sea is answering the Moon. Chapter 5 told you that the Earth's pull reaches out across 384,000 km and holds the Moon in its orbit. The Moon's pull reaches back the same distance, and it moves the whole ocean.

Most people, asked how, say that the Moon pulls the water up towards it, and that is a good first half of the story. It raises an immediate problem.

:::predict
The Moon pulls the sea towards it, and the sea heaps up a little on the side of the Earth facing the Moon. Yet there are two high tides a day at Haji Ali, about twelve and a half hours apart. Where does the second one come from?
- There is a second heap of water on the opposite side of the Earth, facing away from the Moon
- The Sun makes the second one
- The first heap sloshes back across the ocean and arrives again half a day later
???
The first. There are two heaps, one under the Moon and one on the far side, and the Earth turns under both of them each day. The Sun raises smaller tides of its own, which add to or subtract from the Moon's. Why there should be a heap on the side *away* from the Moon is the real puzzle of this section, and the answer is in how gravity weakens with distance.
:::

The Moon pulls on every part of the Earth, and, as Chapter 5 found, its pull is weaker the farther away you are. The sea on the near side of the Earth is about 6,400 km closer to the Moon than the Earth's centre, and the sea on the far side about 6,400 km farther. So the Moon pulls the near sea hardest, the Earth's centre a little less, and the far sea least of all: about 7% less than the near sea. Now think about the differences. Compared with the Earth as a whole, the near sea is pulled a little *towards* the Moon. The far sea is pulled a little less than the Earth as a whole, so, compared with the Earth, it is left behind: a little *away* from the Moon. Two small tugs, in opposite directions, on opposite sides.

Those tugs are tiny, far too small to lift water off the Earth; the Moon does not pull the sea up. What they do, over hours, is make the water *flow*. Where the sideways part of the tug is biggest, the sea creeps along the surface towards the point under the Moon and towards the point opposite, and piles up there into two low, broad heaps, the ==tidal bulges==. On an Earth covered entirely in water the heaps would stand nearly a metre high. The solid rock flexes too, by about 20 cm at most. As the Earth turns, Mumbai is carried under one heap, then into the trough, then under the other: two highs and two lows in each turn.

:::note Tidal bulges
The two broad heaps of ocean water, one towards the Moon and one away from it, raised by the *difference* in the Moon's pull across the Earth. Water flows along the surface into them; it is not lifted up.
:::

:::fig c14_tides() | The Moon pulls the near side of the Earth hardest and the far side least (the difference is about 7%, drawn here about four times larger). Relative to the Earth's centre, the near sea is tugged towards the Moon and the far sea away from it. Over hours those tugs make water flow sideways into two heaps, hugely exaggerated here. As the Earth turns, each place passes under both heaps every day. The Moon's distance is not to scale.

The turn is not quite a day long. By the time the Earth has turned once, the Moon has moved on along its orbit, about 12°, and the Earth needs another 50 minutes to catch up with it. So the heaps come round every 24 hours 50 minutes, high tides about 12 hours 25 minutes apart, and each day's high tide is about 50 minutes later than the day before: the same daily delay as the Moon's rising, which is how the flower-sellers' schedule drifts. That matching delay is how people knew the Moon drives the tides long before anyone could say how; the Sun's clock repeats every 24 hours exactly, and the tides do not keep it.

The Sun does raise tides of its own, less than half as big as the Moon's; by the numbers, about 46%. It is enormously more massive than the Moon, but it is 390 times farther away, and tides depend on the *difference* in pull across the Earth, which falls off with distance far faster than the pull itself. Twice a month, at new Moon and at full Moon, the Sun and Moon lie in a line and their heaps add: the biggest highs and lowest lows, called ==spring tides==, from the way the water springs up, nothing to do with the season. At the quarter Moons the Sun's heaps sit in the Moon's troughs and partly cancel them: the gentle ==neap tides==. Spring to spring is about 14.8 days.

:::note Spring and neap tides
Spring tides: the largest tides, at new and full Moon, when the Sun's and Moon's bulges line up. Neap tides: the smallest, at the quarter Moons, when they partly cancel. "Spring" here means springing up, and happens all year.
:::

Real tides are messier than two tidy heaps. Continents block the flow, the sea floor drags on it, bays funnel it, the wind pushes it, and the Earth's spin swings it, so every harbour has its own pattern and its own tide table, worked out from years of measurement. Mumbai's tables show two highs a day, usually of unequal height, with the biggest rise and fall around new and full Moon. On one recent day the forecast for Mumbai was a high water of 3.93 m and a low of 1.43 m: two and a half metres of sea arriving and leaving again within a few hours, which is what covers the causeway. Other days differ; published tide tables, such as those from INCOIS, carry the real numbers. [The Sky](#explore-sky) explorer shows the two bulges as the Moon goes round, drawn as a schematic, because the real sea never has time to settle into the simple shape.

:::story tides

:::recall 5
"The Moon is in space, and there's no gravity in space." Chapter 5 had an answer about the Moon falling round the Earth. What do the tides add to it?
???
Gravity does not stop at the top of the air. The Earth's pull reaches 384,000 km to the Moon and keeps it falling round us (Chapter 5), and the Moon's pull reaches 384,000 km back and moves the whole ocean twice a day. If there were no gravity in space there would be no orbit and no tides, and the causeway at Haji Ali would stay dry.
:::

:::careful
> "The Moon lifts the water up towards it."

Lifting is not what happens, and it could not make a second heap on the far side. The Moon's pull is a little stronger on the near side of the Earth than on the far side. Relative to the Earth as a whole, that tugs the near sea slightly towards the Moon and leaves the far sea slightly behind. Over hours, these tiny differences make water flow sideways along the surface into two heaps. The tide is water that has flowed along. Nothing picked it up.
:::

:::check
Are spring tides something that happens in spring?
???
No. They happen twice every month, at new Moon and at full Moon, in every season, when the Sun's and the Moon's tidal bulges line up and add. The name means the water "springs up". Neap tides, the smallest, come at the two quarter Moons in between.
:::

One last thing the tides do, slowly. The heaps drag on the turning Earth and the Earth drags on the heaps, and the friction does two things: it lengthens the day by about two thousandths of a second each century, and it pushes the Moon gradually outward, by about 3.8 cm a year. We know the second figure because astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and laser pulses bounced off them from Earth have been timed ever since.

## The causeway timetable

:::case The causeway timetable
Your cousin visited Haji Ali with you last Saturday. You reached the shore at 10:30 in the morning and the causeway was dry, with the sea at its lowest; that night the Moon was full. She wants to go back next Saturday at the same time, "because that's when it was open."

You have three clues from this chapter:

- High and low tide come about 50 minutes later each day, following the Moon.
- The two lows in a day are about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.
- The biggest rise and fall come at new and full Moon; the smallest at the quarters.

(These Saturday times are made up for the puzzle. The real ones are in the tide tables.)

Will the causeway be dry next Saturday at 10:30? If not, when should she go, and what else will be different about the tide that day?
???
No. Seven days of 50-minute delays add up to about 5 hours 50 minutes, so last Saturday's 10:30 low falls at about 4:20 in the afternoon next Saturday. The other low of the day, 12 hours 25 minutes earlier, is at about 4 in the morning. At 10:30 she would arrive roughly halfway between a low and a high, with the sea rising over the causeway, the most dangerous time to be anywhere near it.

She should go in the late afternoon, around 4:20, after checking the published table, because real tides at Mumbai drift unevenly. One more change: a week after full Moon is the third quarter, so next Saturday's tides are neaps. The sea will rise and fall less than at the full-Moon spring tide she saw. About a fortnight after the first visit, around new Moon, the times come back near where they started and the big tides return.
:::
