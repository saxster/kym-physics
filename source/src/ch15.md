Chapter 14 leaned on the Sun at every turn. It lights the Moon, it drives the seasons, and twice a month its pull joins the Moon's to send the biggest tides over the causeway at Haji Ali. It has been doing all this, as far as the rocks can tell, for about four and a half billion years. And in Chapter 7 you learned to keep accounts of energy: nothing comes from nowhere. A bonfire the size of the Sun would be ash in a few thousand years. So what is the Sun doing?

The answer needs the smallest things in the book and the largest, in that order. It starts with light, which turns out to arrive in packets. It goes down into the centre of the atom, where Chapter 1 of *The Invisible World* left off, and finds there the thing that keeps the Sun shining. Then it turns outward: what stars are made of, how far away they are, how they end, and what the whole universe is doing. Two of the people on this road were told, at first, that they must be wrong.

## 15.1 Light comes in packets

:::scene Bern, 1905

In 1905 a clerk in the Swiss patent office in Bern, Albert Einstein, published four papers in the journal *Annalen der Physik*. One of them changed how we measure space and time; another gave the world E = mc², which you will meet in Section 15.3. But the Nobel Prize he was eventually given was for the first of the four, which was about something that looks small: what happens when light falls on a metal.

Here is the effect. Shine light on a clean metal surface and, for some metals and some colours of light, electrons come flying out of it. That is the ==photoelectric effect==, and after Chapter 11 you have a ready picture of it. Light is a wave; a wave carries energy; the energy shakes electrons loose. A brighter light is a bigger wave, so it should shake them out harder, and a very dim light should need a little time to build up enough energy before any electron can leave.

:::note Photoelectric effect
Electrons being knocked out of a material by light falling on it. Each electron is freed by one packet of light; the packet must carry at least the energy that binds the electron.
:::

:::predict
Two lamps shine in turn on the same clean metal: a very bright red lamp and a dim violet one. Which knocks electrons out?
- The bright red lamp, because it delivers far more energy
- Both, the red one more strongly
- Only the violet one, however dim it is
???
Only the violet one, for the calcium example below, and it works the instant it is switched on, however dim it is. Make the red lamp as bright as you like and nothing comes out. Make the violet lamp brighter and *more* electrons come out, each with the same top speed as before. Measured carefully, the effect breaks the wave picture in five places at once: a threshold colour below which nothing happens, whatever the brightness; no delay; a number of electrons that follows the brightness; a top energy that does not; and a top energy that rises in a straight line with the light's frequency.
:::

Einstein's explanation was that light delivers its energy in packets. Each packet, now called a ==photon==, carries an energy fixed by the light's frequency: *E* = *hf*, where *h* is a very small number called Planck's constant, 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds. Violet light has a higher frequency than red, so each violet photon carries more energy. When light meets the metal, one photon gives its whole energy to one electron. If that is more than the energy binding the electron to the metal, the electron leaves with the difference; if it is less, the electron stays, and no number of such photons will help, because they arrive one at a time. Brightness is simply how many photons arrive each second. Brighter red means more too-small packets. Dim violet means a few packets that are each big enough.

:::note Photon
A packet of light. Its energy is *E* = *hf*: Planck's constant times the frequency. Brighter light means more photons per second, never bigger ones.
:::

:::five Photon
? What is it?
A single quantum packet of light (or any electromagnetic radiation), carrying an energy proportional to the wave's frequency: *E* = *hf*.
? Why is it called this?
Einstein called the packets *light quanta* in 1905; the physical chemist Gilbert Lewis coined *photon* in 1926 from the Greek *phos* (*phot-*), light, to match *electron* and *proton*.
? What problem did it solve?
In the photoelectric effect, dim violet light knocks electrons out of calcium instantly while blazing red light never knocks out a single one; waves that spread their energy smoothly could not explain why colour alone sets a sharp threshold.
? Why does it work?
Whenever light is emitted or absorbed by matter, it hands over its energy one whole packet at a time to one electron; if a single packet (*hf*) is smaller than the energy binding the electron, the electron cannot leave, no matter how many packets arrive per second.
? How does it help?
It explains the photoelectric effect, why ultraviolet light causes sunburn while infrared from a remote control cannot, how solar cells and camera sensors count light, and why atoms emit and absorb sharp spectral lines.
?? How do we know?
Shine monochromatic light on a clean metal in a vacuum and measure the stopping voltage of the freed electrons: below a threshold frequency no electrons appear at any brightness, and above it their maximum kinetic energy rises in a straight line with frequency, with slope *h*.
?? What it isn't
A photon is not a tiny hard marble with mass: it has zero rest mass, always travels at *c* in empty space, and still produces wave interference patterns when sent through two slits.
?? Where does it stop working?
In everyday lamps and radio antennas where trillions of photons arrive together, the individual packets blur into the smooth classical electromagnetic wave of Chapter 13.
:::

The energies involved are tiny, so physicists use a small unit, the ==electronvolt==. Visible photons carry between about 1.63 eV, for deep red, and 3.26 eV, for violet. A clean calcium surface holds its electrons with 2.71 eV. A violet photon of 3.26 eV, as drawn below, frees one and leaves it with up to 3.26 − 2.71 = 0.55 eV of motion. A photon from your television remote, which flashes infrared light at a wavelength of about 940 nm, carries 1.32 eV: not enough, so the remote could shine on calcium all day and nothing would leave. Everyday light is a torrent of these packets: if a tenth of a 100-watt bulb's output were yellow light, it would throw out about 3 × 10¹⁹ photons a second, so many that they blur into the smooth flow of energy you kept accounts of in Chapter 7.

:::note Electronvolt (eV)
A unit of energy for atoms and light: the energy an electron gains when pushed through one volt, about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Visible photons carry 1.63 to 3.26 eV.
:::

:::fig c15_photoelectric() | Left: a bright red lamp frees no electrons from calcium, because each of its many photons carries less than the 2.71 eV needed; a dim violet lamp frees a few at once, because each of its photons carries 3.26 eV. Right: the energy of one photon for the remote's infrared, for red and for violet light, against the 2.71 eV that calcium demands.

:::home See the invisible
Point a television remote at your phone's camera in a dim room, look at the screen, and press a button. The remote's tip flashes a pale light on the screen that your eyes cannot see in the room: infrared, at about 940 nm, a colour just beyond red. Only some cameras show it; if the back camera shows nothing, try the front one. This is light from a small lamp, a torch at a colour you cannot see. It is not the heat of a warm object, and the phone is not "seeing heat". Each of these photons carries about 1.32 eV, less than any visible photon, which is one reason the remote cannot hurt you.
:::

So is light a wave or a stream of packets? Young's card in Chapter 11 gave fringes that only waves make. The photoelectric effect gives thresholds that only packets make. Both experiments are right, and the honest statement is both at once: light travels like a wave and delivers its energy in packets. Nobody has a picture of a single thing that does both. The equations do both, and they have never been caught out.

:::scene Dacca, 4 June 1924

Nineteen years later a physics lecturer in Dacca, Satyendra Nath Bose, worked out a new way of counting photons that gave Planck's formula for the light from a hot body. The *Philosophical Magazine* in London had not replied to his paper. So he wrote to Einstein, beginning "Respected Sir", and asked him, if he thought the work worth it, to arrange its publication, since he did not know enough German to do so himself. Einstein translated the paper with his own hand, sent Bose a postcard on 2 July, and had it printed in August with a note of his own. Particles that can be counted Bose's way are now called bosons, and photons are the first of them.

:::story photons

:::recall 11
"Light is a wave, full stop." Chapter 11 showed you Young's fringes and Arago's spot. Does the photoelectric effect mean Chapter 11 was wrong?
???
No. The fringes are real and only a wave makes them. The threshold in the photoelectric effect is real too, and only packets make it: a dim violet lamp frees electrons at once while a bright red one never does. Light travels like a wave and delivers its energy in packets of *hf*. Chapter 11 told you half of what light does; this section tells you the other half.
:::

:::careful
> "A brighter light must give each electron more energy."

Brighter light means more photons each second, and each photon's energy is fixed by the colour alone. So a brighter lamp frees more electrons, each with the same energy as before. If the colour is below the metal's threshold, a brighter lamp frees more of nothing. Remember the remote: it cannot free an electron from calcium however hard you press the button.
:::

## 15.2 The nucleus and radiation myths

:::scene Kenilworth, Peddar Road, Bombay, 19 December 1945

A few minutes' walk from Haji Ali, in a house called Kenilworth on Peddar Road, a new institute opened its doors on 19 December 1945. Its founder, Homi Bhabha, had written to his father from Cambridge in 1928 that business and engineering were not for him and that he was burning to do physics; the bargain they struck was an engineering degree first. In March 1944 he wrote to the Sir Dorab Tata Trust proposing an institute for fundamental research, and the Tata Institute, TIFR, began in Bangalore that June before moving to Bombay. Among those who joined it was Bibha Chowdhuri, who had spent the war years leaving photographic plates on mountains at Darjeeling and Sandakphu so that cosmic rays, particles raining in from space, would streak across them, and had found in them the curved tracks of a new kind of particle.

Particles from space pass through you every day. The word for anything that streams out of a source, from radio waves and the remote's infrared to X-rays and the fragments of atoms, is ==radiation==, and it is a word that frightens people. Two fears are common. One is that all radiation is dangerous. The other, held by somewhere between 63% and 75% of students asked, is that anything that has been *near* radiation becomes radioactive itself. The remote has answered the first fear. The second needs the inside of the atom.

:::note Radiation
Anything that streams outward from a source: light of every colour, radio waves, X-rays, and the particles and gamma rays that some nuclei throw out. Most radiation is harmless; the harmful kinds carry enough energy per packet to knock electrons off atoms.
:::

*The Invisible World* told you how Rutherford's students fired alpha particles at gold foil and found that about one in eight thousand bounced back. An atom is almost entirely empty space around a ==nucleus== about 10⁻¹⁵ m across, a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom itself. If the nucleus were a marble one centimetre across, the atom would be a kilometre wide. Inside it, protons and neutrons are held together by the strong nuclear force, which is very strong and reaches only a very short way. Chemistry, burning and cooking never touch it.

:::note Nucleus
The tiny, heavy centre of an atom, made of protons and neutrons held by the strong nuclear force. About 10⁻¹⁵ m across, 100,000 times smaller than the atom.
:::

:::evidence Geiger and Marsden's gold-foil backscatter | Manchester, 1909
How can anyone be sure the nucleus is a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom? Before 1911 the leading model of the atom, proposed by J. J. Thomson (who had discovered the electron in 1897), pictured the atom as a soft sphere of positive charge with electrons dotted through it like plums in a pudding. In 1909 Ernest Rutherford suggested to Hans Geiger and an undergraduate student, Ernest Marsden, that they check whether any alpha particles fired at a thin sheet of gold foil — hammered to only about a thousand atoms thick — ever bounced *backwards*. In Thomson's soft-pudding model, an alpha particle should plough straight through every atom with at most a tiny fraction of a degree of deflection. Sitting in the dark counting tiny flashes on a zinc-sulfide screen through a microscope, Geiger and Marsden found that while almost all alphas went straight through, about 1 in 8,000 ricocheted back towards the source at angles greater than 90°. Rutherford later said it was "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you". In 1911 he did the geometry: only if all the positive charge and nearly all the atom's mass were concentrated into a tiny, hard target under 10⁻¹⁴ m across could the electric repulsion (*F* = *kq*₁*q*₂ ÷ *r*²) grow strong enough at close approach to hurl a fast alpha particle backwards. The rest of the atom is open space.
:::

Every atom of a given element has the same number of protons in its nucleus — its atomic number *Z* (6 for carbon, 92 for uranium) — because the proton count sets how many electrons the neutral atom holds and therefore all its chemistry. But nuclei of the same element can hold different numbers of neutrons, giving them different mass numbers *A* (protons plus neutrons). Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons are ==isotopes==. Almost 99% of the carbon in your body is carbon-12, written [[nuc:12:6:C:]], with 6 protons and 6 neutrons; a tiny fraction is carbon-14, [[nuc:14:6:C:]], with 6 protons and 8 neutrons, made high in the atmosphere by cosmic rays. Chemically the two behave identically, because their electron clouds are the same. Inside the nucleus, the extra two neutrons make [[nuc:14:6:C:]] unstable: with a ==half-life== (the time for half the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay) of about 5,730 years, one of its neutrons turns into a proton and throws out a fast electron (a beta particle).

:::note Isotope
Atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons (*Z*) in their nuclei but different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers (*A*): for example, stable carbon-12 ([[nuc:12:6:C:]], 6 neutrons) and radioactive carbon-14 ([[nuc:14:6:C:]], 8 neutrons).
:::

In 1896 Henri Becquerel found that a lump of uranium ore, left on wrapped photographic plates in a drawer, darkened them through the paper. Some nuclei are unstable: of their own accord, now and then, they throw out a particle or a flash of light and change into something else. Such nuclei are ==radioactive==. Each event carries around a million electronvolts, against a few electronvolts for anything an atom does with its electrons, which is why this radiation can do what light cannot: knock electrons clean off the atoms it passes. That is what "ionising" means, and ionising radiation is the kind that can harm living tissue. Pass the three emissions through an electric field between two charged plates or across a magnetic field (Chapter 13), and they sort themselves out by their charge and mass. ==Alpha== (α) particles are helium-4 nuclei ([[nuc:4:2:He:2+]]), heavy and carrying +2 charge: they are the most strongly ionising, are stopped by a single sheet of paper or a few centimetres of air, and bend gently towards the negative plate. ==Beta== (β⁻) particles are fast electrons ([[nuc:0:-1:e:]]), nearly 7,300 times lighter and carrying −1 charge: they are moderately ionising, pass through paper but are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium, and bend sharply the opposite way, towards the positive plate. ==Gamma== (γ) rays are high-energy electromagnetic photons with no mass and zero charge: they are the most weakly ionising per centimetre, need thick lead or concrete to reduce their intensity, and travel dead straight through both electric and magnetic fields without bending at all.

:::note Radioactive
A nucleus is radioactive if it changes by itself, throwing out an alpha particle, a beta particle or a gamma ray as it does so. Most nuclei never do.
:::

:::predict
After a chest X-ray, is the patient radioactive?
- Yes, for a few hours
- Yes, very slightly, for ever
- No
???
No. An X-ray is a beam of high-energy photons; they pass through the body, some are absorbed, and when the machine switches off there is nothing left behind. Being *exposed* to radiation and being *contaminated* with radioactive material are different things, and the difference is the whole answer to the second fear.
:::

Think of sunlight and mud. Stand in sunlight and you are exposed to radiation; step into the shade and it stops, and nothing of it stays on you. Walk through mud and some of it comes with you until it is washed off. Radioactive material is the mud: dust on your skin or clothes, or breathed in or swallowed, which keeps emitting wherever it is. That is ==contamination==. X-rays, gamma rays and light are the sunlight: they pass, and are gone. Spices and other foods are routinely irradiated with gamma rays to kill insects and germs, without making them radioactive: approved irradiation uses controlled kinds and energies of radiation. One honest exception: a beam of neutrons *can* make the atoms it hits radioactive, which is exactly why neutrons are never used on food. Whether irradiation can make something radioactive depends on its kind and energy. That is a different question from whether exposure can harm living tissue.

:::note Contamination
Radioactive material where it should not be: on skin, hair or clothing, or inside the body. It keeps emitting until it is removed. Being exposed to a beam is not contamination.
:::

:::fig c15_exposure() | Exposure and contamination are different things. Left: an X-ray beam passes through a hand onto a film; switch it off and the beam stops, leaving no radioactive material in the hand. Right: radioactive dust on the skin keeps emitting until it is washed away. Both exposure to the beam and contact with the dust need controls, but only the dust keeps emitting after the machine is off.

You are a little radioactive yourself, and always have been. Every detector ticks steadily even with no laboratory source nearby, recording ==background radiation== from the environment around us: radon gas seeping naturally out of granite and soil (usually the largest single share), rocks and building materials under our feet, traces of potassium-40 and carbon-14 in our food and bones, cosmic rays raining in from space, and medical X-rays. Cosmic rays give the average person about 0.40 millisieverts a year of dose; a twelve-hour flight adds 0.02 to 0.03; the rocks and food add more, and in some places the ground gives ten times the average. A dose below 100 millisieverts counts as low. The question with any radiation is never "is it there?" but how much above background, of what kind, and for how long.

:::story radiation

:::careful
> "Anything that has been near radiation becomes radioactive."

The patient after a diagnostic X-ray and the spices after approved food irradiation do not become radioactive. That is different from radioactive *material* getting on or into something and continuing to emit. Some other radiation, including neutron beams, can change nuclei. Keep the kind and energy of the radiation in view, and keep "exposed" and "contaminated" apart.
:::

:::school
IGCSE topic 5.1 is the nuclear model of the atom (Rutherford's gold-foil alpha-scattering evidence, proton number *Z*, nucleon number *A*, and isotopes) and 5.2 is radioactivity: background radiation sources, comparing α, β and γ in ionising effect, penetrating power and deflection in electric and magnetic fields, nuclear decay equations, half-life (subtracting background count rate first!) and safety. When the syllabus says "ionising", it means knocking electrons off atoms, and "contamination" there means what it means here: radioactive material where it should not be.
:::

No. The kinds and energies used in approved food irradiation can kill insects without making the food radioactive. No radioactive material is put into the chilli. ## 15.3 E = mc² and the Sun's fire

:::scene Kungälv, Sweden, Christmas 1938

Lise Meitner had fled Germany that summer. She spent Christmas in the small Swedish town of Kungälv with her nephew Otto Frisch, and brought with her a letter from her old colleague Otto Hahn in Berlin. He had been firing neutrons at uranium and had found, among the products, barium: an atom only about half as heavy as uranium. It made no sense. Nuclei chipped; they did not halve. The story goes that the two of them walked out into the snow to think about it, and on that walk they saw what Hahn had done. The uranium nucleus had split in two. They called it ==fission==, and they worked out that the two pieces together have slightly less mass than the nucleus they came from, and that the missing mass had become about 200 million electronvolts of energy, more than a hundred thousand times what any chemical reaction releases per atom. Their paper appeared in *Nature* in February 1939.

:::note Fission
A heavy nucleus splitting into two lighter ones. The pieces have a little less mass than the original, and the difference comes out as energy.
:::

The mass had become energy by the rule from the third of Einstein's 1905 papers, *E* = *mc*². Mass and energy are two names for one thing, and the exchange rate is the speed of light squared: 9 × 10¹⁶ joules for every kilogram, so that one gram of mass, fully converted, is 9 × 10¹³ J, about 25 million kilowatt-hours on the bill from Chapter 7. Nothing ordinary converts mass to energy at anything like that rate. A nucleus can, because the strong force that binds it is so strong that the binding itself has a measurable mass.

:::eq
*E* = *mc*²
:::

Now the Sun. Astronomers of the nineteenth century had two candidates for its energy, and both fail.

:::predict
If the Sun were a giant ball of coal, burning, how long could it keep shining as brightly as it does now?
- About as long as it already has: billions of years
- A few million years
- A few thousand years
???
A few thousand years. Burning is chemistry, a few electronvolts per atom, and no chemical fuel the size of the Sun could last longer than that; besides, nothing like coal could survive at the Sun's temperature. The other old idea, that the Sun shines because it is slowly shrinking under its own gravity, reaches only about a hundred million years, and the rocks say that there was liquid water on the Earth nearly four billion years ago. Something far richer than burning is going on, and it is the opposite of fission.
:::

In the centre of the Sun the temperature is about 15 million kelvin, and hydrogen nuclei, bare protons, rush about. Protons repel each other, as Chapter 12’s charges do. Most pairs do not have enough motion energy to overcome that electrical barrier even here. But quantum physics gives them a small chance of passing through it, called *tunnelling*. The hot, dense core brings enormous numbers of pairs close together, so some reach the short distance where nuclear forces can bind them. In several steps, four protons and two electrons become a helium nucleus and two neutrinos. That is ==fusion==. For the complete mass account, compare four hydrogen atoms with one helium atom: the helium atom has about 0.71% less mass. The difference leaves as energy, by *E* = *mc*². The neutrinos carry some straight out of the Sun; most of the energy eventually escapes as sunlight, after a journey of a hundred thousand years or more being passed between particles on the way out.

:::note Fusion
Light nuclei joining to make a heavier one. In the Sun, four hydrogen nuclei become one helium nucleus and 0.71% of their mass becomes energy. In the Sun it happens in the hot, dense core and depends on quantum tunnelling through electrical repulsion.
:::

:::five Nuclear fusion
? What is it?
The joining of two light atomic nuclei into a heavier nucleus whose mass is slightly less than the sum of the pieces, releasing the mass difference as energy according to *E* = *mc*².
? Why is it called this?
*Nuclear* refers to the atom's nucleus; *fusion* comes from Latin *fusio*, melting together, borrowed in the 1930s as the opposite of nuclear *fission* (splitting).
? What problem did it solve?
Neither chemical burning (which would last a few thousand years) nor gravitational shrinking (about a hundred million years) could explain how the Sun has shone for 4.5 billion years; fusing hydrogen into helium releases millions of times more energy per kilogram than any chemical reaction.
? Why does it work?
Quantum tunnelling lets some protons get past their electrical repulsion, and the strong nuclear force binds the final helium nucleus. Across the full reaction chain, four protons and two electrons become a helium-4 nucleus and two neutrinos. Comparing the masses of four hydrogen atoms with one helium atom accounts for the electrons: about 0.71% of the starting mass becomes released energy, yielding about 6.4 × 10¹⁴ J per kilogram of hydrogen.
? How does it help?
It explains what powers the Sun and every main-sequence star, how stars forge heavier elements out of hydrogen and helium, and why the Sun has used only about 0.03% of its mass in 4.5 billion years.
?? How do we know?
We measure the Sun's luminosity (3.8 × 10²⁶ W), compare hydrogen and helium atomic masses to verify the 0.71% mass difference, and detect the neutrinos streaming straight out of the Sun's core from the fusion reactions.
?? What it isn't
It is not chemical burning (which only rearranges outer electrons and leaves nuclei untouched), and it is not nuclear fission (which splits heavy nuclei like uranium).
?? Where does it stop working?
A star’s fusion rate depends strongly on temperature and density, as well as on the nuclei involved. It is not simply a switch that turns on when protons move fast enough to touch: tunnelling matters. Building nuclei heavier than iron generally absorbs energy instead of releasing it.
:::

:::fig c15_fusion() | In the Sun's core, four protons and two electrons become, in several steps, a helium nucleus and two neutrinos. The bars use atomic masses to keep the complete electron account: the helium atom retains about 99.29% of the starting mass of four hydrogen atoms, with about 0.71% released as energy. The bars share one scale; the small difference is enlarged in the inset. The solar matter is a plasma, so the atom masses are bookkeeping, not a picture of atoms inside the Sun.

Put numbers on it and the Sun's fire makes sense at last. Every second the Sun turns about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium, and about 4 million tonnes of that mass leaves as light. That sounds like a catastrophe until you weigh the Sun: it has about 2 × 10³⁰ kg, and in four and a half billion years of this it has used up about 0.03% of itself. Most of its protons have never fused at all.

:::work
Energy from one kilogram of hydrogen fused: 0.0071 × (3 × 10⁸ m/s)² ≈ 6.4 × 10¹⁴ J
The Sun's output: 3.8 × 10²⁶ J every second
Mass turned into energy each second: 3.8 × 10²⁶ ÷ (3 × 10⁸)² ≈ 4.3 × 10⁹ kg, about 4 million tonnes
Hydrogen fused each second: 4.3 × 10⁹ ÷ 0.0071 ≈ 6 × 10¹¹ kg, about 600 million tonnes
:::

:::recall 7
"Energy is never created. So where does the Sun's energy come from?" Chapter 7 kept a ledger with stores and transfers. What has to go in the ledger to make the Sun's accounts balance?
???
Mass. Four million tonnes of the Sun's mass disappear each second and the same amount of energy appears, at the rate *E* = *mc*². Once mass is counted as a store of energy, the Sun's books balance exactly: nothing is created, and the total of mass-energy stays the same. Chapter 7's rule survives; it just has a larger ledger than you knew.
:::

Since January 2024 a spacecraft has been watching all this without a blink. ISRO's Aditya-L1 flies in a loop around a point in space called L1, about 1.5 million km from the Earth towards the Sun, one per cent of the way there, where the pulls of the Sun and the Earth balance with the motion round the Sun so that a craft keeps pace with the Earth all year, with the Sun always in view and the Earth never in the way. It does not sit still at the point; it circles it in a halo orbit once every 178 days, and it arrived there on 6 January 2024.

:::careful
> "The Sun is burning, like a huge fire."

Burning is a chemical change: atoms swap partners and their electrons rearrange, and the nuclei are never touched. A coal Sun would be ash in a few thousand years. The Sun shines by fusion, which changes the nuclei themselves, hydrogen into helium, and releases about a million times more energy per atom than any flame. Say "shining", or "fusing", and keep "burning" for the kitchen.

## 15.4 What stars are made of

:::scene Calcutta, 1920

Nobody can visit a star, and yet by 1920 astronomers had been reading the recipe of the Sun for sixty years. The trick is the spectrum from Chapter 11. Spread a star's light into a rainbow and it is crossed by dark lines, hundreds of them in sunlight; Joseph von Fraunhofer counted about 600 in 1815. Each kind of atom swallows its own set of colours, so each element leaves its own pattern of lines, like a barcode. In 1860 Gustav Kirchhoff matched one of the Sun's patterns to sodium, the first element ever identified in a star. Helium was found in the Sun's light before anyone had found it on Earth.

But the stars' barcodes differ. Some show strong hydrogen lines; some, the hottest and bluest, show almost none. The first guess was the obvious one: different lines, different ingredients. In Calcutta a young physicist, Meghnad Saha, saw another possibility. The lines an atom can make depend on where its electrons are sitting, and that depends on how hot the gas is. Heat a gas enough and its atoms lose electrons altogether; an atom with no electron cannot make its usual lines at all. In 1920 Saha wrote the equation that says, for any temperature and pressure, what fraction of the atoms have lost their electrons. Alfred Fowler hosted him at Imperial College in London that year and helped him revise the papers, and the equation has been the key to every star's spectrum since.

:::predict
A hot blue star shows almost no hydrogen lines in its spectrum. Does that mean it contains almost no hydrogen?
- Yes: no lines, no hydrogen
- No: it is so hot that its hydrogen atoms have lost their electrons, and cannot make the lines
- The lines are there but drowned out by the blue light
???
No. In the hottest stars nearly every hydrogen atom has been stripped of its electron, so there is nothing left to swallow the hydrogen colours, and the lines vanish. In the coolest stars the lines are weak for the opposite reason: the electrons all sit in their lowest level, and the visible lines need an electron one step up. The hydrogen lines are strongest in stars of middling temperature, and that is the trap: "different-looking spectrum" was read as "different stuff" when it mostly meant "different temperature".
:::

:::fig c15_spectra() | Three stars' spectra, schematic. The dark lines of hydrogen are faint in the hottest star, where the hydrogen is ionised, strong in a star of middling temperature, and faint again in the coolest, where the electrons sit in the lowest level. All three stars are mostly hydrogen. Line positions follow the wavelength axis; the colour strips are normalised to compare line strength.

At Harvard, Annie Cannon had spent years sorting the spectra of stars into classes by their lines; put in order of temperature, hottest first, the classes run O, B, A, F, G, K, M. Then, in 1925, a young astronomer at Harvard, Cecilia Payne, took Saha's equation and asked it the question nobody had asked properly: allowing for temperature, how much of each element is really there? The answer was that the Sun and the stars are overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium, with everything else a trace. It was so unexpected that the astronomer Henry Norris Russell told her it was clearly impossible, and her thesis went to print with the hydrogen result labelled "spurious". It was confirmed within three years, by Russell among others. Later the astronomer Otto Struve called it "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy".

The modern figures: about 73% of the Sun's mass is hydrogen and 25% is helium, leaving about 2% for everything else. The Earth's crust, by contrast, is mostly oxygen, silicon and aluminium, which is why everyone had expected the Sun to be made of the same. And the stuff of a star is not fire, and not gas as you know it. At those temperatures atoms are broken into nuclei and loose electrons, a state called ==plasma==; the Sun's ==absorption lines== are made in its cooler outer layers, where some atoms still hold their electrons.

:::note Plasma
A gas so hot that its atoms have lost electrons, leaving charged nuclei and free electrons mixed together. Stars are made of it.
:::

:::note Absorption lines
Dark lines in a spectrum where atoms in a cooler gas have swallowed particular colours. Each element has its own set, but how strong the lines are depends on temperature as much as on how much of the element there is.
:::

:::eq
**Wien's displacement law (star colour and temperature)**: *λ*_peak × *T* = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m·K
:::

:::story star-stuff

:::careful
> "Different lines in two stars' spectra mean they are made of different things."

Mostly they mean the stars are at different temperatures. Nearly all stars are made of the same stuff, hydrogen and helium with a sprinkling of everything else; what changes with temperature is which atoms still have the electrons they need to make their lines. Saha's equation lets you allow for that, and when Payne did, the differences in recipe almost vanished.
:::

:::

:::check
Why did astronomers expect the Sun to be made of the same stuff as the Earth's crust, and what did they get wrong?
???
Many of the Sun's dark lines matched elements known on Earth, and it seemed natural that a star should be made of the familiar stuff. The mistake was to read the strength of a line as the amount of the element. Line strength depends on temperature too, because only atoms with their electrons in the right places can make a given line. Once Saha's equation allowed for that, the Sun came out as mostly hydrogen and helium.

## 15.5 How far are the stars?

Hold up your thumb at arm's length against the far wall of the room. Close one eye, then the other. The thumb jumps against the wall. Bring it closer to your face and the jump is bigger; hold it farther away and the jump shrinks. The jump is called ==parallax==, and it is the only direct way we have of measuring the distance to a star.

:::note Parallax
The apparent shift of a nearby object against a distant background when you look from two places. The farther the object, the smaller the shift. For a star, the two places are the Earth six months apart.
:::

:::predict
You hold a pen at arm's length and note how far it jumps against the wall when you switch eyes. A friend holds it twice as far from your eyes. How big is the jump now?
- Twice as big
- The same
- About half as big
???
About half as big. The shift depends on how far apart your eyes are compared with the distance to the pen, and doubling the distance halves the angle. Now imagine the pen is a star. Your eyes, 6 or 7 centimetres apart, are hopeless. The farthest apart two viewpoints can ever be, for anyone on the Earth, is the two sides of its orbit round the Sun, 300 million kilometres apart: look in January and again in July.
:::

Even with that baseline the nearest stars shift by a hair. Astronomers define a star's parallax as half the total shift, the angle you would get from a baseline of one Earth–Sun distance, and measure it in arcseconds, each 1/3,600 of a degree. How small is that? A ₹1 coin is 22 mm across. It would look one arcsecond wide from about 4.5 km away, a little less than the length of the Bandra–Worli Sea Link. The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, has a parallax of 0.77 arcseconds, the coin seen from about 6 km, and every other star shows less. Friedrich Bessel was the first to measure one, in 1838, for a star called 61 Cygni. Today the satellite Gaia measures them for nearly a billion stars, to a few millionths of an arcsecond.

:::fig c15_parallax() | Parallax. Seen from the Earth in January and again in July, a nearby star appears to shift against the far stars behind it. The parallax *p* is half the total shift, the angle at the star between the Sun and the Earth. Inset: the same effect with your thumb and two eyes. Not to scale: for the nearest star the angle is under one arcsecond.
:::live parallax

The distance at which a star's parallax would be exactly one arcsecond is called a ==parsec==, and it is 206,265 times the Earth–Sun distance, or 3.26 ==light-years==, a light-year being the distance light travels in a year. Proxima is 1.3 parsecs away, 4.25 light-years: its light takes four and a quarter years to reach you, where the Sun's takes eight minutes (Chapter 10). The arithmetic is simple: distance in parsecs is one divided by the parallax in arcseconds. The difficulty is all in the measuring, and it sets a limit.

:::note Parsec and light-year
A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 × 10¹⁵ m. A parsec is the distance at which a star's parallax is one arcsecond: 3.26 light-years. Both are distances, never times.
:::

:::work
Parallax measured to ±0.001″. For the distance to be good to 10%, the parallax must be at least 0.01″.
Distance = 1 ÷ 0.01 = 100 parsecs, about 326 light-years.
Beyond that, this method alone becomes too rough.
:::

:::scene Harvard, 1912

Our galaxy is far bigger than 100 parsecs, and the other galaxies are far beyond it, so another way was needed. Henrietta Leavitt was one of the "computers" at Harvard College Observatory, women hired to measure photographic plates. She was studying the Small Magellanic Cloud, a cloud of stars so far away that all its stars are at very nearly the same distance from us, and in it a kind of star that pulses, brightening and fading over days or weeks: ==Cepheids==, named for the first one found, Delta Cephei. In 1912 she reported that the brighter-looking Cepheids always had the longer pulses. Because all the stars in the Cloud are at about the same distance, brighter-looking meant truly brighter. So a Cepheid's pulse told you its true brightness, and comparing that with how bright it looked told you how far away it was, anywhere you could find one. Leavitt could not set the scale herself, because nobody then knew the distance to the Cloud; others did that later. With her measuring tape, Edwin Hubble found the distances to other galaxies, and then found something stranger, which is Section 15.7.

:::note Cepheid
A large, pulsing star whose brightness rises and falls over days or weeks. The slower the pulse, the brighter the star really is, so its pulse gives its distance.
:::

:::story star-distances

:::careful
> "A light-year is a length of time."

It is a distance: how far light travels in one year, about 9.5 million million kilometres. Proxima Centauri is 4.25 light-years away, which also tells you that its light took 4.25 years to arrive, so you see it as it was four years ago. But the unit measures the road, not the journey.
:::

:::school
IGCSE 6.2.2 uses the light-year as the unit for the distances of stars and galaxies. Write it, and think of it, as a distance.
:::

:::

:::check
Leavitt's brighter-looking Cepheids pulsed more slowly. Why could she trust that "brighter-looking" meant "truly brighter"?
???
Because all her Cepheids were in the Small Magellanic Cloud, at very nearly the same distance from us. When distance is the same for every star, the ones that look brighter are the ones that really are brighter. That is what let the pulse, which anyone can time, stand in for the true brightness, which nobody can see directly.
:::

## 15.6 How stars die

:::scene At sea, Bombay to England, 1930

In 1930 a nineteen-year-old from Madras, Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, nephew of the C. V. Raman of Chapter 11, boarded the SS *Pilsna* at Bombay for England, where he was to study. He had finished college at nineteen. On the long voyage he began working on a problem that sounds harmless: what happens to a star like the Sun when its fuel runs out?

The answer begins with the squeeze. When the core of a Sun-like star has turned its hydrogen into helium, nothing holds it up against its own gravity, and it shrinks. The electrons in it are pressed closer and closer. Then a rule from the quantum world takes over, the Pauli principle: no two electrons can be in the same place at the same time doing the same thing. Press them closer than that rule allows and they push back, with a pressure that has nothing to do with their electric charge and is far stronger than ordinary repulsion. Think of a local at rush hour, where every seat is taken; however hard the crowd pushes at the doors, the seats do not get smaller. The electrons' rule is like that, only stricter. The shrinking stops, and what is left is a ==white dwarf==: the dead core of a star, held up by the refusal of its electrons to be crowded further.

:::note White dwarf
What is left of a Sun-like star when its fuel is gone: a core held up by the pressure of electrons that cannot be squeezed closer together. This degeneracy pressure is a quantum effect, far stronger than electric repulsion.
:::

:::predict
A white dwarf is held up by its crowded electrons. Add more mass to it. Does it get bigger or smaller?
- Bigger: more stuff takes up more room
- Smaller
- The same size: the electrons are already as close as they can be
???
Smaller. More mass means more gravity, and the extra gravity crushes the electrons closer still. The more massive the white dwarf, the smaller it is, the opposite of everything you handle in daily life. Chandrasekhar's question on the *Pilsna* was where that ends.
:::

He added the new physics of relativity to the calculation, and the answer was a limit. As the mass goes up, the radius comes down, faster and faster, and the models give a radius of zero at about 1.4 times the mass of the Sun. That is the ==Chandrasekhar limit==, and it means that no white dwarf can exist above it: for anything heavier, even the electrons' refusal cannot stop the collapse, and the star must end some other way. Those other ways have names you may have heard, neutron stars and black holes, and they belong to another book. In 1935 the astronomer Arthur Eddington ridiculed the idea in public. Chandrasekhar was right, and in 1983 he was given the Nobel Prize for it.

:::note Chandrasekhar limit
The greatest mass a star can end its life with and still become a white dwarf: about 1.4 times the Sun's mass, about 2.8 × 10³⁰ kg. Heavier cores collapse further.
:::

:::fig c15_white_dwarf() | How a white dwarf's size depends on its mass, schematic. Adding mass makes it smaller, and the models reach zero size at about 1.4 Suns, the Chandrasekhar limit. The Sun, at one solar mass, will end below the limit, as a white dwarf. The shape of the curve is drawn in outline only.

The Sun has one solar mass, under the limit, so this is its future: in the very far future, its core will become a white dwarf. Many stars born far heavier than 1.4 Suns shed much of their mass as they age and still end below the limit. The ones that cannot are the ones Chandrasekhar's limit was really about: when their cores collapse, they blow off their outer layers in a cataclysmic ==supernova== explosion, seeding space with the carbon, oxygen, silicon and iron forged inside them, and leaving behind a neutron star or a black hole. About 4.6 billion years ago, a vast cloud of hydrogen, helium and such recycled star-dust pulled itself together under gravity to form our own Solar System. As the cloud shrank, any slight initial rotation made it spin faster and flatten into a swirling ==accretion disk== around the young protostar at the centre, just as a spinning skater pulls her arms in. Close to the hot young Sun, only high-melting-point rock and metal could condense into solid grains, accreting into the four small, dense, rocky inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars). Farther out beyond the "frost line", where it was cold enough for water, methane and ammonia ices to freeze as well, growing cores had far more solid material to sweep up and grew massive enough to capture huge envelopes of hydrogen and helium gas, forming the four giant outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). Every iron atom in your blood and every silicon atom in the beach sand at Juhu was forged inside an earlier star and gathered in that spinning disk.

:::story star-death

:::school
IGCSE *6.1.2* and *6.2.1* ask for the accretion model of Solar System formation (gravity pulling a spinning cloud of gas and dust into a flat accretion disk, explaining why the four inner planets are small and rocky while the four outer planets are large and gaseous) alongside the life cycle of stars: nebula → protostar → main-sequence star → red giant (or red supergiant) → planetary nebula + white dwarf (or supernova → neutron star / black hole for the most massive stars).
:::

:::careful
> "The electrons hold the star up because they are all negative and repel each other."

Electrons do repel each other, but that is not what holds a white dwarf up. The pressure comes from the Pauli rule, that no two electrons can be in the same place doing the same thing, and it is a different effect, far stronger than electric repulsion. Squeeze a white dwarf and it is the rule, not the charge, that pushes back.
:::

:::check
Will the Sun become a white dwarf?
???
Yes. The Sun's mass is 1 solar mass, below the Chandrasekhar limit of about 1.4, so when its fuel is spent its core can be held up by its electrons. Only a core above the limit must collapse further.
:::

## 15.7 The stretching universe

In Chapter 9 the horn of a local dropped in pitch as the train passed you: the sound waves from a source moving away are stretched, so their frequency falls. Light does the same. The lines in the spectrum of a star moving away from us sit at slightly longer wavelengths than they would in a laboratory, shifted towards the red; a star coming towards us shows them shifted towards the blue. The first is called a ==redshift==, and it is the hinge on which the biggest discovery in this book turns.

:::note Redshift
A stretching of light waves, so that every line in a spectrum appears at a longer wavelength than in the laboratory. It shows a source moving away, or, for distant galaxies, space stretching while the light travels.
:::

From 1912 Vesto Slipher measured the light of the spiral "nebulae", the faint smudges that Leavitt's tape would soon show to be other galaxies, and found nearly all of them redshifted: racing away, some at 1,800 km/s. Then Edwin Hubble and Milton Humason, in 1929 and 1931, put Slipher's speeds next to Cepheid distances and found a plain rule, now ==Hubble's law==: the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes, in proportion. The current value is about 22 km/s for every million light-years of distance, so a galaxy 100 million light-years away recedes at about 2,200 km/s.

:::note Hubble's law
A galaxy's speed away from us is proportional to its distance: *v* = *H* × *d*, with *H* about 22 km/s for each million light-years.
:::

:::predict
Every galaxy we measure, in every direction, is moving away from us, and the farther ones faster. Does that put us at the centre of the universe?
- Yes: everything is moving away from here
- No: an observer in any galaxy would see exactly the same thing
- Only the nearby galaxies are really moving
???
No. Draw ants on a ruler at the 2, 4, 7 and 12 cm marks, and stretch the ruler so that it doubles in length in one minute. The ant at 2 cm sees the others move away at 2, 5 and 10 cm per minute: speed in proportion to distance, exactly Hubble's law. Now sit on the ant at 7 cm instead: it sees the others recede at 5, 3 and 5 cm per minute, the same law again. Every ant sees itself at the centre, and none of them is. None of the ants moved of its own accord. The ruler stretched.
:::

:::fig c15_ruler() | Ants on a stretching ruler. Top: the ruler at the start, with ants at 2, 4, 7 and 12 cm. Bottom: one minute later, when the ruler has doubled. The ant at 2 cm sees the others recede at 2, 5 and 10 cm per minute, in proportion to their distance; the ant at 7 cm sees 5, 3 and 5 cm per minute. Every ant sees the same law, and no ant is the centre.
:::live expansion

A loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven does the same in three dimensions: every raisin sees every other raisin move away, faster if farther. Notice what does not happen: the raisins do not grow. Neither do galaxies. Gravity holds each galaxy together, and only the space between them stretches. That is the better way to picture the redshift too. The light from a distant galaxy has been travelling for hundreds of millions of years through space that was stretching; the waves were stretched with it, and arrive longer than they left. The galaxies are passengers. The stretching of space is the thing.

That is what the ==Big Bang== means, and it is not an explosion. An explosion happens at a place and throws things out into the empty space around it. There is no place where the universe began and no empty space it is expanding into: the stretching happens everywhere at once, and every observer, in every galaxy, sees it spreading away from them. Run it backwards and everything we can see was once far closer together, hotter and denser, everywhere. Run Hubble's rule backwards and the simple sum gives about 13.6 billion years; the careful figure, allowing for how the stretching has changed its pace, is 13.8 billion years.

:::note Big Bang
The name for the beginning of the universe's stretching, about 13.8 billion years ago, when everything we can see was far closer together. It happened everywhere; there is no place in space where it was.
:::

If the early universe was really hot and dense everywhere, its thermal glow should still be all around us. About 380,000 years after the beginning, the expanding plasma cooled to around 3,000 K — cool enough for protons and electrons to combine into neutral hydrogen atoms for the first time — and the universe suddenly became transparent. The orange-white thermal glow that filled all of space at that moment has been travelling in every direction ever since. As space stretched by a factor of about 1,100 over the next 13.8 billion years, the wavelength of that ancient light stretched by the same factor of 1,100, shifting it out of the visible spectrum, past the infrared, and into the microwave band at a temperature of just 2.725 K above absolute zero (peak wavelength around 1 mm, right in the band J. C. Bose explored in Calcutta). In 1965 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, testing a sensitive horn antenna in New Jersey, found a faint, uniform microwave hiss coming equally from every part of the sky, day and night, that refused to go away even after they cleared out the pigeons nesting in the horn. It was the ==cosmic microwave background radiation== (CMBR): the cooled afterglow of the hot early universe, and the second great pillar of evidence for the Big Bang alongside Hubble's redshift of galaxies.

:::note Cosmic microwave background (CMBR)
Microwave radiation filling all of space and arriving equally from every direction with a thermal spectrum of 2.725 K. It is the light released when the hot early universe first cooled enough to become transparent, stretched by a factor of about 1,100 into the microwave band as space expanded.
:::

:::home A balloon universe
With an adult, draw a dozen dots on an uninflated balloon and one short wavy line between two of them. Inflate it slowly and compare the gaps: the farther-apart dots separate more. No dot on the surface is a special centre. The wavy line stretches too, modelling the stretching of light. Watch for a limit of the model: ink dots stretch with the rubber, whereas galaxies held together by gravity do not simply grow with expanding space. The balloon also has a centre inside it; our universe does not need that extra place in the picture.
:::

:::story expanding

:::careful
> "The Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space, and the galaxies are flying away from it."

Nothing is flying out from a point into empty space. Space itself is stretching, everywhere, and the galaxies are carried apart with it, like the ants on the ruler. Every galaxy sees all the others receding, so none is at the centre, and there is no spot you could visit where it began. The rulers, balloons and loaves all have an edge and a middle; the universe has neither.
:::

:::school

"Theory" there means a well-tested explanation, never a guess.

## The star that's mostly hydrogen

:::case The star that's mostly hydrogen
An astronomer in 1920 lays three spectra on the desk. Star A is hot and blue, and shows almost no hydrogen lines. Star B is a little hotter than the Sun, and its hydrogen lines are the strongest thing in its spectrum. Star C is cool and red, and its hydrogen lines are faint again. Many of the dark lines in all three match metals known on Earth.

The astronomer concludes that Star B is made largely of hydrogen, Stars A and C of something else, and that the Sun, like the Earth's crust, is mostly the familiar heavy stuff.

Use Saha's idea and Payne's result to say what all three stars are really made of, and where the reasoning went wrong.
???
All three are mostly hydrogen and helium, like the Sun: about 73% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, with about 2% left for everything else. Star A's hydrogen is so hot that it has lost its electrons, so it cannot make the lines. Star C is so cool that its electrons sit in the lowest level, and the visible hydrogen lines need an electron one step up. Star B is at the temperature where hydrogen shows best. The astronomer read line strength as amount, when it depends on temperature as much as on quantity. That is the mistake Saha's equation corrects and Payne's thesis exposed: the stars are mostly hydrogen, and the Earth is the odd one out.
:::

This is where the book stops, and it stops where physics does: in front of questions nobody can answer yet. What is light, between the wave that makes Young's fringes and the packet that frees an electron? The equations do both; a picture that does both is missing. What makes the monsoon come, the giant sea breeze or the shifting rain belt? Chapter 8's argument is still going on, and you are living inside the evidence. Does hot water really freeze first? Nobody agrees. And the stretching universe: the model says that everything was once far closer together, and says nothing about what, if anything, came before, or whether "before" is even a question.

Every answer in this book was once a question like these, held by someone who did not know the answer and refused to pretend: Aryabhata in his boat, Bose at his desk in Dacca, Payne over her plates with "spurious" written across her best result, a nineteen-year-old at the rail of the *Pilsna*. They looked, they guessed, they predicted before they checked, they kept what they saw apart from what they thought, they did the sum, and they said plainly what they did not know.
