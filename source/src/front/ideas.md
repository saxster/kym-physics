## Five ideas that run through everything

Physics can look like a long list of separate laws and formulas. It is not. Almost everything in this book comes from five connected ideas, used again and again in different places. When something puzzles you, ask which of the five is at work.

:::raw
<ol class="ideas">
<li><b>Forces change motion; they do not keep it going.</b>A moving thing keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line unless something pushes or pulls on it. A sliding book slows down because the table rubs against it, not because the book runs out of push. A force is what makes something speed up, slow down or turn. And a force is never alone: whenever one object pushes another, the second pushes back on the first just as hard, in the opposite direction.</li>
<li><b>Energy is never used up; it only changes form and spreads out.</b>You can store energy by lifting a stone, stretching a spring, warming a pot of water or speeding up a ball. When anything happens, energy moves from one store to another, and the total amount stays exactly the same. What changes is how spread out that energy is. Once a bouncing ball has shared its energy with the floor and the air around it as tiny random jiggles of atoms, the energy is all still there, as warmth, but it is too scattered to gather back and lift the ball again.</li>
<li><b>Everything is made of restless particles.</b>Even a cold iron bar is made of atoms jiggling in place, and the air in a quiet room is made of molecules racing about faster than a rifle bullet, colliding billions of times a second. Temperature tells us how hot something is; for a gas, it tracks the average energy of the particles’ random motion. Pressure is the steady drumbeat of trillions of them bouncing off a wall. Melt, boil, cool or squeeze something, and you are just changing how its particles move and how closely they pack.</li>
<li><b>Waves and fields carry energy across a gap without carrying matter across.</b>Drop a pebble into a pond: the ripples travel across the water to the far bank, while a floating leaf only bobs up and down in place. Speak across a room: a pattern of squeezed and stretched air travels to your friend's ear, while the air in front of your mouth stays near your mouth. A magnet pulls an iron pin across empty space, and sunlight crosses 150 million kilometres of vacuum to warm your face, carried by electric and magnetic fields.</li>
<li><b>One set of rules governs both Earth and sky.</b>For thousands of years people assumed that the heavens followed one set of rules and the Earth another. They do not. The gravity that pulls a dropped stone to the floor is the very same pull that holds the Moon in orbit around the Earth and the Earth in orbit around the Sun. The glowing gases in a streetlamp emit and absorb the very same colours of light as the gases in a distant star, which is how we know what the stars are made of without ever visiting them.</li>
</ol>
:::

And underneath all five is a single habit of mind: **keep what you observe separate from the model you use to explain it, and test every model by predicting what will happen next.** What you see or measure is the observation. The story of forces, particles or waves that explains it is the model, and a good experiment can always sharpen it or prove it wrong.
