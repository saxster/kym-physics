## A letter to Kym

:::raw
<p class="salute">Dear Kym,</p>
:::

Sit still for a moment. Really still: feet on the floor, hands in your lap. Now here is the strange part. You are moving at about 1,600 kilometres an hour. The ground under Mumbai is carried round by the turning Earth at that speed, faster than a passenger jet, and you are going with it. At the same time the whole Earth is racing round the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second. Since you started reading this letter you have travelled further than the length of the Western line, and you have not felt a thing.

Why not? Fifteen hundred years ago, in Kusumapura, near today's Patna, an astronomer named Aryabhata gave a beautiful answer. When you sit in a boat sliding smoothly downstream, he said, it is the trees on the bank that seem to move backwards. The stars seem to wheel across the sky for the same reason: it is we who are turning. It took the rest of the world more than a thousand years to agree with him, and it took Galileo and Newton to explain *why* steady motion cannot be felt. That explanation is the first big idea in this book, and you already half know it from every local train that has started so smoothly you only noticed when the platform began to slide away.

Physics is the study of how things move, push, pull, shine, warm up, cool down and hum. It explains why a cricket ball and a coin dropped together land together, why the steel thali feels colder than the wooden board when they sit in the same room, why the sky is blue and the Marine Drive sunset is orange, why the causeway to Haji Ali disappears under the sea twice a day, and how a magnet spinning inside a coil sends electricity to the fan above your head. It also explains the Moon, and how a spacecraft from Sriharikota could land gently near its south pole in 2023.

I should tell you something first: I am not a physicist. I don't know much physics at all. What I have tried to do is make the book I would have loved to read when I was your age: one that tells you who found each idea and how, that lets you try things in the kitchen, and that never just hands you a formula to learn by heart. Much of what is in here was new to me too. To make sure it is right, every explanation was built from the free courses that MIT and OpenStax share with the world, and every fact and number was checked against them and worked out again. Where the people who really know physics still argue about something, the book says so.

A lot of physics feels wrong at first. That is not because physics is weird. It is because your everyday experience is full of friction and air, and they hide the simple rules underneath. So almost every section of this book starts with an idea that seems obviously true: that a moving thing slowly runs out of push, that heavy things fall faster, that summer comes when the Earth is closer to the Sun, that something comes out of your eyes when you look. Clever people believed each of these for centuries. Then someone did an experiment that showed what really happens. You will do many of those experiments too, with things from the kitchen, your phone and a torch.

Before each answer the book will ask you to **predict first**. Please really do it: pick an answer before you read on. A prediction that turns out wrong is not a failure. It is the most useful moment in learning, because it shows you exactly where your picture of the world needs to change. Nobody is grading you here.

The book tries to explain everything in the simplest way that is still *true*. The physicist Richard Feynman, who was famous for making hard things clear, liked to say that knowing the name of something is not the same as knowing something about it. So you will see the thing first and learn its name afterwards. When a word sounds like an explanation but is only a label, like "energy makes it go", the book tries to show you the spring and the gears instead. If something still doesn't make sense, that is the book's fault, not yours. Tell me, and we will find a better way to explain it together.

The heroes are real people: a Dutch engineer who dropped two lead balls from a church tower and heard a single thud, a doctor on a ship who noticed that sailors' blood was the wrong colour, a man in Calcutta who rang a bell with invisible waves through the walls of a building, a physicist on a ship to Bombay who wondered why the sea is blue, and a nineteen-year-old on another ship from Bombay who worked out how stars die. When you see a word with a little book beside it, tap it, and you will get the whole story.

With love,

:::raw
<p class="sign">Amar</p>
