## About the models and numbers in this book

### Where the numbers come from

Most numbers in this book are real measurements: the speed of sound and of light, the strength of gravity, the size of the Earth and the distance to the Moon, the heat it takes to warm water, the voltage of a cell. They come from MIT OpenCourseWare and OpenStax (listed in <a href="#credits">Where this book comes from</a>), checked against standard references such as NIST, NASA, ISRO and the India Meteorological Department, and every calculation was redone by computer. They are rounded to make them easy to read, so another book may give slightly different last digits.

A few numbers are made up on purpose to make an example easy to follow, like the mass of an imaginary trolley. Whenever a number is invented, the text says so.

### The models, and where they break

**Points and blocks.** Drawing a cricket ball, a car or the Moon as a single dot is perfect for working out how it moves as a whole. It says nothing about spin, squashing or what is inside.

**No friction, no air.** Many examples switch friction and air off, the way Galileo did, to show the simple rule underneath. Real things do have friction and air, and the book always comes back to say how much difference they make.

**Rays of light.** A ray is a pencil line showing which way light travels. It is excellent for mirrors, shadows and lenses. It cannot explain colours in a soap bubble or the bright spot in a shadow; for those, light behaves as a wave.

**Field lines.** The lines drawn around a magnet show which way a compass would point and where the pull is strongest. They are a drawing, not strings in space.

**The circuit as a loop of rope.** Thinking of the charges in a wire as a loop of rope that the battery keeps moving is a good model for why the current is the same everywhere in a simple loop. Like every analogy, it stops working if you push it too far, and the text says where.

**The Sky explorer and diagrams of the Solar System.** Drawn to scale, the Sun, Earth and Moon would be dots too small to see across a screen-wide gap. So the sizes are enlarged, and the explorer says when it does this. The tidal bulges are drawn hugely exaggerated: in the open ocean the real ones are around a metre high, on a planet 12,700 kilometres across.

**Diagrams in general.** Every picture has been chosen to show one thing clearly. When you meet a new one, ask the question this whole book is about: what does this show truthfully, and how do we know?
