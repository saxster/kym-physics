:::scene Marine Drive, Mumbai, any clear evening

Stand on the sea wall at Marine Drive a little before sunset and look west, out over the Arabian Sea. The Sun is orange and getting redder as it sinks. The sky near it is yellow and orange too. But look straight up, and the sky overhead is still blue, and behind you, over the city, it is blue as well. One Sun, one sky, one lot of air, and three colours at once. After the Sun has gone the blue drains slowly away and the sky goes dark.

A common guess is that the sky is blue because it reflects the sea, which does not explain the orange, or why the sky is blue over a desert. The real answer came in two steps, beginning in London.

:::scene London, 1869

John Tyndall, lecturing at the Royal Institution, filled a long glass tube with air and a little vapour and shone a strong beam of light down it. As a fine haze of tiny particles formed inside, something happened that anyone could see. Looked at from the side, the beam glowed sky blue. Looked at end-on, through the whole length of the tube, the lamp behind it had turned orange, like a setting Sun. A tube of air and a lamp had made a sky and a sunset on a bench. Tyndall noticed one thing more, which we will come back to: the blue light coming out of the side of the tube was polarised.

Two years later a young English physicist, John William Strutt, better known by his title as Lord Rayleigh, worked out the mathematics of what Tyndall had seen: how much light a particle far smaller than a wavelength sends sideways, and how strongly that depends on the colour. The rule he found carries his name, and it turned out to need no haze at all. Much later he showed that the molecules of the air are enough on their own.

#### Step 1: what scattering is

Light is a travelling shake of electric and magnetic influence (you will meet those influences properly in Chapter 13). When light passes an air molecule, it shakes the molecule's electrons back and forth. A shaking electric charge sends out light of its own, in all directions, like a tiny antenna. So each molecule takes a very small share of the sunlight passing it and re-sends it sideways. That is ==scattering==: not a bounce, but a catch and a re-broadcast.

#### Step 2: blue scatters more

How much a tiny antenna re-sends depends strongly on how fast it is shaken, and so on the colour of the light. Blue light has a shorter wavelength than red, which means more shakes per second. For scatterers far smaller than the wavelength, the share of light scattered goes as one over the wavelength multiplied by itself four times. This is ==Rayleigh scattering==, and the fourth power is what makes the numbers dramatic.

:::work
**Blue against red.** Blue light has a wavelength of about 450 nm, red about 700 nm.
Scattering goes as 1 ÷ λ⁴, so the ratio is (700 ÷ 450)⁴ = 1.56⁴ = 5.9.
Air scatters blue light about six times as strongly as red.
:::

Notice what the sum does not say. It does not say that only blue is scattered. Every colour is scattered; blue simply gets scattered about six times more than red, and the colours in between fall in between.

:::fig s_scatter_bars() | How strongly air scatters each colour, compared with red at 700 nm. The bars follow the one-over-wavelength-to-the-fourth rule: violet at 400 nm is scattered more than nine times as much as red, blue at 450 nm about six times as much.

#### Step 3: the blue sky

Look anywhere in the sky except at the Sun itself. What reaches your eye from that direction is light that was travelling somewhere else, until an air molecule between you and empty space caught it and re-sent it towards you. The sky is sunlight, re-sent sideways by the air, and because the air re-sends blue six times more readily than red, the re-sent light is blue. Take the air away and there is nothing to do the re-sending: on the Moon, which has no air, the sky is black at noon with the Sun blazing in it.

#### Step 4: the orange sunset

The same rule, run the other way. At noon the sunlight that reaches you has crossed the thickness of the atmosphere more or less straight down, and lost only a little blue on the way. At sunset the Sun's light comes to you along a slanting path through the air that is far longer: roughly six times as much air when the Sun is 10° above the horizon, and about eleven times as much at 5° (these are rough figures, from treating the air as a flat layer). Along that long path most of the blue is scattered out of the direct beam, sent sideways to light up other people's skies. What arrives straight from the Sun is what is left: yellow, then orange, then red. The Sun has not cooled. Its light has been sieved by the air it crossed, and the blue was the part that fell through the sieve.

So the two colours have one cause. The blue over your head is the blue that was taken out of someone else's sunset, and the orange in front of you is sunlight with its blue taken out and sent to someone else's sky. Marine Drive, facing west over open sea, is one of the best places in the city to watch both at once.

:::fig c11_sky() | One rule, two colours. At noon (left), sunlight crosses a short stretch of air and the sky all round you is the blue it scattered sideways. At sunset (right), the beam crosses a much longer stretch, most of the blue is scattered away before it arrives, and the direct light that remains is orange. Not to scale, and the air layer is drawn far thicker than it is.

#### Step 5: Tyndall's extra clue

Now the word from the tube. Light shakes across its direction of travel, the way a wave on a rope shakes up and down while it runs along the rope. Ordinary sunlight is a jumble of shakes in every sideways direction. Light whose shake has one definite direction is ==polarised== light, and the property is called ==polarisation==.

Scattering sorts shakes. An electron can only re-send light in the directions it is not shaking along, so light scattered off at right angles to the sunbeam comes away shaking mostly in one direction. That is why Tyndall's blue glow was polarised, and why the blue sky is too, most strongly about 90° away from the Sun. You can check it. Polarised sunglasses pass shakes in one direction only. Look at a patch of blue sky well away from the Sun through them and slowly rotate the glasses: the patch darkens and brightens. It will not go black, because light that has been scattered twice or more arrives with its shakes jumbled again. Test the glasses first on a phone or laptop screen, which gives out polarised light: if the screen darkens as you turn the lenses, the lenses are polarised. If nothing changes, they are only tinted, and the sky will not perform for them.

:::careful
> "The sky is blue because it reflects the sea."

The sea has nothing to do with it. The sky is just as blue over Rajasthan and in the middle of Russia, and it is blue over the Arabian Sea at noon when the sea beneath it is grey with monsoon cloud. The air does it, catching sunlight and re-sending the blue. What makes the sea blue is a question of its own, and a scientist from Calcutta answered it from the deck of a ship.
:::

:::home The sky in a glass
Fill a clear glass with water, add a few drops of milk and stir, and in a darkened room shine a torch through it from one side. Look at the glass from the side and the beam has a bluish tinge; look through the glass towards the torch and the torch looks orange. Too milky and everything goes white, so start with very little milk and add a drop at a time. This is a model of the sky rather than a copy of it: the specks of fat in milk are much bigger than air molecules, so the imitation is rough, but the sorting of blue from orange is the same.
:::
