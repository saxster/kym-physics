:::scene Calcutta, 1895

In 1895 Jagadish Chandra Bose, a physics teacher at Presidency College in Calcutta, gave a public demonstration of something nobody in the room could see. On one side stood his transmitter, a spark that set electric charge surging back and forth tens of thousands of millions of times a second. Twenty-three metres away, about seventy-five feet, beyond two intervening walls, stood a receiver. When the spark fired, the receiver rang a bell and set off a small charge of gunpowder. Nothing visible had crossed the room. The story goes that the Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal, Sir Alexander Mackenzie, presided; accounts written later disagree about the date and about how many walls there were, so hold those details loosely. The experiment itself is not in doubt.

Hertz, in Karlsruhe eight years earlier, had made Maxwell's waves with wavelengths of metres. Bose went the other way. His waves were between 2.5 centimetres and 5 millimetres long, which made them, in modern language, microwaves, and at the short end millimetre waves. He chose that range partly for a practical reason: his laboratory at Presidency College was small, and short waves can be steered and focused in a small space. To do the steering he built, from scratch, almost every part of the kit: a spark transmitter, lenses of dielectric material to focus the waves, a polariser, horn-shaped antennas to gather them, a grating to split them, and a receiver, the coherer, that could tell when a wave had arrived.

:::scene London, January 1897

In January 1897 Bose lectured on his waves at the Royal Institution in London, at the invitation of Lord Rayleigh. It was the same lecture hall where Faraday had shown his motor and his iron ring. Bose was thirty-eight. In December 1901, on a hill in Newfoundland, Marconi reported hearing three faint dots, the letter S, sent from Cornwall across the Atlantic by waves of the same family; sceptics have argued ever since about whether he heard a signal or static. Bose was not in that race, and it is worth being exact about why. In 1901 a patent application for his crystal detector was filed in the United States, not by him but on his behalf by his friends Sara Bull and Sister Nivedita; it was granted in 1904 as US patent 755,840. Bose did not pursue it commercially, and it lapsed. The often-repeated line that he refused to patent anything is too simple; what is true is that he did not try to make money from the work.

In 2012 the IEEE placed a plaque in the main building of Presidency College, naming his experiments of 1894 to 1896 a Milestone in the history of electrical engineering.

#### Step 1: what a five-millimetre wave is

An electromagnetic wave is a ripple of electric and magnetic fields travelling at the speed of light, 300,000 kilometres a second. Its wavelength is the distance from one crest to the next; its frequency is how many crests pass each second. Multiply them and you always get the speed of light, so a shorter wave swings faster.

:::work
**How fast do Bose's waves swing?**
frequency = speed ÷ wavelength = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s ÷ 0.005 m = 6.0 × 10¹⁰ per second.
Sixty thousand million swings a second: 60 gigahertz.
A metre-long radio wave swings 300 million times a second, two hundred times more slowly.
:::

#### Step 2: why short waves behave like light

The shorter a wave, the more it behaves like a ray of light: it can be focused by a lens, bent, split, blocked by a polariser, and aimed. Metre-long waves spread round corners and wash over whole buildings. Bose's millimetre waves could be shaped with lenses and horns small enough for a bench, which is exactly why he could do optics with them: reflection, refraction, polarisation, all with waves the eye cannot see. Those same waves pass through walls that stop visible light, which is how they reached the bell. Whether a wall is "transparent" depends on the wave, not on the wall.

#### Step 3: how the bell rang

The receiver had to turn an arriving wave into something that could ring a bell. Bose's coherer did that: a device whose electrical contact changed when the waves hit it, so that a current from an ordinary battery could suddenly flow and work the bell. The arriving waves carried the signal, while the battery supplied the energy to ring this bell. Many receivers use local power to amplify a faint signal. But that is not a rule for every receiver: a crystal radio can make a faint sound using energy taken from the radio wave itself, with no battery.

#### Step 4: the same waves today

The physics has not changed since 1895. A wave five millimetres long still swings sixty thousand million times a second, still focuses through a lens, still passes through a wall, and the slice of the ladder from centimetres down to millimetres is now crowded with the signals that carry modern life about.

:::careful
> "Bose invented radio."

The documented record is narrower and more interesting. He made and detected millimetre waves in 1894 to 1896, built the first instruments for handling them, and showed them in Calcutta and London before Marconi's Atlantic signal. Hertz made the waves first; Marconi built the long-distance business. Bose's contribution was the short-wave optics, and it was far ahead of its time.
:::

:::careful
> "Walls stop waves."

Walls stop *some* waves. Visible light does not get through brick; Bose's millimetre waves did. What a material blocks depends on the wavelength, which is also why a phone works indoors but a torch does not shine through a door.
:::
