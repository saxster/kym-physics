:::scene The arsenal at Munich, 1798

In the workshops of the Munich arsenal, cannon were made from solid brass. A rough casting was clamped in place, a steel borer was pressed against it, and horses walking in a circle turned the barrel against the borer for hours, cutting out the bore. The man in charge was Count Rumford, an American-born soldier and inventor who had risen high in the service of the ruler of Bavaria. He had noticed something that everyone in the workshop knew and nobody thought about: the brass got very hot. Hour after hour, as long as the horses walked, the metal and the brass shavings came out hot enough to hurt.

Rumford did think about it, and it worried him, because of what the best science of the day said heat was.

#### Step 1: the fluid called caloric

The leading theory in 1798 said that heat was a substance. Chemists called it ==caloric==: an invisible, weightless fluid that lived inside every object and flowed from hotter things to colder ones, the way water flows downhill. It was a good theory, as wrong theories go. It explained why a hot spoon warms the tea and not the other way round. It explained why things swell when heated: the caloric pushed their particles apart. It gave chemists a tidy picture in which heat could be poured, stored and measured, and a body held a fixed amount of it.

In that picture, friction warmed the brass by squeezing caloric out of it, as you might squeeze water from a sponge. And a sponge, squeezed hard enough and long enough, runs dry.

#### Step 2: the blunt borer

Rumford arranged a test. He took a brass barrel, fixed a *blunt* steel borer against it so that it would scrape and rub rather than cut, and enclosed the end of the barrel in a wooden box filled with cold water. Then he set the horses walking. No fire was anywhere near. The only thing happening to the brass was that it was being rubbed.

The water in the box warmed. It kept warming. In his report to the Royal Society, published in 1798, he recorded the moment with some satisfaction: at two hours and thirty minutes, the water boiled.

:::fig s_rumford() | Rumford's test, in outline. A brass barrel turns against a blunt steel borer, driven by horses; the end of the barrel sits in a wooden box of cold water with a thermometer in it. There is no fire. As long as the horses walk, the water warms, and after two and a half hours it boils.

Then he asked the question that mattered. The brass had been rubbed for hours and had given out enough heat to boil a box of water. Did it show any sign of running dry? It did not. As long as the horses kept walking, the heat kept coming, and there was no reason to think it would ever stop. A sponge that never runs dry is not holding a fixed amount of water. A lump of brass that gives out heat for as long as you rub it is not holding a fixed amount of caloric.

:::work
**How much energy did the water take?**
Warming 1 kg of water by 1 °C takes 4186 J.
From a cool 20 °C to boiling at 100 °C is a rise of 80 °C.
So each kilogram of water in the box needed 80 × 4186 ≈ 335,000 J, about a third of a megajoule, and the horses supplied every joule of it by walking in a circle.
:::

#### Step 3: what the horses were really supplying

Whatever the water was getting, it was coming from the horses. The horses pushed the barrel round against the borer; the borer resisted; something was being done against that resistance, continuously, for two and a half hours. In the language of the chapters ahead, the horses were doing *work*, and the work was turning into warmth in the brass and then in the water.

Rumford's own guess was that heat was a kind of motion rather than a fluid. He could not say what was moving. Nobody could, for another fifty years, until Joule in Manchester measured exactly how much work made how much warming, and until physicists accepted that matter is made of particles that are never still.

#### Step 4: what an object actually has

Here is the modern answer, which is what caloric was reaching for and missing. Every object is made of particles that jiggle, spin and vibrate. The energy of that motion and of the particles’ interactions is the object's ==internal energy==, also called its ==thermal energy== here. Warm an object and its particles jiggle faster: its internal energy rises. Rub it, hammer it, stir it, and the same thing happens, because pushing on the particles speeds them up too. Afterwards there is no way to tell which route the energy took. A kilogram of water warmed by a flame and a kilogram warmed by a paddle wheel are identical.

So the brass in Munich did not contain heat. It contained internal energy, and the horses kept adding more. The word *heat* survives, but physicists now use it for one thing only: energy *on the move* from a hotter object to a colder one, because of the difference in their temperatures. Heat is a transfer, like a payment, and you cannot store a payment. You can only store what it buys.

:::careful
> "The brass had heat inside it, and the drill squeezed the heat out."

Nothing was squeezed out, because heat is not a thing an object holds. The brass held internal energy in its particles’ motion and interactions. Rubbing added more, for as long as the rubbing went on, and the extra energy flowed as heat into the colder water. If the brass had really held a fixed stock of heat, it would have run dry long before the water boiled.
:::

#### Step 5: why caloric lasted so long

It is easy to laugh at caloric. Do not. It fitted most of what people could see, and it took a very odd experiment to catch it out, because almost nothing in ordinary life rubs for two and a half hours without stopping. Rumford's result sat uncomfortably beside the fluid theory for decades, and the theory was only abandoned when Joule's careful numbers and the particle picture of matter arrived together. Being wrong for a good reason, and then letting go when the evidence turns, is what science looks like from the inside.

:::home Rumford's test in ten seconds
Rub a pencil eraser hard against a desk for ten seconds, then touch it with your fingertips. It is warm. Nothing hot touched it. Your arm did work against friction, and the work became internal energy in the rubber. Rub for twenty seconds and it is warmer still. There is no sign of the eraser running out of heat, for exactly the reason Rumford's brass did not.
:::
