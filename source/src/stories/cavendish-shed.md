:::scene Clapham, near London, 1798

In a big house on the edge of Clapham Common, south of London, lived one of the richest men in Britain and one of the shyest. Henry Cavendish had turned the upper rooms into an observatory and the drawing room into a laboratory. The one social event he never missed was the dinner before the Royal Society's weekly meeting, and anyone who went up to speak to him there was likely to get a high-pitched squeak and a view of his back. The story goes that he left notes for his housekeeper in the hall rather than speak to her, and that a second staircase was built so that he need never meet his women servants on the stairs. He wore the same old-fashioned suit for years and was sometimes seen after dark walking down the middle of the road.

In 1798, in his late sixties, this man did the most delicate experiment anyone had yet attempted.

#### Michell's machine

The idea was not his. A clergyman and scientist named John Michell had designed the apparatus and built it, then died in 1793 before he could use it. It came to Cavendish, who rebuilt it carefully and kept to Michell's plan.

A wooden rod, six feet long, hangs by its middle from a thin wire. At each end is a small lead ball, five centimetres across and 0.73 kg. Beside each small ball, on opposite sides of the rod, Cavendish could swing in a big lead ball, thirty centimetres across and 158 kg. Newton's rule says every lump of matter pulls on every other lump. So the big balls should pull the small ones towards them, the rod should swing round, and the wire should twist. The pull would be absurdly small. Two one-kilogram bags of sugar a metre apart attract each other with about 0.000 000 000 07 newtons.

A pull that small can be swamped by anything: a draught under the door, the warmth of a hand, a person breathing. So Cavendish sealed the apparatus in a mahogany case, put the case in a closed shed, and stayed out. He cut two holes in the shed walls, set a telescope in each, and watched the ends of the rod from outside, reading their position against a scale to better than a hundredth of an inch.

:::fig c5_cavendish() | Cavendish's balance seen from above. The tiny pull of each big lead ball on the small ball beside it twists the rod round by a hair, and the twist is read through a telescope from outside the shed.

#### What he saw

The rod turned. Not much, but it turned, and it swung slowly back and forth about its new position, taking about fifteen minutes for each swing at first and about seven and a half after he fitted a stiffer wire. From the size of the twist he could work out the force the big balls were exerting: about 0.000 000 17 newtons, or 1.74 × 10⁻⁷ N.

:::work
**Two pulls on the same small ball.** The big lead ball, sitting about 22 cm away, pulls the small ball with 1.74 × 10⁻⁷ N.
The Earth, whose centre is about 6,400 km away, pulls the same small ball with its weight: 0.73 kg × 9.8 N/kg = 7.2 N.
7.2 ÷ 0.000 000 174 ≈ 41,000,000. The Earth pulls forty-one million times harder than a 158 kg ball of lead.
The rule of gravity says exactly how a pull depends on mass and on distance. Knowing both pulls, both distances and the mass of the lead, Cavendish could work out how much matter the Earth contains, and, since its size was known, how dense it is.
:::

His answer: the Earth is 5.448 times as dense as water. His paper printed it as 5.48, because of a small arithmetic slip that nobody noticed until 1821. The modern value is 5.51. He was within about one per cent, in a shed, with a wooden rod and some lead.

Think about what that means. The rocks at the Earth's surface are far less dense than that. A planet averaging 5.5 must have something much denser deep inside it, and Cavendish's number was the first measurement to say so, made without anyone digging a single hole.

:::careful
> "Cavendish measured G, the gravitational constant."

That is how many textbooks put it, and it is not what he did. His paper is called *Experiments to determine the Density of the Earth*, and a density is what it gives. The constant G, the number in the modern formula for gravity, does not appear in print until about 1873, seventy-five years later. What Cavendish measured was the pull between two lumps of lead, and from it he weighed the world.
:::

:::home Why he needed the shed
Hang a drinking straw from a single thread taped to a shelf, in a room with the fan off, and wait until it is perfectly still. Now walk up to it slowly and hold one hand a few centimetres from one end, without touching. Watch the straw begin to turn. The warmth of your hand makes the air beside it rise, and that gentle current is enough to push the straw round. A pull of a ten-millionth of a newton would have been lost in a disturbance like that, which is why Cavendish stayed outside and looked in through a hole.
:::
