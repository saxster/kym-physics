:::scene Sriharikota, 14 July 2023

At 14:35 on 14 July 2023 an LVM3 rocket lifted off from the Second Launch Pad at Sriharikota carrying ==Chandrayaan-3==. The spacecraft weighed about 3.9 tonnes: a propulsion module of 2,148 kg to do the travelling, and a lander of 1,752 kg, the ==Vikram lander==, with a 26 kg rover called ==Pragyan== folded inside it.

It did not follow one straight path from the launch pad to the Moon. Its journey began with loops around Earth. The rocket put it into a long thin loop around Earth, and then for three weeks the spacecraft went round and round, each loop a little bigger than the last, before it let go of Earth at all. The slow road is the physics of Chapter 5 made visible, so it is worth walking down it step by step.

#### Step 1: the parking orbit

The rocket's job ended a few minutes after launch, with the spacecraft in an orbit that ==ISRO== had planned as about 170 km above Earth at its lowest point and about 36,500 km at its highest. That shape is an ellipse, Kepler's shape, with Earth at one focus; the low point is the *perigee* and the high point the *apogee*. Once there, no engine was running. The spacecraft was coasting, falling around Earth exactly as the Moon does, swinging fast through perigee and slow through apogee.

#### Step 2: loops that grow

Then came the firings, each one as the spacecraft swept through perigee. ISRO called them "Earth-bound perigee firings". The engine burned for a while, the spacecraft gained speed along its path, and the engine stopped. Nothing visible happened at once; the spacecraft was still at perigee, still a couple of hundred kilometres up. The effect appeared half an orbit later. With more speed at the low point it coasted out further before gravity turned it round, so the *apogee* rose while the perigee hardly moved.

ISRO's public updates record the orbit after each firing:

:::work
15 July, after the first firing: 173 × 41,762 km.
17 July, after the second: 226 × 41,603 km.
22 July, after the fourth: 233 × 71,351 km. (The update list skips the third firing and gives no orbit for a further one on 25 July.)
Kepler's third law says a bigger orbit takes longer. Taking ISRO's figures as heights above the surface, one lap took about 12.5 hours after the first firing and about 23.9 hours after the fourth.
At perigee of that fourth orbit the spacecraft was moving at about 10.6 km/s; at apogee, 71,000 km out, at about 0.9 km/s. Kepler's second law in action: fast when near, slow when far.
:::

:::fig s_orbit_raising() | Chandrayaan-3's Earth-bound loops, drawn to scale with the Earth from ISRO's figures: the orbit after the first perigee firing on 15 July, the orbit after the fourth on 22 July, and the start of the translunar loop of 1 August. Every firing happened at the low point on the left; every firing showed up as a higher far point on the right.

Why loops rather than a straight shot? ISRO's pages say what was done, not why, and this book will not guess for them. What the physics tells you is what each burn *does*: a short push at the fastest point of an orbit buys a big rise in its far point, and between pushes the spacecraft rides for free on gravity alone.

#### Step 3: letting go of Earth

On 1 August the engine fired once more at perigee, and this time the apogee leapt out to about 369,000 km: a loop that would reach the Moon's distance. ISRO called the manoeuvre trans-lunar injection. For four days the spacecraft climbed, slowing all the way as Earth's pull drew on it, towards a Moon that was moving along its own orbit to meet it.

#### Step 4: the brake behind the Moon

Here is the step most people get backwards. To be caught by the Moon, a spacecraft must *slow down*. Arriving from Earth, it is moving too fast for the Moon's weak pull to hold; left alone it would not be captured. So on 5 August, at 19:12, as it passed its closest to the Moon, Chandrayaan-3 fired its engine against its own motion for 1,835 seconds, about half an hour. The Moon's gravity closed round it, and it was in a lunar orbit, 164 km at its nearest and 18,074 km at its farthest, taking about 28 hours a lap. ISRO's press release says the orbit was "as intended".

The loops now shrank instead of growing: 170 × 4,313 km on 6 August, 174 × 1,437 km on 9 August, 151 × 179 km on 14 August and 153 × 163 km on 16 August, a near-circle about two hours round. On 17 August the lander separated from the propulsion module, which stayed behind in lunar orbit. By 20 August the lander was in a 25 × 134 km orbit, skimming within 25 km of the surface at its lowest.

#### Step 5: 18:04 on 23 August

The powered descent began on the afternoon of 23 August 2023, and at 18:04 Indian time Vikram touched down softly near 69.4° S. On 26 August the spot was named Shiv Shakti Point. Forty days had passed since launch, and eighteen since the brake behind the Moon.

The next day Pragyan rolled down its ramp. Over the following days it drove about 101 metres across the surface, and its laser instrument, which fires at a patch of soil and reads the colours in the flash, confirmed sulphur in the ground near the pole. A day on the Moon lasts about fourteen Earth days, with a night as long again, and the mission had been planned for one lunar day. On 4 September the lander and rover were put into sleep mode; on 22 September ISRO tried to re-establish contact, and that is where its public updates end.

Chandrayaan-3 had a predecessor. ==Chandrayaan-1== orbited the Moon in 2008 and 2009 carrying, among other instruments, NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper, which found the signature of water and hydroxyl in the lunar soil, announced on 24 September 2009. ISRO has also said that one of its own instruments on that mission saw water earlier; that claim is disputed.

:::careful
> "The engine fires to push the spacecraft outwards, away from Earth."

Every one of the Earth-bound firings pushed it *forwards* along its path, at perigee, adding speed. The orbit stretched outward half a lap later, because a faster spacecraft coasts further before gravity turns it round. It is Newton's cannon again: fire the ball faster and the loop gets longer.
:::

:::careful
> "Once it was in orbit round the Moon it had basically landed."

Eighteen days and a string of further manoeuvres separate the brake of 5 August from the touchdown of 23 August: the orbit had to be shrunk and rounded, the lander separated, and the final descent flown on its own engines. Getting into orbit is the easy half.
:::
