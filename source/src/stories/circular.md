:::scene Paris, 1673

Whirl a stone on a string and the string goes taut and tugs at your hand. Something, it seems, is pulling the stone outward, and the faster you whirl, the harder it pulls. In 1673 the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens published a book on pendulum clocks, the *Horologium Oscillatorium*, and in it he set out his rules for exactly that tug. He had worked them out years earlier, in 1659, in a manuscript called *De vi centrifuga*, and the word is his: *centrifugal*, fleeing the centre. His rules were right. The tug grows with the square of the speed, so doubling the speed makes it four times as big, and it shrinks as the circle gets wider.

Eleven years after Huygens' book, Isaac Newton sent Edmond Halley a manuscript in which he named a force too, and pointed it the other way: *centripetal*, seeking the centre. Both men had the same mathematics. The difference is which way the arrow points, and that difference is the whole of this story.

#### Step 1: Going round is accelerating

A stone whirled at a steady speed is not moving steadily. Its speed is constant, but its velocity, which includes direction, changes every instant: north, then east, then south. In Chapter 1 you learned that any change of velocity is an acceleration, so the stone is accelerating the whole way round, even at constant speed. Which way? Draw the velocity arrow at one moment and again a moment later, and the change between them points towards the centre of the circle. The acceleration of anything moving in a circle points inward, and its size is v²/r: the speed squared, divided by the radius. That is Huygens' rule, and it is why a car taking a bend at 100 km/h needs four times the grip it needs at 50.

#### Step 2: Something real must pull inward

An acceleration needs a force, pointing the same way: inward. For the stone it is the string's tension. For a car on a bend it is friction from the road on the tyres. For the Moon going round the Earth it is gravity. Whatever real force happens to point at the centre is doing the job, and Newton's name for the job is ==centripetal force==. It is a role, not a new kind of force: a centripetal force is always a tension or a friction or a gravity or the push of a wall, and it never gets a separate arrow of its own on a diagram.

But the string really is taut, and it really does tug your hand outward. What pulls on it? The stone does. This is the third law: the string pulls the stone inward, so the stone pulls the string outward, equally hard. There is an outward force in the picture, and it is real. It acts on the *string*, not on the stone. Nothing pulls the stone outward. The stone is being pulled in, and that is the only reason it goes round at all.

#### Step 3: Let go

If the inward force stops, the stone does not fly outward. It goes straight on, along the line it was moving on at that instant, which is the tangent to the circle. In a 1980 study of 47 university students, 30 per cent drew a ball released from a whirling string carrying on along a curve, as if the circle were somehow still in it; the "flies outward" picture is just as common in classrooms. Both are wrong in the same way: once nothing pushes, nothing bends.

Cut a gap in the rim of a paper plate and roll a marble round the inside. It leaves through the gap in a straight line, along the tangent, not outward and not curving. A hammer thrower lets go and the hammer flies off along the tangent; a drop of water on a spinning bicycle wheel leaves along the tangent too.

Where the rim is cut away nothing pushes it any more, and it carries straight on along the line it was moving on. The dashed line is the outward direction that nothing pushes it in.

#### Step 4: What you feel on a turning bus

Now the feeling that started it all. A bus turns left and you, standing in the aisle, lean to the right as if something had shoved you outward. Watch yourself from the pavement instead. Before the turn you and the bus are moving straight ahead. The bus turns; you, with nothing yet pushing you sideways, carry straight on, exactly as Newton's first law says. The bus comes round to meet you: its floor drags your feet and its wall or pole presses your shoulder, and those pushes are *inward*, towards the centre of the bend. From outside, nothing pushed you outward. You went straight while the bus turned under you. The "flung out" feeling is the feeling of not yet having been turned.

#### Step 5: The honest note about rotating frames

Huygens was not simply wrong. If you insist on describing the world from inside the turning bus, treating the bus as if it stood still, then Newton's laws stop working unless you add a made-up outward force on everything, and that is what Huygens' ==centrifugal force== is. Physicists call it fictitious: no object exerts it, and it appears only because the frame you are measuring in is turning. Engineers use it every day as a bookkeeping device, and it is fine if you know what you are doing. In this book we stand on the pavement, where every force has an object that exerts it, and from the pavement the centrifugal force does not exist.

One place the turning of the frame is big enough to measure is the Earth itself. The ground you stand on is going round the Earth's axis once a day, so a weighing scale at the equator reads very slightly less than it would if the Earth stood still: the correction is about 0.034 m/s², a third of one per cent of g. At Mumbai's latitude it is about 0.032 m/s². That is centrifugal bookkeeping doing real work.

:::work
**Water in a swinging bucket** (the radius is a guess).
Swing a bucket of water in a vertical circle of radius 0.8 m. At the top the bucket is upside down, and the water stays in if the bucket is moving fast enough to need an inward, which here means downward, acceleration of at least g. The condition is v²/r ≥ g, so v ≥ √(9.8 × 0.8) = 2.8 m/s: one full swing every 2π × 0.8 ÷ 2.8 = 1.8 s, or faster.
Swing it at 5 m/s and the inward acceleration is 5² ÷ 0.8 = 31 m/s², about 3.2 g. The bottom of the bucket has to push the water inward with three times its weight. Nothing is holding the water in; the bucket is pulling it round.
:::

:::careful
> "When I let go of the whirling ball, it flies outward."

It flies along the tangent: straight on, in the direction it was moving at that instant. Outward would mean away from the centre along the radius, and nothing pushes it that way. Watch the marble leave the plate, or the drops leave a spinning wheel.
:::

:::careful
> "Centrifugal force throws me against the door when the car turns."

Nothing throws you. You keep moving straight while the car turns, and the door comes round and meets you; then the door pushes you inward and you turn with the car. Every real force on you points towards the centre of the bend. The outward "force" is a way of describing the ride from inside the car, and it has no object exerting it.
:::

:::school
Your IGCSE syllabus describes circular motion as motion in a circular path due to a force perpendicular to the motion. The word "centrifugal" never appears in it. In an exam, name the real inward force: tension, friction, gravity or the push of a surface.
:::

:::home The plate with a gap
Cut a gap about the width of your thumb in the rim of a paper plate. Put the plate on a smooth floor and set a marble rolling round the inside, fast enough to hug the rim. Before it reaches the gap, say out loud which way it will go. It leaves in a straight line along the rim's direction at the gap. Mark its path with a strip of tape and compare it with the line from the centre of the plate out through the gap.
:::
