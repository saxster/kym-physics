:::scene Batavia, Java, 1840

A Dutch ship lay in the harbour at Batavia, the port that is now Jakarta, and its young German doctor was bleeding sailors. Bleeding was still ordinary medicine in 1840, and Julius Robert Mayer had done it many times at home. But here, in the heat of the tropics, something was different. The blood he drew from the sailors' veins was a bright red, far brighter than the dark venous blood he knew from Europe.

The story Mayer told afterwards, as far as it can be reconstructed, runs like this. Blood is bright red when it is rich in oxygen. Venous blood is normally dark because the body has used the oxygen to burn food and keep itself warm. In a hot climate the body needs far less warming, so it burns less, and the blood comes back to the heart still bright. That led Mayer to a much larger thought. The body's warmth, the food it burns, and the work its muscles do must all be forms of one thing, swapped one for another, with nothing created and nothing destroyed.

He wrote it up in 1842. Almost nobody read it. He was a doctor, not a professor, and his paper was not the kind of thing physicists read. Recognition came in the 1860s, when the idea he had reached by a strange road was already becoming the centre of physics.

#### Step 1: a quantity that can be measured

:::scene Manchester, 1840s

At about the same time, in Manchester, the son of a brewer was measuring the same idea. James Joule let heavy weights fall on strings. The strings turned a paddle wheel sitting in a tub of water, and the paddle churned the water. Then he read a thermometer in the tub. The water was warmer. Not by much, but by a measurable amount, and the amount was always the same for the same fall.

:::fig c8_joule() | Joule's measurement, in outline. Falling weights turn a paddle in a tub of water, and a thermometer in the water climbs a little. The same falling always produces the same warming.

That is the key: falling and warming were *exchangeable at a fixed rate*, like two currencies. So much falling bought so much warming, every time. In today's units, warming one kilogram of water by one degree takes 4186 joules, which is the same energy as a 10 kg weight falling about 43 m. The warming from one fall in a laboratory is tiny, and Joule's thermometers had to be very good. They were.

:::work
**How far must a 10 kg weight fall to warm 1 kg of water by 1 °C?**
Energy needed: 4186 J.
Energy from the fall: mass × 9.8 × height = 10 × 9.8 × h.
So h = 4186 ÷ 98 = about 43 m. Joule needed many smaller falls, and a thermometer that could read hundredths of a degree.
:::

#### Step 2: the honeymoon

:::scene Chamonix, 1847

In the summer of 1847 Joule married Amelia Grimes and went to the Alps. Near Mont Blanc the couple met William Thomson, a young Scottish physicist who would later become Lord Kelvin and who had just heard Joule speak at a meeting in Oxford. The meeting is true, and the two men became lifelong allies.

The story goes further. Thomson, it is said, found Joule carrying a long thermometer, intending to measure whether the water at the bottom of a waterfall was warmer than at the top, since the falling water's energy had to go somewhere. It is a lovely story. Treat it as a legend: it surfaced about thirty-five years later, and no measurement was ever reported. What is real is the idea behind it, which Joule had already tested with his paddle: when something falls and stops, the energy of its fall does not vanish. It turns up as warmth.

#### Step 3: the blocks

The physicist Richard Feynman, a century later, gave a picture for this idea that is worth retelling in our own words.

A child has a set of wooden blocks, say twenty of them, and every night a parent counts them. Twenty, always twenty. One night there are only seventeen. The parent looks around and finds three under the sofa. Another night, eighteen, and the window is open: two are in the garden. Then one night nineteen, and none to be found anywhere, but the child's toy box is locked. So the parent weighs the box. It is heavier than an empty box by exactly the weight of one block. Another night the bathwater is murky and nobody can see into it, but the water level stands a little higher than usual: by just the volume of two blocks.

After a while the parent stops needing to see the blocks at all. The rule has become: the number is always twenty, and if you cannot see them, work out where they are hiding. Count the ones in view, add the weight of the box, add the rise of the bathwater, and the total is twenty, every single night.

Energy is the number of blocks. It cannot be seen directly. It is a number you *calculate*: so much for a thing's speed, so much for its height above the ground, so much for the stretch in a spring, so much for the jiggling of its particles. Add up the lot, and the total never changes. That is the law of ==conservation of energy==. When the total seems to have dropped, physicists do not shrug. They look for the locked box.

#### Step 4: stores and transfers

School gives these hiding places a name: ==energy stores==. A raised ball and the Earth together hold energy in a gravitational store. A moving ball holds energy in a kinetic store. Food and petrol hold it in a chemical store. A hot object holds it in a thermal store, the jiggling of its particles. When energy moves from one store to another, by a push, by an electric current, by heating, or by a wave such as light or sound, that is an ==energy transfer==. Joule's experiment, in those words: the gravitational store of the weight and the Earth drops, and the thermal store of the water rises by the same amount.

:::fig s_energy_bars() | A dropped ball, counted at four moments. At the top, all its energy is in the gravitational store. Halfway down, half has moved to the kinetic store. At the floor, all of it is kinetic. At the top of the first bounce, the ball is lower than it started, and the missing part is now the thermal store of the ball and floor, plus a little sound. The total bar is the same height in every column. The numbers are made up for a model, with a bounce that keeps about 70% of its height.

#### Step 5: where it goes

Here is the part people most often get wrong. Drop a ball and it bounces lower each time, then stops. It is tempting to say its energy has been used up. It has not. At each bounce the ball and the floor are squashed and warmed, and a click of sound spreads out into the room. The energy is all still there, as slightly faster jiggling in the floor, the ball and the air. It has spread out so thinly that it can never be gathered back into the ball. It is not destroyed; it is scattered, and scattered energy is less useful.

If you time the clicks of a bouncing ball with a phone, you can see this. Suppose one bounce lasts 0.60 s from click to click and the next 0.50 s. The height of a bounce goes with the square of its time, so the second bounce reached (0.50 ÷ 0.60)², which is about 69%, of the first one's height. About 31% of the ball's energy had moved into warmth and sound in one bounce.

:::careful
> "The ball stops bouncing because its energy ran out."

Energy never runs out; it moves. At every bounce some of the ball's energy becomes warmth in the ball and the floor, and a little becomes sound. After a dozen bounces all of it has spread into the room as slightly faster jiggling of particles. The total is unchanged. What has changed is that it is now spread so thinly that it cannot be gathered up to lift the ball again.
:::

:::home Warm hands
Rub your palms together hard for twenty seconds. They are warm. Nothing hot touched them. Your muscles did work against the friction between your hands, and that work became warmth in your skin, exactly as Joule's falling weights became warmth in his water. Now hold your palms still, pressed gently together, for twenty seconds. There is no sliding friction doing work between the hands, though your muscles still use energy and your skin may feel warm. Pressing without displacement does no mechanical work on the other hand; moving against friction does.
:::

Mayer saw it in blood, Joule in a thermometer, and neither used the word *energy* in the way we do now. The word came into its modern meaning over the following decades, as physicists realised that the same number kept its total through every change they could devise.
