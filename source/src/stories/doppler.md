:::scene Prague, 25 May 1842

Christian Doppler, thirty-eight years old, stood up before a handful of members of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences and read them a paper with a bold idea in it. Waves of any kind, he argued, should arrive at a different rate if their source is moving towards you or away from you. Towards you, they arrive more often, so a sound should seem higher. Away from you, less often, so lower.

For his best example he pointed at the sky. Double stars, pairs of stars circling each other, often show different colours, and Doppler thought their motion towards and away from us had tinted them. That example was wrong. Stars get their colours mostly from how hot they are, and a star's speed is far too small a fraction of the speed of light to shift its colour visibly. But the idea was right, and three years later somebody found a way to test it, with sound instead of starlight.

#### Step 1: a train of squeezes

Sound in air is a train of squeezes. A horn squeezes the air beside it, the squeeze shoves the next bit of air, and so on outward, and each squeeze travels at the speed of sound: about 349 metres a second in Mumbai's warm air (30 °C), a little less when it is cooler. A horn that makes 300 squeezes a second sends them out one after another, and if the horn is standing still they spread out evenly spaced all round it, like rings on a pond.

#### Step 2: the horn moves

Now let the horn move. Each squeeze still spreads out from the place where it was made, at the same speed as before; the air does not care how the horn is moving. But by the time the horn makes its next squeeze, it has moved on. So the squeezes ahead of the horn are made closer together, and the squeezes behind it are made farther apart. Their speed through the air is fixed; their spacing has changed; so the rate at which they arrive has changed too. Ahead of the horn they arrive more often, and you hear a higher note. Behind it they arrive less often, and you hear a lower one. This change in the note you hear, caused by motion, is the ==Doppler effect==.

:::fig c9_doppler() | Compressions spreading from a horn that is moving to the right. Each ring started where the horn was when it made that compression, so the rings are bunched ahead and spread out behind. The listener ahead receives them more often (a higher note) and the one behind less often (a lower note). The horn is drawn moving at about a third of the speed of sound, far faster than any train, so that the bunching shows.

:::scene Utrecht, 1845

Testing this needed something that could carry a steady note at speed, and in 1845 there was exactly one such thing: a railway. Christophorus Buys Ballot, a Dutch scientist, put horn players on an open wagon on the line between Utrecht and Maarssen, and had them hold one note while a locomotive pulled the wagon past musicians standing beside the track, people with perfect pitch who could name a note on hearing it. As the wagon came towards them, the note they heard was higher than the one being played. As it passed and drew away, it dropped below. Doppler was right, at least for sound.

Many textbooks say Doppler ran this experiment himself. He did not. It was Buys Ballot's, three years after Doppler's paper.

#### Step 3: how much does the note change?

:::work
**A horn at 150 Hz, sound at 340 m/s, the horn coming towards you at 35 m/s** (a fast car).
Coming: the squeezes are packed into a shorter distance, so more arrive each second:
150 × 340 ÷ (340 − 35) = 167 Hz.
Going: 150 × 340 ÷ (340 + 35) = 136 Hz.
The rise (+17 Hz) is a little bigger than the fall (−14 Hz). The effect is not symmetrical.
:::

Now a Mumbai local. Suppose its horn sounds at 300 Hz and it passes you at 80 km/h, which is 22 m/s, through warm air where sound travels at 349 m/s. (Both numbers are guesses; the sum is what matters.) Coming towards you the horn sounds at 320 Hz; going away, 282 Hz. The ratio is about 1.14, which on a piano is a drop of a little more than one whole tone as the train passes.

#### Step 4: what the driver hears

The driver hears the horn's true note, because the driver moves with it: each squeeze reaches the cab with the same spacing it was made with. Only motion between the source and the listener matters, which is why two passengers chatting on a moving train hear each other perfectly normally.

And the change is not a smooth slide. While the train is still far down the track, almost all of its motion is towards you, so the note sits steadily high. It drops quickly in the second or two as the train passes, then sits steadily low. If you have ever heard a passing horn as "neeeee-yowwww", that quick drop in the middle is the Doppler effect happening.

The same thing happens to light, as Doppler guessed, only the shifts are tiny unless the source is moving at a good fraction of the speed of light. Nothing on Earth does. Whole galaxies do, and in Chapter 15 the colour shift of their light, towards the red, tells us that they are moving away from us. Doppler's stars were the wrong example and the right idea.

:::careful
> "As the train comes closer the horn gets louder, so of course it sounds higher."

Louder and higher are different things. The horn does get louder as it nears you, because its sound has spread over a smaller area by the time it reaches you. The note rises for a different reason: the squeezes arrive more often. A horn standing still gets louder as you walk towards it and does not change its note at all.
:::

:::home The passing horn
From a safe place indoors, listen for a passing vehicle that holds one steady horn note as it comes and goes. Hum the note you hear while it is approaching, then the note after it has passed, and check the two hums against a tuner app on a phone. The second will be lower. For comparison, use the live model in Chapter 9: keep its horn still and change only its loudness. A real engine being revved changes its own pitch as well as its loudness, so it is not a fair test of motion alone. Please do not do this on a railway platform; the local train's horn is loud and the platform edge is no place for an experiment.
:::
