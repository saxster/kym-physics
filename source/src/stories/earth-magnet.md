:::scene London, 1600

Sailors in 1600 trusted their compass needles with their lives, and nobody could say why the needle pointed north. One thing many of them did believe was that garlic ruins a compass. William Gilbert, writing his book *De Magnete* in London, had heard that one and called it "fable and falsehood". He could afford to be blunt: the Italian scholar Giambattista della Porta had already breathed garlic all over a lodestone and found it worked exactly as before.

For the pointing itself, Gilbert did something cleverer than arguing. He had a lodestone, a naturally magnetic rock, ground into a sphere and called it a *terrella*: a little Earth. Then he moved a small magnetised needle over its surface and watched which way it tilted.

Over the terrella's "equator" the needle lay level. Moved towards either "pole", it tilted, one end dipping more and more steeply towards the stone. Sailors already knew that compass needles do the same on the real Earth: level near the equator, dipping as a ship sails towards the north. The little Earth copied the big one. So the big one, Gilbert concluded, is behaving like a giant version of his stone: the Earth itself is a magnet. No magic mountain at the top of the world, no garlic.

:::fig s_terrella() | Gilbert's terrella, drawn with the field it would make. A small needle lies level over the equator and dips more steeply towards the poles. Over Mumbai's latitude on the real Earth a free needle dips about 28° into the ground.

He also noticed, in the same book, that the amber kind of attraction (the story *Rubbing doesn't make charge* is about it) and the magnet's pull are different things. The magnet grabs iron and ignores paper; rubbed amber lifts paper and ignores iron. Four hundred years later that distinction still holds.

#### Step 1: what a compass is

Hang a bar magnet from a thread so that it can turn freely and it swings round until one end points roughly north. That end is called the magnet's north pole, and that is all a compass is: a small magnet free to turn. The name is a trap, though. Unlike poles attract, so if the north pole of your needle is pulled towards the far north, whatever is up there must be a *south* magnetic pole. It is: the magnetic pole near Earth's geographic North Pole is, as a magnet, a south pole. Confusion arises because people call it "the north magnetic pole", meaning the one in the north.

#### Step 2: the field

Lay a compass anywhere near a magnet and its needle settles in a definite direction. Move it a little and the direction changes a little. Join up those directions and you have drawn the magnet's field lines: the direction a compass's north end points at every place. Near a bar magnet the lines run out of its north end, round, and back into its south end, and continue through the magnet to make closed loops. Where the lines crowd together the field is strong; where they spread out it is weak.

Two facts about poles matter here. They always come in pairs: cut a magnet in half and you get two complete magnets, each with a north and a south, never a lone north. And a field line is a drawing of directions, not a track that anything travels along.

#### Step 3: the Earth's field in Mumbai

The Earth's field has a size and a direction at every place, and the direction is not along the ground. In Mumbai, according to the World Magnetic Model that navigation systems use, the field points almost exactly at true north (within a fraction of a degree) and dips about 28° downwards into the ground. Its total strength is about 43.6 microtesla, about 87% of the round "5 × 10⁻⁵ tesla" that textbooks quote for the Earth as a whole. Of that, the horizontal part, the part a compass needle can follow, is about 38 microtesla. At the old Alibag observatory down the coast the dip is 27°. Gilbert could not measure any of this; he could only see the dip. He had the shape right.

A phone's magnetometer (the free phyphox app shows it) will give you a rough version of these numbers, something like 40 to 45 microtesla in total. Phone sensors are uncalibrated and easily thrown by nearby steel, so treat the reading as a sketch, not a measurement.

#### Step 4: what we are not telling you

Gilbert thought the Earth was a magnet all the way through, like his stone. It is not that simple, and the sources this book is built from do not explain what really makes the Earth's field, so this story will not pretend to. The honest sentence is: the Earth *acts like* a huge magnet, with its magnetic south pole in the far north. What makes it act that way belongs to a later book.

:::careful
> "The Earth's North Pole is a magnet's north pole."

As a magnet, the pole in the north is a south pole. That is why the north end of every compass needle is pulled towards it: unlike poles attract. "Magnetic north" means the place a compass points to, not the kind of pole that sits there.
:::

:::careful
> "A compass needle points along the ground towards the north."

The field it is following dips into the ground, by about 28° in Mumbai. A compass needle is pivoted so that it can only turn in the flat, so it shows you the horizontal part of the field and hides the dip. Gilbert's needle, free to tilt, showed both.
:::

:::home A compass on a leaf
Stroke a steel sewing needle about twenty times with a fridge magnet, always in the same direction, lifting the magnet clear between strokes. Lay the needle gently on a small leaf floating in a bowl of water, away from anything made of steel, and wait. It turns and settles pointing north–south. Turn the leaf and let go: it comes back. Handle the needle with care.
:::

Mumbai has its own chapter in this story. From 1841 an observatory at Colaba measured the Earth's field, hourly from 1846, and did so for sixty years, until the city's new electric trams threatened to swamp the readings and the instruments were moved down the coast to Alibag. That tale is told in *Chased out of Bombay by the trams*. In Chapter 13 you will float the needle, read the phone's magnetometer, and then meet the surprise Gilbert never saw: a current in a wire can move a compass too.
