:::scene Kusumapura (Patna), 499 CE

In the year 499, in Kusumapura, near today's Patna, a young astronomer named Aryabhata finished a short book of mathematics and astronomy. He was about twenty-three. The book is called the ==Aryabhatiya== after him, and scholars still argue about its exact date; some put it nearer 510. One of its claims was so far ahead of its time that people went on arguing against it for a thousand years.

The stars, he wrote, only seem to move. Just as a person in a moving boat sees the trees on the bank slide backwards, the stars seem to drift westward across the sky each night because the Earth turns beneath them.

#### Step 1: the boat

Think about the boat first, because it is the whole argument. Sit in a boat moving steadily down a river and look at the trees on the bank. They glide backwards past you. Nothing is happening to the trees. The motion is yours, and your eye has handed it to the shore.

Aryabhata's point was that the night sky is the bank. The stars rise in the east, wheel overhead and set in the west because the ground under your feet is turning the other way, towards the east, carrying you with it.

#### Step 2: you cannot tell by looking

Here is why the argument lasted a thousand years. Watch the stars rise and set, as carefully as you like, and you cannot tell which of two things is happening: a huge sky turning around a still Earth once a day, or a still sky with a small Earth turning inside it. The two pictures predict exactly the same view. Seeing is not enough. You need an experiment that only one of the two pictures can pass.

Aryabhata chose the turning Earth. Most astronomers came round to the same view only in the 1600s, and the experiment that settled it did not arrive until 1851.

:::fig s_aryabhata_boat() | Left: from a moving boat the trees on the bank seem to slide backwards, though it is the boat that moves. Right: from a turning Earth the stars seem to drift west, though it is the ground that moves east, carrying you with it. The two views look identical from inside; that is why the question stayed open for so long.

#### Step 3: why you don't feel it

If Earth turns once a day, Mumbai is moving fast. Work it out: Mumbai's circle of latitude is about 37,800 km round, and the city travels the whole of it in 24 hours.

:::work
Distance Mumbai travels in a day: 2 × π × 6,371 km × cos(19.08°) ≈ 37,800 km.
Speed: 37,800 km ÷ 24 h ≈ 1,580 km/h, which is about 440 metres every second.
:::

Nobody feels it. That is not because the number is wrong but because of what you learned in Chapter 2: you never feel steady motion, only changes in it. On a local train at full speed you can pour a drink; it is the braking that throws everyone forward. Earth's spin is the steadiest motion there is, and the only "change" in it is the gentle bending of your path round the axis once a day. That bending takes a pull of about 0.03 m/s², a third of one per cent of gravity. No bathroom scale in Mumbai is sensitive enough to notice it.

#### Step 4: shadows, not demons

Aryabhata made a second claim in the same book, and it was just as bold. An eclipse of the Moon, he said, is the Earth's shadow falling on the Moon; an eclipse of the Sun is the Moon's shadow falling on the Earth. The stories of his time blamed a demon that swallowed the Sun and the Moon. Aryabhata replaced the demon with geometry, and the geometry is the one we still use. The Moon-phases story works through it.

#### Step 5: the proof, 1,352 years later

The experiment that only a turning Earth can pass is a pendulum. In 1851 Léon Foucault hung a heavy bob, about 28 kg, from a wire about 67 m long under the dome of the Panthéon in Paris and set it swinging. A pendulum keeps swinging in the same plane; nothing about a swinging weight can make it turn. Yet over the hours the line of its swing drifted slowly round the floor. The pendulum was not turning. The floor was, and Paris with it. The newspapers asked their readers, "Have you seen the Earth turn?" That story has its own page in this book.

Two cautions, because websites exaggerate Aryabhata. He did not put the Sun at the centre of the universe, whatever you may read. And the suspiciously exact "accuracy" percentages quoted for his astronomy online have no good source behind them. What he did say was narrower and still remarkable: the ground is moving, and the sky is not.

:::careful
> "If the ground were really moving at 1,500 km/h we'd feel it, or be flung off."

You feel changes in motion, never motion itself; that was Chapter 2's whole point. Earth's turn is perfectly steady, and the bending it gives your path is about a third of one per cent of gravity. Gravity wins by a factor of three hundred, and nobody is going anywhere.
:::

:::careful
> "The Sun goes round us. I can see it move across the sky."

You can see that the Sun and the ground change their angle to each other. That is all seeing can tell you. The trees seem to move past the boat too. The pendulum, not the eye, decided which one is turning.
:::

:::home The creeping shadow
On a sunny morning stand a pencil upright in a lump of clay, or push it through a bottle cap, on a balcony or windowsill that gets sun for a couple of hours. Every fifteen minutes, mark where the tip of its shadow falls. The marks creep along in a line. The Sun crosses the sky at fifteen degrees an hour, so in two hours it has moved thirty degrees, and your pencil's shadow has swung round to show it. Then ask Aryabhata's question: what moved, the Sun or the floor? Never look directly at the Sun.
:::
