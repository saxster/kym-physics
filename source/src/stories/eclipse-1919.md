:::scene Príncipe, an island off West Africa, 29 May 1919

It rained all morning. Arthur Eddington and his companion Edwin Cottingham had sailed from England, set up a telescope on a plantation on the island of Príncipe, and waited weeks for an eclipse that was due at two in the afternoon. Now the sky was full of cloud. When the Moon's shadow arrived, the Sun showed through gaps, and Eddington took sixteen photographs of whatever he could see, hoping that a few stars had come out on the plates beside the blacked-out Sun.

He was not there to look at the eclipse. He was there to see whether the Sun bends light.

#### The prediction

In 1915 Einstein had finished a new theory of gravity, and it made a prediction nobody could check in a laboratory. A ray of starlight passing close to the Sun should be bent by the Sun's pull, by 1.75 seconds of arc for a star right at the Sun's edge. The older way of reckoning, treating light as something that falls like a stone under Newton's gravity, gave about half that. A test had to tell 1.75 from 0.87, and that is a very small angle.

:::work
**How small is 1.75 seconds of arc?** A degree has 60 minutes, a minute 60 seconds, so 1.75 seconds is 1.75 ÷ 3600 of a degree, or 0.000 008 5 in the units mathematicians use for angles (radians).
A one-rupee coin is about 22 mm across. It looks 1.75 seconds wide from 0.022 ÷ 0.000 008 5 = 2,600 m away: a coin seen from two and a half kilometres.
The Sun itself looks about 1,900 seconds wide, so the shift to be measured was about a thousandth of the Sun's own width.
:::

Stars next to the Sun can be photographed only when the Sun is blotted out, so the test needed a total eclipse, and 29 May 1919 was a good one: the Sun would sit in front of the Hyades, a cluster of bright stars. Frank Dyson, the Astronomer Royal, organised two expeditions. Eddington and Cottingham went to Príncipe. Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson went to Sobral, in northern Brazil, where they had a clear sky, 5 minutes and 13 seconds of totality, and two instruments: a large telescope fed by a mirror, and a small 4-inch telescope brought as a backup.

:::fig s_eclipse_bend() | The test. Starlight grazing the Sun is bent towards it, so during an eclipse the star appears shifted outwards from its true place. Einstein's theory predicted a shift of 1.75 arcseconds at the Sun's edge; the older reckoning gave about half.

#### Three answers

Back home, the plates were measured against photographs of the same stars taken months later with the Sun elsewhere. Príncipe, through its clouds, yielded two usable plates with five stars each and a shift of 1.61 arcseconds. Sobral's small backup telescope gave the clearest pictures of all: 1.98 arcseconds. Sobral's main telescope gave 0.93, close to the old prediction, but its images were blurred; the heat of the day had distorted the mirror and thrown the stars out of focus. Dyson's team set that result aside, for reasons they described in their report, and kept the other two.

On 6 November 1919, at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society in London, the result was announced: the light bends, and it bends by Einstein's amount, not Newton's. The newspapers took it up within days. Einstein, until then known mainly to physicists, became the most famous scientist in the world.

#### The argument, sixty years on

In 1980 two historians of science, John Earman and Clark Glymour, looked again at the three numbers and made an accusation. The expedition had thrown away the one measurement that disagreed with Einstein and kept the two that agreed. Eddington, they noted, had believed in the theory before he sailed. The charge spread, into a popular book, into Stephen Hawking's *A Brief History of Time*, which called the result "sheer luck, or a case of knowing the result they wanted to get", and into a thousand classrooms.

Then people checked. In 1978 and 1979 staff at the Royal Greenwich Observatory re-measured the surviving Sobral plates with modern machines. The blurred plates that had been set aside gave 1.55 ± 0.34 arcseconds, in agreement with Einstein after all; the blurring had spoiled the original measurement, not the sky. The physicist Daniel Kennefick then went through the expedition's own papers and found that the decision to drop the blurred plates was Dyson's, not Eddington's, and Dyson was cautious about Einstein's theory and had no reason to tilt the result. Eddington, analysing separately, refused Dyson's later suggestion to average all three numbers, which would have let the suspect plates in. Another physicist, Clifford Will, put it simply: in Eddington's place, "I would have done the same thing".

So the plates were dropped for a reason that was written down before anyone knew whether it mattered, which is the only honest way to drop data. Later measurements, with better instruments, have confirmed the bending many times since. And the argument itself is part of the story: the Royal Society's motto is *nullius in verba*, "take nobody's word for it", and that applies to Eddington, to Earman and Glymour, and to this page.

:::careful
> "The 1919 eclipse proved Einstein right."

One measurement never proves a theory. The eclipse agreed with Einstein's number and disagreed with the old one, within its errors, and the suspect plates, re-measured, agreed too. What turned a result into knowledge was repetition by other people with other instruments over the following decades.
:::
