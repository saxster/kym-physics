:::scene Princeton, New Jersey, 18 April 1955

Albert Einstein died in hospital in Princeton on 18 April 1955, at the age of 76. He had asked to be cremated, and he was. But the pathologist who examined his body that morning, Thomas Harvey, removed the brain first and kept it. He had no permission to do so, from Einstein or from his family. When the family learned what had happened, Einstein's son Hans Albert agreed, reluctantly, after the fact, on one condition: the brain must be used only for science, and anything learned from it must be published in proper scientific journals.

#### What Harvey did

Harvey weighed the brain, 1,230 grams, which is an ordinary size, photographed it from every side, preserved it, and divided it into about 240 small blocks so that thin slices could be put on microscope slides. He sent slides to a few scientists he hoped would study them. Then, for the most part, nothing happened. Harvey moved house several times across the United States, and the blocks went with him, in two large glass jars packed in a cardboard box that had once held cider.

In 1978, twenty-three years later, a young journalist named Steven Levy tracked Harvey down and found the box. In 1997 Harvey drove the brain across America in the boot of a car with a writer, Michael Paterniti, who wrote a book about the journey. In 1998, forty-three years after the death, Harvey gave what remained to the pathology department of the hospital in Princeton where it had started. His heirs later passed the rest, and fourteen never-published photographs, to a medical museum in the United States, and a museum in Philadelphia shows thin slices of it on microscope slides.

#### What the scientists found

Every few years a study of Einstein's brain makes the news. In 1985 a researcher reported that one small region had more supporting cells per nerve cell than in eleven other brains she compared it with. In 1999 a team working from Harvey's photographs said one of the lobes was unusually shaped. In 2012 another team found extra folds in the frontal lobe. In 2013 another reported a thicker band of fibres joining the two halves.

Each of these has critics, and the criticisms are worth more to you than the findings, because they are the same criticisms you will learn to make of any claim. The eleven brains in the first study came from people whose average age was 64; Einstein was 76. Nobody measured the slides without knowing whose they were. And the deepest objection, made by the psychologist Terence Hines, is about counting. Every brain has features that are unusual in some way, because every brain is different. If you have one brain and you know in advance that its owner was a genius, you will find something unusual in it, and you will remember the unusual things that fit your story and forget the ones that do not. To learn what makes a genius you would need many brains of geniuses and many brains of everyone else, measured by people who do not know which is which. That study has never been done, and one brain in two jars cannot stand in for it.

:::careful
> "There must be something special in Einstein's brain. That is where the genius was."

Perhaps, but no one has shown it, and a single brain cannot. This is the lesson of Chapter 0 in its hardest form: an observation only means something against a model of what you expected to see, and with a sample of one, you can fit any model you like. The honest statement is that his brain was an ordinary weight and that studies of it disagree with one another.
:::

#### Where the physics was

What made Einstein Einstein is not in the jars. In 1905, as a 26-year-old clerk in a patent office in Bern, he published four papers in one year: that light comes in packets, which won him the Nobel Prize and which you meet in Chapter 15; that the jiggling of pollen grains in water proves atoms are real; that space and time are not what they seem; and that mass and energy are two forms of one thing, which is the fire inside the Sun. In 1919 his prediction that the Sun bends starlight was tested at an eclipse, and that tale is told on its own page.

None of that came from an unusual fold. It came from a habit of taking a simple question seriously for years: what would you see if you rode beside a beam of light? The hospital kept the organ. The method is in his papers, which anyone can read, and in the chapters that follow.

:::careful
> "Harvey was a villain who stole the brain."

He took it without permission, and that was wrong by the rules of 1955 as much as by today's, when nothing like it could happen without consent in advance. But he was not a thief who sold it. He kept it for forty years, gave slides to anyone who asked seriously, and handed it back. The story is a lesson in consent, not a crime thriller.
:::
