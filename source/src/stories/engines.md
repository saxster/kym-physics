:::scene Paris, 1824

In 1824 a young engineer named Sadi Carnot paid a Paris printer to run off six hundred copies of a booklet, 118 pages long, about the engines that were changing the world. Across the Channel, British steam engines were pumping water out of mines and driving mills. They burned coal and did work. Carnot asked a question nobody had asked clearly: for a given amount of burning, how much work can an engine possibly do? Could a cleverer design go on getting more and more, without limit?

Almost nobody bought the booklet. It was a decade before other engineers and physicists picked it up, and when they did, it became the foundation of the science of heat and work.

#### Step 1: a picture borrowed from water

Carnot's father, Lazare, had studied water engines: the mill wheels that turned when a stream fell over them. A water wheel does work because water falls from a higher level to a lower one, and the work it can do depends on how much water falls and how far. The son, as historians summarise his thinking, saw a ==heat engine== the same way. Heat flows from something hot, the boiler, to something cold, the air or the river outside, and on the way down some of it can be made to do work, as falling water turns a wheel. The words are the historians'; the picture is his.

#### Step 2: the water has to arrive at the bottom

The picture has a consequence that is easy to miss. The water that turns a mill wheel does not vanish into the wheel. It arrives at the bottom, every drop of it, and flows away. If the heat in an engine is like falling water, then the heat must *arrive* at the cold side too. An engine cannot turn all of its heat into work. There must always be a cold place for some of the energy to go.

This is exactly what you see at a power station. The great cooling towers are not a fault in the design. They are the bottom of the waterfall, where the energy that did not become electricity is handed to the air.

#### Step 3: how far the heat falls

Carnot's conclusion, in today's words, was that the most work an engine can possibly get from a given flow of heat depends on how far the heat falls: on how hot the hot side is and how cold the cold side is. It does not depend on what the engine is made of or what fluid runs round inside it. A bigger temperature difference allows more work; a small one allows very little. That is a limit no cleverness can beat, and it was the first such limit anyone had found.

#### Step 4: a number for how good

The word for how much of the input becomes useful output is ==efficiency==: useful energy out, divided by energy in. A ==steam engine== of the kind Carnot knew manages about 17%. A petrol engine manages about 30%, a diesel about 35%, a coal-fired power station about 42%. An electric motor, which is not a heat engine at all and has no waterfall inside it, manages about 98%. These are representative figures; real machines vary.

:::work
**A steam engine, for every 100 J the fire gives**
Useful work out: 17 J.
Handed to the cold side as warmth in the air and water: 100 − 17 = 83 J.
Efficiency: 17 ÷ 100 = 17%.
For a coal power station at 42%: 42 J become electricity, 58 J warm the surroundings.
:::

#### Step 5: nothing is destroyed

Read the 83 J carefully. They are not destroyed; energy never is. They are still energy, now spread out as slightly faster jiggling in the air and the river, so thinly that no engine could gather them up again. That is the second great fact about energy, standing beside the first. The first says the total never changes. The second says that whenever energy is turned into a useful form, some of it always ends up spread out as warmth. Carnot found the second fact before anyone had properly stated the first.

:::careful
> "An engine that is 17% efficient destroys the other 83% of the energy."

It destroys nothing. The 83 J leave as warmth in the surroundings: the hot exhaust, the warm river, the steam from the cooling tower. The total is still 100 J. What has changed is that 83 J are now spread so thinly through the air that they can never again be made to turn a wheel. Efficiency measures how much stayed useful, not how much survived.
:::

:::home The bottom of the waterfall
After a laptop has been used normally, notice the warm air near its accessible vent, keeping the vent clear. There is no need to reach behind an appliance or go near a mixer or any moving part. Useful work and warmth leaving the machine can happen together. Look round your flat and name three machines that pass some energy to their surroundings this way: each has its own small version of a cooling tower.
:::
