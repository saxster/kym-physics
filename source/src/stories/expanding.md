:::scene Mount Wilson, California, 1929

Through the 1920s Edwin Hubble, at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, did two things to galaxy after galaxy. He measured how far away each one was, using Henrietta Leavitt's blinking Cepheids as his measuring tape. And, with his colleague Milton Humason, he measured the speed at which each was moving towards or away from us, using a trick that Vesto Slipher had been using since 1912. Slipher had already found something odd: nearly every spiral galaxy he looked at was rushing *away*, some at up to 1,800 kilometres a second.

In 1929 Hubble put the two measurements side by side, and in 1931 he and Humason extended the result to fainter galaxies. The pattern was plain. The farther away a galaxy was, the faster it was receding, and in proportion: twice as far, twice as fast. Written as a rule, now called Hubble's law, the speed v is the distance d multiplied by a constant:

:::eq
**v = H × d**
:::

Measurements since have converged on H close to 22 kilometres per second for every million light-years of distance. A galaxy 100 million light-years away is receding at about 2,200 kilometres a second.

#### Step 1: how you measure a galaxy's speed

The trick is in the light. Every element leaves its fingerprint of lines in a spectrum, as the story *What the stars are made of* explains, and the lines sit at exactly known colours. In the light of a receding galaxy, every line has moved towards the red end, to a longer wavelength than it has in a laboratory. That shift is called redshift, and the faster the galaxy recedes, the bigger it is. The first picture to reach for is the Doppler effect you can hear from a passing train horn: the pitch drops as the source moves away. For a nearby galaxy that picture works well enough. Keep reading, because for the universe as a whole there is a better one.

#### Step 2: ants on a ruler

Picture a rubber ruler with ants standing on it at the 2 cm, 4 cm, 7 cm and 12 cm marks. You are the ant at 2 cm. In one minute the ruler is stretched to twice its length, so every mark moves to double its number: your three neighbours are now at 8, 14 and 24 cm, and you are at 4.

:::fig s_ruler_ants() | A rubber ruler stretched to double its length in one minute. Seen from the ant at 2 cm, the ants that were 2, 5 and 10 cm away have moved 2, 5 and 10 cm farther in that minute: the farther away an ant, the faster it recedes. The same is true from any ant, so there is no centre.

Measure from where you stand. The ant that was 2 cm away is now 4 cm away: it moved 2 cm in the minute. The one that was 5 cm away is now 10 cm away: it moved 5 cm. The one that was 10 cm away moved 10 cm. Speed in proportion to distance, exactly Hubble's law. Now be the ant at 7 cm instead and do the sums again: the ants that were 5, 3 and 5 cm away moved 5, 3 and 5 cm. Every ant sees every other ant receding at a speed proportional to its distance, and every ant could claim to be the one standing still. None of them is at the centre, and none of them walked anywhere. The stretching of the ruler moved them apart.

#### Step 3: what stretches and what does not

That is the better picture of the universe. The galaxies are not flying apart *through* space like shrapnel; the space between them is growing, and they are carried along, passive participants in the expansion. Inside a galaxy, gravity holds the stars together, so the galaxy itself does not grow, just as the raisins in a rising loaf stay the same size while the dough between them swells. You, Mumbai and the Milky Way are not stretching.

Light is. A wave of light setting out from a distant galaxy travels for millions of years through space that is stretching as it goes, and the wave is stretched with it. By the time it arrives, every wavelength is longer than when it left: redshifted. For nearby galaxies this gives the same answer as the Doppler picture; for the farthest ones only the stretching picture gives the right one. Astronomers describe the amount by the fraction a wavelength has grown, written z.

#### Step 4: running it backwards

If everything is moving apart now, everything was closer together before. Run the stretching backwards, far enough, and all the matter we can see was packed together at enormous density and temperature. That beginning is called the Big Bang, and it is the most misdrawn event in physics. It was not an explosion at a spot in space, with galaxies flying out of it into emptiness. It happened everywhere at once, because *everywhere* was what was being stretched. There is no location you can visit where the stretching of space began. Every point, including the one you are sitting on, was once in the thick of it.

How long ago? Divide a distance by the speed and you get a time, so one over Hubble's constant is a rough age for the universe, and it comes out at about 13.6 billion years. The careful figure, allowing for the stretching having slowed and then sped up over its history, is 13.8 billion years, give or take about a hundred million.

:::school
Your IGCSE syllabus says that distant galaxies are "receding" and that redshift "supports the Big Bang Theory", and it may say that all the matter in the universe was once "present at a single point". A careful way to picture it is space stretching between the galaxies, with the whole of space once squeezed far closer together, everywhere. The syllabus also writes Hubble's law as H₀ = v/d; the 22 kilometres per second per million light-years above is about 72 kilometres per second per megaparsec in the units it uses.
:::

:::careful
> "The Big Bang was an explosion in space, and we can point to where it happened."

There is no such place. The stretching began everywhere at once, and every galaxy, ours included, sees all the others receding. The ruler had no centre either.
:::

:::careful
> "Everything is moving away from us, so we must be at the centre."

Every ant on the ruler thought the same, and each was equally right and equally wrong. Speed in proportion to distance is exactly the pattern that looks the same from everywhere.
:::

:::home A universe on a balloon
Draw a dozen dots on a balloon with a marker, and one short wavy line. Blow it up slowly. Every dot moves away from every other dot, and the dots farthest apart separate fastest, while each dot itself stays small. The wavy line stretches as the rubber does: that is the redshift. Pick any dot and call it home; the view is the same from all of them. The balloon has a centre, inside it, where there is no rubber at all; the universe does not have even that.
:::
