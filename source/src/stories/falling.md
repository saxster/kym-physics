:::scene Delft, 1586

Two men, Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot, have climbed about thirty feet, some nine metres, up the tower of the Nieuwe Kerk in Delft, in the Netherlands, carrying two lead balls, one of them ten times heavier than the other. On the ground below lies a wooden board. They let the two balls go at the same instant and listen. Aristotle, whose physics had ruled for nearly two thousand years, said that heavier things fall faster, in proportion to their weight, so the heavy ball should land while the light one is still a tenth of the way down, and the board should sound twice. It sounds once. One thud.

Stevin, a mathematician, published the result in 1586. Galileo Galilei would not arrive in Pisa, with its leaning tower, for another three years.

#### Step 1: Aristotle's prediction

It is worth seeing why Aristotle's rule was believed for so long: it agrees with what you see. Drop a stone and a feather together and the stone wins easily. Drop a coin and a sheet of paper and the coin wins. Everyday falling really does sort things by weight, and Aristotle made the pattern into a law. Ten times the weight, ten times the speed. The belief has not gone away. When 478 university students in the United States were tested in 1985, 82 per cent believed that what a thing is made of affects how it falls even with the air taken away.

:::fig c4_two_predictions() | Delft, 1586. Left: what Aristotle's rule predicts for two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, dropped about nine metres onto a board: the light ball should still be near the top when the heavy one lands. Right: what Stevin and de Groot heard. One thud.

#### Step 2: Philoponus tries it

Aristotle was not the last word even in the ancient world. In Alexandria in the sixth century, John Philoponus wrote that if you drop two very different weights from the same height, the difference in the time they take is tiny, nothing like the difference in their weights. He had, it seems, tried it. The observation did not catch on, and the rule carried on for another thousand years.

#### Step 3: Why one thud settles it

:::work
**The Delft drop in numbers.** The height is about 30 feet, which is 9.1 m. A ball dropped from rest falls a height h in a time √(2h ÷ g), with g = 9.8 m/s².
√(2 × 9.1 ÷ 9.8) = 1.37 s. If Stevin and de Groot were right, both balls hit the board 1.37 s after release.
If Aristotle were right, the light ball would fall at a tenth of the speed and take ten times as long: about 13.7 s. It would still be hanging near the top of the drop when the heavy one landed.
Nobody needs a stopwatch to tell one thud from two thuds twelve seconds apart. That is what makes the test decisive: the two predictions are not close.
:::

#### Step 4: The tower that probably never was

The famous version of this story has Galileo dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa. The only source for it is his late-life secretary Viviani, writing long afterwards; Galileo himself never described doing it. Most historians think it was a thought experiment or, at most, a private test. The real drop, with real lead and a real board, happened in Delft, three years before Galileo reached Pisa, and it is Stevin's thud that this story is named for.

#### Step 5: Why the feather still loses

So why does a feather lose to a stone, every time? Because of the air. Anything moving through air is pushed backward by it, and the push, called ==air resistance== or drag, grows as the thing speeds up: roughly four times the drag at twice the speed. A falling object has two forces on it: its weight, pulling down and never changing, and drag, pushing up and growing. The moment they are equal, the net force is zero and the speed stops changing. That steady speed is the ==terminal velocity==, or terminal speed, and it is different for different things. A spread-out, light thing like a feather or a flat sheet of paper reaches it almost at once, at a crawl. A dense, compact thing like a lead ball would have to fall a long way before its drag caught up with its weight.

That is why the test is weight *against* drag, not weight alone. Crumple a sheet of paper into a tight ball and drop it beside a flat sheet of the same paper. Same weight; the ball lands first, because it presents far less to the air. A raindrop about 2 mm across falls at roughly 6.5 m/s and no faster; the biggest drops top out near 10 m/s. A skydiver falling head-down reaches about 350 km/h. In every case the limit is set by the balance of weight and drag. With no air there is no drag, no limit and no sorting by weight. Physicists call falling with nothing but gravity acting ==free fall==, and in free fall everything falls alike.

#### Step 6: A hammer and a feather on the Moon

:::scene Hadley Rille, the Moon, 2 August 1971

The cleanest version of Stevin's experiment was done where there is no air at all. At the end of the last moonwalk of Apollo 15, the astronaut David Scott held out a geological hammer of 1.32 kg in one hand and a falcon's feather in the other, about 1.6 m above the ground, and let go of both together, for the television cameras. They landed together. "How about that! Mr Galileo was correct in his findings," he said.

The Moon pulls about a sixth as hard as the Earth, so the fall was slow: about 1.38 s from 1.6 m, against 0.57 s for the same drop on Earth. But slow for both, and together.

:::fig c4_moon_strobe() | A hammer dropped from 1.6 m, drawn every 0.2 s, on Earth and on the Moon. On Earth it lands in 0.57 s, and a feather dropped beside it would lag far behind in the air. On the Moon, where g is 1.67 m/s², the hammer and the feather fall side by side and land together after 1.38 s.

#### Step 7: Why it is true

Stevin showed *that* heavy and light fall together. The *why* took another hundred years and belongs to Newton, and it is one of the neatest cancellations in physics. The Earth pulls a heavier ball harder: ten times the mass, ten times the pull. But a heavier ball is also harder to get moving: ten times the mass, ten times the sluggishness. The two effects are both in proportion to the mass, so they cancel exactly, and every mass speeds up at the same rate. Chapter 3 gives you the second half of that argument and Chapter 5 the first.

:::careful
> "Heavy and light things always fall together."

Only when air is out of the way. In air, a tennis ball dropped with a cricket ball lands a little later, because its drag is a bigger share of its weight. The true rule is: with nothing but gravity acting, everything falls alike. Air adds a second force that depends on size and shape, and that is what sorts the feather from the stone.
:::

:::careful
> "The feather fell slowly on the Moon because there's no air to hold it up, and there's hardly any gravity there."

Air never holds a feather *up*; on Earth it slows the feather down. On the Moon the feather fell slowly for the same reason the hammer did: the Moon's pull is about a sixth of the Earth's. There is gravity on the Moon, which is why both of them fell at all.
:::

Hold a ₹1 coin and a ₹5 coin side by side at the same height over a steel thali and let go together. Listen for one clink, not two. Then drop a flat sheet of A4 paper beside a sheet crumpled into a tight ball. Then crumple two sheets together into one ball and race it against a single-sheet ball: twice the weight, nearly the same size. Which races are decided by weight, and which by air? Try the coins again from a chair, with an adult steadying it, and listen harder.
