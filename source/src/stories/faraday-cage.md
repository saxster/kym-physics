:::scene The Royal Institution, London, 1836

By 1836 Michael Faraday had already built the first electric motor and discovered how to make electricity from magnetism. Now he wanted an answer to a simpler-sounding question. When you put electric charge on a hollow metal object, where does the charge go, and does anything at all happen inside?

He did not do it with a tin can. He built a cube twelve feet along each side, about 3.7 metres, a wooden frame covered all over with metal foil, big enough to stand in. He connected the outside to an electrostatic generator, a machine that builds up charge by friction, and charged the cube so strongly that sparks jumped from its surface. Then he went inside, carrying an electroscope, a little instrument with two thin gold leaves that spring apart when the slightest charge reaches them. He walked round inside his charged box, holding the electroscope to the walls, the corners, the air in the middle. The leaves never moved. Outside, sparks; inside, as he put it, not "the least influence".

#### Why the inside is quiet

A metal is a material in which some of the electric charge is free to move. Put extra charge on a lump of metal and the charges push each other apart, as like charges do, and spread out as far from one another as they can get, which is over the outside surface.

Now ask what would happen if there were any electric push left inside the hollow. The free charges in the metal would feel it and move. They keep moving, rearranging themselves on the surface, until there is nothing left to move them, and that happens only when the inside is completely quiet. So the charges always end up arranged in exactly the pattern that cancels every push inside. It is not that the metal "blocks" anything. The charges on the shell build a push of their own that undoes the one from outside, wherever it comes from and however strong it is. That is a ==Faraday cage==, and any closed metal shell is one.

:::fig s_faraday_cube() | Faraday's foil cube. The charge sits on the outside surface and rearranges itself until the inside is free of any electric push, so the gold leaves of the electroscope hang straight.

#### Cages you have been inside

A car is a Faraday cage with wheels. If lightning strikes it, the charge runs round the metal body and down to the ground, and the people inside, surrounded by metal, feel nothing. The same is true of an aeroplane, and aeroplanes do get struck. Mumbai's monsoon thunderstorms are a good moment to remember that a hard-topped car is one of the safer places to be, and an open field one of the worst.

A microwave oven is a cage turned inside out: the box keeps the microwaves in, and the mesh in the glass door is a wall to them even though you can see through it. The bags that computer parts are shipped in are flimsy Faraday cages against stray charge. Hospital scanner rooms are lined with metal so that radio noise from outside cannot get in.

:::home The phone in the foil
Wrap a phone completely in two layers of kitchen foil, with no gaps, and ask someone to call it from another phone. It does not ring. Radio waves, which are made of the same electric and magnetic pushes Faraday was studying, cannot reach the antenna through a closed metal shell. Unwrap it and it rings. The foil is not thick, and it does not need to be: what matters is that the shell is closed.
:::

:::careful
> "The metal blocks the electricity, like a wall blocks rain."

Nothing is blocked. Charge on the metal shell moves about until its own push exactly cancels the outside push everywhere inside. Faraday did not find a weakened effect inside his cube; he found none. A wall against rain always leaks a little. This does not, because the cancellation is not a barrier but a balance.
:::

Faraday himself was the least cage-like of men. He had little schooling, taught himself from the books he bound as a bookbinder's apprentice, and became the greatest experimenter of his century. Sitting inside a box he had charged to spark-point, watching two gold leaves not move, is exactly the kind of thing he found delightful.
