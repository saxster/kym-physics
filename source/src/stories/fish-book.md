:::scene The Royal Society, London, January 1684

In January 1684 the young astronomer Edmond Halley showed the Royal Society a neat piece of reasoning. Kepler had found, sixty-five years earlier, that the planets farther from the Sun take longer to go round in a particular way. Halley showed that this pattern follows if the Sun pulls each planet with a force that weakens with the square of the distance: four times as far, a quarter of the pull.

Three of the Society's members, Halley, Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke, then asked the harder question. If the force is like that, what path would a planet follow? Would it be the ellipse that Kepler had found? Nobody could prove it. Wren offered a prize, a book worth forty shillings, to whoever could. Hooke said he already had the proof. Two months went by and he produced nothing.

#### The ride to Cambridge

In August 1684 Halley rode to Cambridge to put the question to Isaac Newton. What curve would the planet follow? An ellipse, said Newton. He had worked it out years before. He looked for the paper and could not find it. He promised to send the proof.

Three months later a nine-page treatise in Latin arrived, *De Motu*, "On Motion". Halley saw at once what it was and asked for more. Newton, who disliked appearing in print, expanded the nine pages over the next eighteen months into three volumes. The Society agreed to publish. Then it looked in its accounts.

#### The fish

The Royal Society had just spent its money on a book about fish. *De Historia Piscium*, "The History of Fishes", had been begun by the naturalist Francis Willughby, who died in 1672, and finished by his friend John Ray. It was a serious work of natural history and a very expensive one to print: five hundred copies had cost the Society £400, which comes to sixteen shillings a copy. And it did not sell. The Society was broke.

So Halley paid for the *Principia* himself. He was not a rich man. He paid the printer, corrected the proofs, and put his own work aside to push the printing through. The Society, which owed Halley his salary as its clerk, settled what it owed him and Hooke in copies of the fish book instead of money. Later, when Halley was asked to measure the length of a degree of the Earth, the Society voted him "£50 or fifty Books of Fishes". In the end his costs were paid back from the sales, because the *Principia*, unlike the fish, sold.

:::work
**Prize money and fish money.** Wren's prize was a book worth 40 shillings. A copy of the fish book had cost the Society 400 pounds ÷ 500 copies = 0.8 pounds, and there are 20 shillings in a pound, so 16 shillings.
So the prize for proving the ellipse would have bought two and a half fish books. Newton's proof, when it came, filled three volumes and was paid for by the man who asked the question.
:::

#### What was in the book

*Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, "The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy", came out in 1687. In it are the three laws of motion you meet in Chapters 2 and 3: a moving thing keeps moving unless a force changes its motion; a force changes motion in proportion to its size; every push has a partner push back. And in it is the rule that a single pull, weakening with the square of the distance, drops an apple, holds the Moon in its orbit and steers every planet, which is Chapter 5. One set of rules for the Earth and the sky. That is the book that a history of fishes nearly stopped.

It is worth noticing what the story is not. The fish book was not a bad book, and the Society was not foolish to print it. It simply ran out of money at the wrong moment, as institutions do. The *Principia* exists because one person happened to ask the right question, recognised the answer when he saw nine pages of it, and was willing to pay for the rest. Ideas do not publish themselves.

:::careful
> "Newton worked alone and the world just received his book."

Newton did the physics alone, and that part is as remarkable as it sounds. But the book exists because Halley asked, pushed, edited, proof-read and paid. Most of what you will read in this book reached you by a chain like that: someone who saw the point, and someone who found the money.
:::
