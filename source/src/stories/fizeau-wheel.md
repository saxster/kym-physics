:::scene Suresnes, outside Paris, July 1849

Hippolyte Fizeau was 29. His father was a professor of medicine, his family had produced doctors for generations, and he had started at the Paris medical school himself in 1840, until migraines drove him out. He travelled, recovered, and came back to a different subject. He sat in Arago's lectures at the Observatory, took a course on optics, borrowed his brother's engineering notes, and with his school friend Léon Foucault learned the new art of photography and took the first photograph of the Sun, sunspots and all. By 1849 he had a question that nobody had answered on the ground: how fast does light go?

#### The problem

Rømer had shown in 1676, from the late eclipses of one of Jupiter's moons, that light takes time to travel; that story is in Chapter 10. But that was a measurement across the solar system. On Earth, across a room, across a city, light seemed instant. No clock of 1849 could be started as a flash left and stopped as it returned.

Fizeau's answer was to make the light stop itself.

#### The wheel

Take a wheel with 720 teeth around its rim, and so 720 gaps. Shine a brilliant light, a limelight, through one gap and out through a telescope. Fizeau set his telescope in the bell tower of his father's house at Suresnes, on the western edge of Paris, aimed at a mirror on the hill of Montmartre, 8,633 metres away. The light crossed the city, hit the mirror, and came straight back towards the wheel.

Spin the wheel slowly, and the returning light comes back through the same gap it left by, and you see it, as a point of light in an eyepiece. Spin it faster. At some speed, by the time the light has made its round trip, the wheel has turned just far enough for a tooth to have moved into the gap's place. The returning light hits the tooth. The point of light vanishes. Spin faster still and the next gap arrives in time; the light reappears. Fizeau drove the wheel with falling weights through gears that multiplied its speed five hundred times, and for the last 110 seconds of each run it held a steady rate while he watched.

:::fig s_fizeau_wheel() | Fizeau's trick. Light leaves through a gap in the spinning wheel, crosses Paris to a mirror 8,633 m away and comes back. At the right speed the wheel has moved half a tooth in the meantime, the light meets a tooth, and the spot in the eyepiece goes dark.

#### The arithmetic

:::work
**The first blackout came at 12.6 turns per second.** One turn passes 720 teeth and 720 gaps, so moving from a gap to the next tooth is 1/1440 of a turn.
At 12.6 turns a second that takes 1 ÷ (1440 × 12.6) = 0.000 055 1 s, about 55 millionths of a second.
In that time the light went to Montmartre and back: 2 × 8,633 m = 17,266 m.
Speed = 17,266 m ÷ 0.000 055 1 s ≈ 313,000,000 m/s, about 313,000 km/s. With his own readings Fizeau published about 315,000 km/s.
The modern value is 299,792.458 km/s. He was about five per cent high, with a cogwheel, a lamp and a mirror on a hill.
:::

The light's round trip across Paris took about 58 millionths of a second. That is why it had always looked instant. Nothing in ordinary life happens that fast, and nothing in 1849 could time it, except a wheel that chopped the beam into pieces shorter than the journey.

#### Afterwards

Fizeau's friend and rival Foucault found a better way, with a spinning mirror instead of a wheel, and by 1862 had the speed within about 0.6 per cent. The two of them also settled, in 1850, an old argument about whether light travels faster or slower in water than in air (slower, which is why a straw looks bent in a glass; see Chapter 10). Foucault got there first, and Fizeau confirmed it seven weeks later with his own apparatus.

Today nobody measures the speed of light. Since 1983 it has been fixed by definition at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second, and the metre is defined from it: the distance light goes in one 299,792,458th of a second. Fizeau's number was the first step from "too fast to notice" to a quantity so well known that we build our ruler out of it.

:::careful
> "Light gets there instantly."

Too fast to notice is not the same as instant. Across Paris and back took 58 millionths of a second. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to reach Mumbai, and the Sun you see at sunset over Marine Drive is where it was eight minutes ago.
:::
