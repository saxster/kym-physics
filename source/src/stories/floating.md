:::scene Syracuse, Sicily, about 250 BCE

Here is the story as it is usually told. The king of Syracuse has given a goldsmith a lump of gold to make into a crown. The crown comes back weighing exactly what the gold weighed, but the king suspects the smith has kept some of the gold and made up the weight with silver. He asks Archimedes to find out without damaging the crown. Archimedes is stumped until, lowering himself into a full bath, he sees the water slop over the side, realises that a body pushes aside its own volume of water, and runs home through the streets shouting *Eureka*, "I have found it", having forgotten to dress.

It is a wonderful story and almost certainly not true. Nobody wrote it down until the Roman author Vitruvius, about two centuries after Archimedes. And in 1586 a 22-year-old Galileo Galilei worked out why it could not have been done that way.

:::scene Florence, 1586

Galileo's little essay, *La Bilancetta*, "The Little Balance", makes a simple point. Vitruvius has Archimedes measuring the water that the crown pushes out of a brim-full vessel, and comparing it with the overflow from pure gold and pure silver. But the difference between the overflow from a crown of pure gold and from one adulterated with silver is small, a spoonful or so of water, and water clinging to the vessel and to the crown would swamp the measurement. Nobody could have decided a king's case that way. Galileo proposed a balance instead: weigh the crown in air, then weigh it hanging in water, and let the water do the measuring. To see why that works, you need the thing Archimedes really did discover.

#### Step 1: What water does to anything inside it

Chapter 6 shows that pressure in water grows with depth. Hold a closed tin under water and the water pushes on every face of it, but it pushes on the bottom face, which is deeper, harder than on the top face. The sideways pushes on opposite faces cancel. The up-and-down pushes do not: the push up on the bottom beats the push down on the top. Every object in a fluid feels this net upward push, whether it floats, sinks or hangs. This is ==buoyancy==; the push is called the buoyant force, and some books call it upthrust.

:::work
**The buoyant force on a 10 cm cube, from the pressures alone.**
Water pressure grows by 1,000 × 9.8 × 0.1 = 980 newtons per square metre for every 10 cm of depth, whatever depth you start at.
The cube's bottom face is 10 cm deeper than its top, so the water pushes up on the bottom with 980 N/m² more pressure than it pushes down on the top. Each face is 0.01 m², so the extra push is 980 × 0.01 = 9.8 N upward.
9.8 N is the weight of exactly 1 kg of water, which is one litre, which is the volume of the cube. The water pushes up on the cube with the weight of the water the cube has pushed aside.
:::

:::fig s_buoyancy_cube() | A cube held under water. The water pushes on every face, but the bottom face is deeper, so the upward push on it is bigger than the downward push on the top. Sideways pushes cancel. What is left over is an upward force equal to the weight of the water the cube displaces: 9.8 newtons for a 10 cm cube, however deep it is.

#### Step 2: Archimedes' principle

The last line of that calculation is Archimedes' real discovery, and it is true for any shape, not just a cube. Here is the argument that shows why. Picture the object gone, and its space filled with water instead. That water would just sit there, floating in the rest of the water, neither rising nor sinking; so the surrounding water must be holding it up with a push exactly equal to its weight. Now put the object back. The surrounding water has not changed, so it pushes on the object exactly as it pushed on the water: upward, with the weight of the water displaced. That is ==Archimedes' principle==: the buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it pushes aside.

Everything about floating follows. If the object weighs less than the water it displaces, the buoyant force wins and it rises; if more, it sinks; if the same, it hangs. And since an object displaces its own volume when fully under, "weighs less than the same volume of water" is the same as "is less dense than water". ==Density==, mass divided by volume, decides. Water is 1,000 kg per cubic metre, which is 1 gram per cubic centimetre. Wood at about 0.7 floats; steel at 7.8 sinks; ice at 0.92 floats with about 92 per cent of itself under the surface.

#### Step 3: Galileo's balance

Now Galileo's method. Weigh an object in air. Then weigh it hanging on a thread, fully under water. It weighs less, and the loss is the buoyant force: the weight of the water it displaces. From the loss you know the volume of water displaced, which is the object's own volume, and from mass and volume you have the density, with no overflow to catch. A worked example from a textbook: a coin weighs 8.630 g in air and 7.800 g under water. The loss, 0.830 g, is the mass of 0.830 cm³ of water, so the coin's volume is 0.830 cm³ and its density is 8.630 ÷ 0.830 = 10.4 g/cm³. Gold is 19.3. This coin is not gold; it is about the density of silver. A balance can decide a king's case. A puddle on the floor cannot.

#### Step 4: Why a steel ship floats

Steel is 7.8 times as dense as water, so a lump of it sinks. A ship of 50,000 tonnes floats because a ship is not a lump. To float, it must displace 50,000 tonnes of water: in the Arabian Sea, whose water is slightly denser than fresh water, about 49,000 cubic metres of it. The hull is shaped to push aside that much sea, and most of what fills the hull's volume is air, cargo and empty rooms, not steel. The ship's *average* density, steel and air together, is less than the sea's, so it floats. Load it and it sinks lower, displacing more; sail it into a fresh-water river and it sinks lower still, because each cubic metre of fresh water weighs a little less. A ball of atta dough sinks in a bucket; press the same dough into a bowl shape and it floats. Nothing about the dough has changed except how much water it pushes aside.

#### Step 5: The egg

Put an egg in a glass of tap water and it sinks: an egg is a little denser than water. Now stir in salt, a spoonful at a time. Nothing about the egg changes. The *water* changes: dissolved salt makes each cubic centimetre of it heavier, and once the salt water is denser than the egg, the egg lifts off the bottom and floats. It takes several spoons. Try the same idea the other way round with an orange: the whole orange floats, because its peel is full of tiny air pockets, and the peeled orange, denser without them, sinks.

:::careful
> "Heavy things sink and light things float."

A 50,000-tonne ship floats and a ₹5 coin sinks. Weight is the wrong thing to compare. Compare the object's density, its mass for its volume, with the density of the liquid: lower floats, higher sinks. "Heavy for its size" is the phrase to use, and it is the only one that gets the ship and the coin both right.
:::

:::school
Your IGCSE syllabus covers density, ρ = m/V. It does not use the word "upthrust", though many school books do; this book says buoyant force, which is the same thing. Whichever word you use, say what causes it: the pressure of the fluid, greater on the bottom of the object than on the top.
:::

:::home Floaters and sinkers
Put an egg in a tall glass of tap water: it sinks. Add salt a teaspoon at a time, stirring until each spoonful dissolves, and count the spoons until the egg lifts. Several are needed; stop when it floats. Put a whole orange in a bowl of water, then the same orange peeled. Then make a boat: a sheet of aluminium foil rolled into a tight ball sinks; the same sheet shaped into a dish floats, and will carry ₹1 coins as cargo. Count how many it takes to sink it, then reshape the foil and try to carry more.
:::
