:::scene Paris, January 1851

Léon Foucault had no degree in physics. He had started in medicine and given it up, and his only training in science was what he had taught himself and picked up as a journalist, reporting the meetings of the Académie des sciences for a Paris newspaper. With his school friend Hippolyte Fizeau he had made the first photograph of the Sun, and in 1850 he had shown, a few weeks ahead of Fizeau, that light goes more slowly through water than through air.

The idea came from a workshop. A thin, flexible rod clamped in the axis of a lathe, when plucked, vibrates in one plane, and when the lathe turns, the plane of vibration does not turn with it. The rod keeps its direction while its support goes round. Foucault saw what that meant for a pendulum, and for the Earth.

#### The three pendulums

Everyone educated in 1851 accepted that the Earth turns; Aryabhata had said so in 499, and after Newton nobody serious doubted it. But nobody had ever *shown* it, in a room, to the eye. Foucault's idea was this. A pendulum swings in a plane, and nothing about a hanging weight can twist that plane round. So if you hang a pendulum and the plane of its swing slowly turns, it is not the pendulum turning. It is the floor.

He tried it first in his cellar, with a two-metre wire. The plane of the swing drifted, exactly as he expected. Then, with the backing of Arago, the director of the Paris Observatory, he hung an eleven-metre pendulum there and showed it to the scientists. He reported it to the Académie on 3 February 1851.

A month later came the public version. Under the dome of the Panthéon, the great domed building in the heart of Paris, he hung a hollow brass sphere 17 cm across, filled with lead to a mass of 28 kg, from a wire 67 metres long. A stylus under the bob brushed through ridges of sand on each swing. Each swing shifted the line it drew by about 2.3 millimetres, and the crowd could watch the lines creep round the floor. Paris came to look. The story goes that the newspapers asked their readers, "Have you seen the Earth turn?"

:::fig s_foucault_turn() | A Foucault pendulum keeps swinging in the same plane while the floor turns beneath it. The amount of turning depends on where you are: a full circle a day at the pole, about 11 degrees an hour in Paris, about 5 degrees an hour in Mumbai, and none at all on the equator.

#### How much it turns, and why not the same everywhere

:::work
**The Panthéon pendulum.** A pendulum's time for one swing there and back is 2π√(L ÷ g). With L = 67 m and g = 9.81 m/s²: 2π × √(67 ÷ 9.81) = 16.4 s. A slow, grand swing.
**The turning of the floor.** The Earth turns 360° in 23 h 56 min, a shade under 15° an hour. At the North Pole the floor under a pendulum would turn the full 15° an hour and the swing-line would come round once a day.
Anywhere else, the floor turns less about the vertical. The rule is 15° × sin(latitude) per hour.
Paris, latitude 48.85°: 15 × 0.753 = 11.3° an hour, a full circle in about 32 hours.
Mumbai, latitude 19.08°: 15 × 0.327 = 4.9° an hour, a full circle in about three days.
On the equator: nothing. The swing-line never turns.
:::

That last line puzzles everyone, and it puzzled the Paris public, so here is the picture. Stand at the North Pole and the ground under your feet spins like a turntable, once a day. Stand on the equator and the ground does not spin under you at all; it tips over, carrying you round the Earth's axis the way a point on the rim of a wheel goes round, and tipping does not twist the direction of a swing. Mumbai is in between: the ground does a bit of spinning and a lot of tipping, and the bit of spinning is what the pendulum reveals.

#### The Earth turns everywhere

By the summer of 1851 the experiment had been repeated in Rennes and in the cathedral at Reims, and in at least 25 cities in the United States. The next year Foucault built a cleaner proof, a spinning wheel on gimbals that holds its direction while the Earth turns beneath it, and he gave it the name it still has, the gyroscope. The original pendulum is in the Musée des Arts et Métiers in Paris, and since 2015 one has hung again in the Panthéon. You can stand under it and watch the floor go round.

:::careful
> "The pendulum turns slowly on its wire."

It does not turn. Nothing is there to turn it: the wire hangs from a point and the weight swings under gravity, and gravity has no twist in it. The pendulum keeps its plane, and the building, the city and the ground turn under it. The hard part of the experiment is believing which thing is moving, which was Aryabhata's whole point thirteen and a half centuries earlier.
:::

A Foucault pendulum cannot be built in a flat; the effect at Mumbai's latitude is slow and the slightest flaw in the hanging spoils it. But if you are ever in a science museum with a tall atrium, look for the sand or the row of toppled pegs on the floor. You are watching the one experiment that answers Chapter 14's first question, and that story, Aryabhata's boat, is told in Section 14.1.
