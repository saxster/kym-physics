:::scene The basilica of Santa Croce, Florence, 12 March 1737

Galileo died in 1642, old, blind and under house arrest. Nine years earlier the Church had tried him for teaching that the Earth moves round the Sun, and a man condemned like that could not be given a grand public tomb. So he was buried quietly, in a small room off the side of the basilica of Santa Croce in Florence, and there he stayed for ninety-five years.

On 12 March 1737 the city finally moved him. His remains were carried to a new monumental tomb in the main body of the church, directly opposite the tomb of Michelangelo, where they still lie. It was a solemn occasion, and not everybody present behaved solemnly. A historian named Giovanni Targioni Tozzetti, and others, took souvenirs. From the body of the most famous scientist in Europe they removed three fingers, a tooth and a bone from his spine.

#### Where the pieces went

The pieces travelled. One finger, the middle finger of the right hand, ended up in Florence's museum of the history of science, standing upright inside a glass egg, where it has been on show for the best part of a century. The vertebra went to Padua, the university town where Galileo had taught for eighteen years and done his best work. The other two fingers and the tooth passed into the hands of a noble family, the Capponi, and in 1905 they disappeared. For a hundred years they were presumed lost.

In 2009 a strange lot came up at an auction: a tall wooden case of unusual shape, made in the nineteenth century and topped with a carved wooden bust of Galileo. Inside was a blown-glass vase from the eighteenth century, and inside the vase, two fingers and a tooth. An anonymous collector recognised what they might be, bought the lot and traced its history back through the Capponi family to the night of 1737. The experts of Florence, led by Cristina Acidini and Paolo Galluzzi, confirmed it. The newspaper reports disagree about which two fingers they are, a thumb and the index finger or a thumb and the middle one, which tells you something about how carefully souvenirs were labelled in 1737. The following spring the museum reopened with a new name, the Museo Galileo, and put all three fingers and the tooth on display.

It happened to be four hundred years since 1609, the year those fingers first held a telescope.

#### What the hands did

It is the hands that are worth thinking about, not as relics but as tools. On 25 August 1609 Galileo climbed the bell tower of San Marco in Venice and showed the Doge a spyglass he had built that made things look eight or nine times closer; the city gave him a pay rise. On the night of 7 January 1610 he pointed a better one at Jupiter and saw three small "stars" in a line beside it. By 15 January he had watched them long enough to know that they were moons going round the planet. The little book he rushed out on 13 March 1610, the *Starry Messenger*, changed what people thought the sky was.

A few years earlier, in Padua, those hands had rolled bronze balls down a grooved ramp to slow falling down enough to time it, the experiment you repeat in Chapter 1. They had swung weights on strings and found that a pendulum's time depends on its length and not on how wide it swings, which is Chapter 9. They had built a balance to weigh things in water, at the age of 22, to show that the famous story of Archimedes and the crown could not have worked as told.

#### Relics and reasons

Florence keeps the fingers the way churches keep the relics of saints, in glass, with labels, for pilgrims. There is something odd in that for a man the Church once condemned, and something odder still. A finger in a glass egg tells you nothing about why Galileo matters. His bones are no different from anyone's. What made him Galileo was a habit: look for yourself, measure, argue it through, and say plainly when the test does not come out clean, as he did when his two dropped balls "did not quite fall together". That habit is not in a museum case. You can pick it up free, and this whole book is an attempt to hand it to you.

:::careful
> "Galileo was a genius, so he saw things nobody else could."

The spyglass was a Dutch invention he heard about and improved. The moons of Jupiter were there for anyone with a telescope and the patience to watch for a week. What he had was not special eyes but a special method: point the instrument, write down what you see night after night, and let the record decide. The fingers that did the pointing are in a glass case; the method is yours if you want it.
:::
