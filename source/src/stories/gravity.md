:::scene Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, 1666

In 1666 Isaac Newton was twenty-three and living at his mother's farmhouse in Woolsthorpe, a village in the English county of Lincolnshire. Cambridge, where he was a student, had closed because of plague, and he had a great deal of time to think. There was an apple tree in the garden. There still is.

Sixty years later, as an old man, he told a friend called William Stukeley that his thinking about gravity had been "occasion'd by the fall of an apple, as he sat in contemplative mood". That is the whole of the evidence: one memory, told six decades after the event. The apple did not hit him on the head. That detail was added by other people, and a librarian at the Royal Society has called the finished story "an 18th-century sound bite". So treat the apple as the story goes, not as a fact.

The question behind it is real, though, and it is one of the best questions anyone has ever asked. An apple falls. The Moon, hanging in the same sky, does not. Why not?

Newton's answer was that it does.

#### Step 1: the Moon is falling

Think back to Chapter 2. A thing left alone keeps moving in a straight line at a steady speed. The Moon does not move in a straight line; it goes round us in a near-circle, month after month. So something must be bending its path towards Earth, all the time. Whatever that something is, it pulls the Moon towards us, and a pull towards us is exactly what makes an apple fall.

Why, then, does the Moon not land on us? Because it is also moving sideways, fast: about 1 km every second. In each second it falls a little towards Earth, but it also moves a long way along, so it ends up at the same height, a little further round. It falls *around* Earth instead of into it. Chapter 5 and the story of Newton's cannon make that idea do real work; here we only need the first half of it. The Moon is falling.

#### Step 2: how far does it fall?

Here is the sum Newton did, with today's numbers. From the size of the Moon's orbit and the time it takes, you can work out how much its path bends. In one second, the Moon ends up 1.36 mm below the straight line it would have followed with no pull at all. One millimetre and a bit, in a second. That is a gentle fall.

Now wait a minute instead of a second. Falls grow with the square of the time, so in sixty seconds the Moon drops 60 × 60 = 3,600 times as far: about 4.9 metres below its straight line.

An apple dropped from your hand falls 4.9 metres in its first *second*.

So the Moon, in a minute, falls what an apple falls in a second. Earth pulls on each kilogram of the Moon 3,600 times more weakly than it pulls on each kilogram of apple.

:::fig s_moon_falls() | The Moon moves about 1 km sideways every second and falls 1.36 mm towards Earth in the same second; over a minute it drops 4.9 m below the straight line, which is exactly an apple's first second of falling. The gap is hugely exaggerated here: 4.9 m against the 61 km the Moon travels in that minute.

#### Step 3: sixty times further, 3,600 times weaker

Now the second number. How far away is the Moon? About 384,000 km from Earth's centre, while the apple is 6,371 km from Earth's centre, down at the surface. Divide: the Moon is about 60 times further from Earth's centre than the apple is.

Sixty times further. Three thousand six hundred times weaker. And 60 × 60 = 3,600.

That is the pattern Newton saw, and it is called the ==inverse square law==: double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter; triple it and the pull drops to a ninth; sixty times the distance and the pull drops to one part in 3,600. "Inverse" because the pull goes down as the distance goes up; "square" because it is the distance *squared* that matters.

:::work
**Newton's check, in modern numbers.**
Pull on an apple at the surface: 9.8 m/s² (every kilogram gains 9.8 m/s of speed each second it falls).
Predicted pull at the Moon, 60 times further away: 9.8 ÷ 60² = 9.8 ÷ 3,600 = 0.0027 m/s².
Pull actually needed to bend the Moon's path into its orbit (from its distance and its 27.3-day month): 0.0027 m/s².
They match. Newton himself found that his two numbers agreed, in his words, "pretty nearly". With today's figures the leftover difference is under 1%, and it comes from the Moon's slightly oval orbit and from Earth moving too.
:::

Why a square, and not just "weaker with distance"? Picture a torch. Its light spreads out as it travels. At twice the distance the same light covers a patch twice as wide and twice as tall, so four times the area, and each bit of the wall gets a quarter as much. Gravity is not light, but it spreads out through space in the same way: the same influence, shared over a bigger and bigger sphere. The sphere's area grows with the square of its radius, and that is where the square comes from.

#### Step 4: the same rule for everything

Newton did not stop at Earth and the Moon. He proposed that *every* lump of matter pulls on every other lump, with a force that grows with both masses and shrinks with the square of the distance between them. That is ==universal gravitation==, and "universal" is the daring word. The same pull that drops a mango on Marine Drive holds the Moon in its month, holds Earth in its year around the Sun, and, in Chapter 5, steers Chandrayaan-3.

Two things about this rule surprise people.

First, it works both ways. Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon pulls Earth back with exactly the same force. That is the third law from Chapter 3. The effects differ only because the masses differ: the Moon swings round a big loop, while Earth wobbles round a small one. In fact both of them circle a shared balance point that lies about 1,700 km *below* Earth's surface.

Second, the pull is tiny between ordinary things. Two 1 kg bags of sugar a metre apart attract each other with about 0.000 000 000 07 newtons, far too little to feel, which is why the kitchen does not slide together. It took until 1798 for Henry Cavendish to measure a pull that small, inside a shed at Clapham, and so weigh the whole Earth.

#### Step 5: Halley's question

In August 1684 the astronomer Edmond Halley visited Newton in Cambridge with a question. Suppose the Sun pulls a planet with a force that weakens with the square of the distance. What path would the planet follow?

An ellipse, said Newton: the very shape Kepler had found from Tycho's measurements of Mars seventy-five years earlier. He had worked it out already. He could not find the paper.

Halley pressed him to write it all down properly. The result, three years later, was the *Principia* of 1687, the book that joined the falling apple and the orbiting Moon under one rule, and that Halley ended up paying to print himself.

:::careful
> "There's no gravity in space. That's why the Moon doesn't fall down."

Eight out of ten thirteen-year-olds believe something like this. But the Moon *is* falling, 1.36 mm every second, and the only thing that can be pulling it is Earth's gravity, reaching across 384,000 km of empty space. It is weak out there, one part in 3,600 of the pull at the ground, but weak is not nothing. Weak is exactly what keeps the Moon in its month.
:::

:::careful
> "So the Moon gets 1.36 mm closer to Earth every second."

No. It falls 1.36 mm below the *straight line* it would otherwise follow. Because it is also moving sideways at about 1 km/s, that straight line was carrying it away from Earth, and the fall just cancels the drift. Its distance from Earth stays almost the same, month after month.
:::

:::home The torch and the wall
Take a phone torch into a dark room and shine it at a wall from one metre away. Mark the edges of the bright patch with your fingers, or stick two bits of tape. Now step back to two metres. The patch is about twice as wide and twice as tall, so it covers about four times the area, and it looks dimmer, because the same light is shared out over four times as much wall. Three metres gives nine times the area. That spreading, one over the distance squared, is the same pattern gravity follows.
:::
