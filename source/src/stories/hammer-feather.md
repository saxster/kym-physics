:::scene Hadley Rille, the Moon, 2 August 1971

It is the last few minutes of the third and final moonwalk of Apollo 15. Commander David Scott stands in front of the television camera in his white suit, holding something in each gloved hand.

"Well, in my left hand, I have a feather; in my right hand, a hammer," he tells the camera. He explains that a long time ago a gentleman named Galileo made a discovery about falling objects, and that there could be no better place to confirm it than on the Moon. "And I'll drop the two of them here and, hopefully, they'll hit the ground at the same time."

He lets go. The hammer and the feather fall side by side, slowly, and land together in the grey dust.

"How about that!" says Scott.

"How about that," says Joe Allen in Houston, over applause in the control room.

"Which proves that Mr Galileo was correct in his findings," says Scott.

"Superb," says Allen.

#### Whose idea it was

The idea was Allen's: he was the astronaut in Houston whose voice the crew heard during their moonwalks, and Scott later said plainly that the demonstration was his. The hammer was the real thing: the 1.32 kg aluminium geological hammer Scott had been using to break rocks. The feather was chosen with care. The lunar module that had carried the two astronauts down to the surface was named *Falcon*, so the feather was a falcon's, from a bird kept at the United States Air Force Academy, whose mascot is the falcon.

#### The honest numbers

The official science report says the two objects were released from about 1.6 metres. People who have since gone through the television film frame by frame put it closer to 1.2 metres, judging from Scott's posture and the known length of the hammer. Nobody on the Moon measured it. On the film, the fall lasts 36 frames at 30 frames a second: about 1.2 seconds. The report also gives a mass for the feather that is a misprint, far too heavy; a feather like that weighs about a gram.

:::work
**Does 1.2 seconds make sense?** On the Moon, falling things speed up at about 1.62 m/s² (often rounded to 1.67 m/s², a sixth of the 9.8 m/s² on Earth). A thing dropped from rest falls a height h in a time √(2h ÷ g).
From 1.2 m on the Moon: √(2 × 1.2 ÷ 1.62) = 1.22 s (or 1.20 s using 1.67 m/s²). The film says about 1.2 s. It fits.
The same drop on Earth would take √(2 × 1.2 ÷ 9.8) = 0.49 s, for the hammer. The feather, on Earth, would still be drifting down long after the hammer had landed.
:::

#### Why it worked

On Earth a feather loses to a hammer because of the air. Anything moving through air is pushed back by it, and a feather is nearly all surface and almost no weight, so the air's push stops it speeding up almost at once. The Moon has no air worth mentioning. Take the air away and there is nothing left to tell a feather from a hammer. The Moon pulls the feather with a tiny force, but the feather is also tiny, and the two go together exactly: twice the mass, twice the pull, and twice the sluggishness. Everything speeds up at the same rate. Chapter 3 gives you the sluggishness half of that argument and Chapter 5 the pull half.

Allen wrote in the mission's science report that the hammer and the feather "were observed to undergo the same acceleration and strike the lunar surface simultaneously", and he added something that is easy to miss. The result, he said, was reassuring, because the journey home depended on that theory. The same rule of gravity that dropped the feather was steering their spacecraft.

:::fig c4_moon_strobe() | A hammer dropped on Earth and on the Moon, drawn every 0.2 s. On the Moon, where g is 1.67 m/s², a feather dropped beside the hammer falls side by side with it and lands at the same instant.

:::careful
> "There is no gravity on the Moon. That is why astronauts bounce."

The hammer fell. It fell at about a sixth of the rate it would on Earth, because the Moon pulls about a sixth as hard, but it fell. Astronauts bounce because, with a sixth of the pull, each step carries them higher and keeps them up longer before they come down, not because nothing is pulling them down.
:::

:::careful
> "Things fall in slow motion on the Moon because there is less air."

Slow because the pull is weaker. No air is a separate fact, and it is the reason the feather kept up with the hammer, not the reason both fell slowly.
:::
