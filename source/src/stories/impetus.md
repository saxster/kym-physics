:::scene Alexandria, about 530

An archer draws a bow and lets go. For a fraction of a second the string pushes the arrow. Then the arrow is on its own, flying across the field with nothing touching it at all. Why does it keep going?

In the sixth century, in Alexandria, a scholar named John Philoponus worried at that question, and he had good reason to. The most respected account of motion in the world, written by Aristotle nearly nine hundred years earlier, said something about it that Philoponus could not believe. Aristotle's rule was that everything which moves is moved by something. An ox moves the cart; a hand moves the stone; when the mover stops, the moving stops too. The rule fits almost everything you have ever seen, which is why it lasted so long. It fails for the arrow. Nothing is touching the arrow, and it flies on.

Aristotle's answer was the air. The bowstring, he said, sets the air moving, and the moving air carries the arrow along behind it. Philoponus found that hard to accept, and you can see why with a ball on a table: fan the air behind it as hard as you like with a notebook and it barely stirs. Air is a feeble pusher. Ride a bicycle fast and you feel on your face which way air really pushes on a moving thing: backwards. So Philoponus proposed something different. The bow, he said, puts something *into* the arrow, a kind of motive power that travels with it and is gradually used up as it flies. The mover was not in the air. It was inside the arrow.

#### Step 1: Why the idea felt right

Hold on to that picture for a moment and notice how good it is. It explains why a harder shot flies further: more of the something goes in. It explains why every arrow eventually drops: the something runs out. And it matches the feeling in your own arm when you throw a cricket ball. You *give* the ball the throw, and the throw seems to travel with it.

It matches so well that most people believe it still. In 1985 two physicists, Halloun and Hestenes, gave a careful test to 478 university students in the United States before their first physics course. Sixty-five per cent explained motion with a force carried inside the moving object; after a term of lectures, 44 per cent still did. Forty-seven per cent believed that a thing with no force on it at all slows down anyway. Nearly fifteen hundred years after Philoponus, his idea is the most popular physics on Earth. It is wrong. It is not stupid, and finding exactly where it goes wrong is the point of this story.

#### Step 2: Bukhara: the idea gets a name

Philoponus wrote in Greek, and his idea went east before it went west. Five hundred years later, in Bukhara, the scholar Ibn Sina discussed it in his great *Book of Healing* and gave the carried something a name of its own: *mayl*, an inclination. An arrow in flight had an inclination to keep going, put there by the bow.

#### Step 3: Paris: Buridan's recipe

In the fourteenth century the idea reached the University of Paris, probably by way of the Arabic writers rather than from Philoponus directly. Jean Buridan called the something *impetus*, and he did more than name it. He said how much of it a moving body carries: more if it moves faster, and more if there is more matter in it. A heavy stone and a light one thrown at the same speed carry different amounts of impetus; the same stone thrown faster carries more.

That recipe, speed multiplied by quantity of matter, is exactly the modern recipe for momentum, which you meet in Chapter 3. Buridan had the right two ingredients more than three hundred years before Newton.

:::work
**Buridan's recipe with modern numbers** (the inputs are rough guesses).
A cricket ball has a mass of about 0.16 kg. Thrown at 25 m/s, which is 90 km/h, its quantity of motion is 0.16 × 25 = 4 kg·m/s.
You, at about 40 kg, shuffling along at 0.1 m/s, carry 40 × 0.1 = 4 kg·m/s. The same.
Buridan would say the ball and you carry equal impetus. Newton would say equal momentum. On the *amount*, they agree completely.
:::

#### Step 4: Where it goes wrong

The mistake is in what *kind* of thing impetus is. Buridan's impetus is a force stored inside the arrow: a push the arrow owns and spends. Here is the test that catches it, and it is the single most useful habit in this book. **For every force you name, name the other object that exerts it.** A force is always one object acting on another. The string on the arrow: two objects. The Earth on the arrow: two objects. Now look at the arrow in mid-flight and ask who is exerting "the force of the shot". The string has gone. The archer is standing fifty metres behind. Nobody is left to do the pushing, so there is no such force. Only two things can act on the flying arrow at all: the air it moves through and the Earth beneath it.

:::fig c2_arrow_forces() | The forces on an arrow in flight, each named with the object that exerts it: the Earth pulls it down and the air pushes it backwards. The blue arrow is the arrow's velocity, which is not a force. Aristotle's forward push from the air is crossed out, because nothing exerts it.

And neither of those two pushes the arrow forward. The air pushes backward, as your face on the bicycle told you. The Earth pulls downward. Both are busy *changing* the arrow's motion: the air slowing it, the Earth bending its path into the familiar arc. Take them away, by firing the arrow in empty space, and it would fly straight for ever at the speed it left the string. The arrow needs nothing inside it to keep going. It needs something outside it to stop.

#### Step 5: What Galileo changed

The man who turned the question round was Galileo Galilei. Instead of asking what keeps a moving thing moving, he asked what stops it, and found that the answer was always something he could name: a floor, a slope, the air. Reduce the rubbing and the stopping takes longer; remove it, in a thought experiment, and the stopping never comes. That is the subject of the story *The ball that never stops*, and of Chapter 2.

Isaac Newton made it the first of his laws of motion in 1687, and he dealt with the arrow in a sentence: a projectile keeps moving except in so far as the air slows it and gravity pulls it down, and the planets, which meet almost no air, keep going for ages. He also kept the good half of Buridan's idea. He defined a body's "quantity of motion" as its velocity and its quantity of matter taken together, which is Buridan's recipe, and today we call it momentum. What a moving thing carries is motion. A force is the only thing that can change it, and a force is never carried; it is always exerted, by something you can point to.

:::careful
> "All right, there's no force inside. But a moving thing still loses its motion slowly by itself, even with nothing touching it."

Nothing has ever been seen to do that. Every slowing you have ever watched had a cause you could name: the floor under the book, the cloth under the ice cube, the air around the ball. Newton's own evidence was the planets, which meet almost nothing and have not slowed down. Nearly half of the students in the 1985 study believed in motion that fades on its own. When something slows, look for the force. It is always there.
:::

:::home The puff test
Put a ping-pong ball, or a tight ball of paper, on a smooth table. Try to move it Aristotle's way: fan the air behind it hard with a notebook, without touching it. Now flick it with a finger and watch it roll. Which did more, the air or the touch? Then throw the paper ball gently across the room and, while it is in the air, name every force on it and the object that exerts each one. If you want to name "the force of the throw", find the object that is exerting it right now.
:::
