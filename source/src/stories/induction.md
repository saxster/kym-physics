:::scene Royal Institution, London, 3 September 1821

In September 1821, a year after Ørsted's needle twitched in Copenhagen, Michael Faraday set up a small dish of mercury at the Royal Institution in London, stood a magnet in it, and dipped a wire into the mercury so that it hung free. When he sent a current through the wire, the wire began to circle round the magnet, and kept circling for as long as the current flowed. On 3 and 4 September 1821 he had built the first electric motor. (Mercury is poisonous. Nobody repeats this version today, and neither will you.)

A current near a magnet feels a push. Faraday had turned that push into movement. The question that kept him busy for the next ten years ran the other way. If a current can make a magnet move, can a magnet make a current?

:::scene Royal Institution, London, 29 August 1831

On 29 August 1831 he wound two separate coils of wire onto opposite sides of an iron ring. One coil he connected to a battery through a switch. The other he connected only to a small compass needle, which would swing if any current passed. There was no electrical connection between the two coils at all.

He closed the switch. The needle jumped, and then settled back to rest, though the current in the first coil was still flowing steadily. He opened the switch. The needle jumped again, the other way, and settled. For as long as the current was simply *on*, nothing happened; the needle moved only at the moments of change. In the same year, across the Atlantic, Joseph Henry found the same thing independently.

:::fig s_faraday_ring() | Faraday's ring. Left: two coils on an iron ring, one on a battery and switch, the other on a needle. Right: what the needle did. It kicked one way when the switch closed, kicked the other way when it opened, and stayed still while the current was steady.

#### Step 1: the motor effect

Start with 1821. A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field feels a force, at right angles to both the current and the field. Make the wire a loop and the forces on its two sides push opposite ways, so the loop turns. Add a trick to reverse the current every half turn, so that the push always carries it onwards, and you have an electric motor: a device that turns electrical energy into movement. Every fan in your flat, every mixer, every local train, is a loop of wire being pushed round by a magnetic field.

#### Step 2: induction, which only happens when something changes

Now 1831. Push a bar magnet into a coil of wire connected to a sensitive meter and the meter flicks. Hold the magnet still inside the coil and the meter reads zero. Pull it out and the meter flicks the other way. Push it in faster and the flick is bigger. A magnet sitting beside a wire does nothing at all; a *changing* magnetic field through a coil pushes the coil's electrons along, and that push appears in the wire as a voltage. This is electromagnetic induction. It does not matter how the change happens: move the magnet, move the coil, switch a nearby current on or off, or turn the coil so that the field passes through it at a different angle. Any change in how much field threads the coil induces a voltage.

The direction has a rule too. The induced current always acts against the change that made it. Push a magnet's north end towards a coil and the coil's current makes the coil a north pole facing it, pushing back; pull the magnet away and the coil's current reverses to tug it back. Experiments at MIT drop a magnet towards a cold copper ring and watch it slow in mid-air, braked by the current it induces.

#### Step 3: the generator

Turn a coil steadily between the poles of a magnet. As it turns, the field through it rises, falls, reverses, rises again, and so a voltage appears in the coil that swings back and forth with every turn. That is a generator, and it is built almost exactly like a motor, run backwards: a motor takes current and gives movement; a generator takes movement and gives current. The electricity it gives is alternating, because the coil is turning.

A generator does not *make* energy. The work done turning the coil is what comes out as electrical energy, no more. So every power station needs something to turn the coil: falling water, steam from boiling water, or wind.

:::work
**Why Mumbai's mains is 50 hertz.**
Mumbai's supply is alternating current at 50 cycles per second. That is the generators' coils reversing their voltage 100 times a second, 50 times one way and 50 the other, all across the grid in step.
:::

Some of Mumbai's electricity comes from exactly this. In the Western Ghats east of the city, in Raigad district, water from a lake at Thokerwadi falls down a steep slope through a pipe to the Bhivpuri power station, built between 1916 and 1922, and spins its turbines. All of its output, about 300 million units a year, is sent to Mumbai. With its two sister stations at Khopoli and Bhira it also forms the city's "islanding" system: the backup that keeps Mumbai lit when the wider grid fails. Power stations everywhere else do the same job with steam or wind turning the coil.

#### Step 4: the transformer, which Faraday also built

Faraday's ring was the first transformer. A changing current in one coil induces a voltage in another, and if the second coil has more turns than the first, the induced voltage is higher; fewer turns, lower. That is how electricity travels. Power stations step the voltage up to anywhere between 120,000 and 700,000 volts for the long lines, because for the same power a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the heat wasted in the wires grows with the square of the current. Ten times the voltage, one hundredth of the loss. Substations near your home step it back down to about 230 volts.

A transformer works only on *changing* current. Feed it a steady direct current and the second coil gives nothing, exactly as Faraday's needle gave nothing while his switch stayed closed.

:::careful
> "A magnet near a wire makes electricity."

Only a changing field does. Move the magnet, or change the current, and a push appears in the wire. Hold everything still and there is nothing, however strong the magnet.
:::

:::careful
> "Generators make energy."

They convert it. Something has to turn the coil, and the electrical energy that comes out is the work that went in, minus losses. The dam, the boiler and the wind are where the energy really comes from.
:::

:::home A magnet that falls slowly
Ask an adult for a strong magnet (the kind sold as a neodymium disc) and a length of copper or aluminium pipe from a hardware shop, wider than the magnet. Drop a steel nut down the pipe: it clatters out at once. Drop the magnet: it drifts down, visibly slower, braked by the currents its moving field induces in the pipe. Nothing in this is electrical, but strong magnets pinch fingers, wipe cards and must be kept away from small children.
:::
