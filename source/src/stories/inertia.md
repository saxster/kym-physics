:::scene Padua, about 1604

Galileo Galilei could not time a falling ball. A ball dropped from head height is on the floor in less than half a second, far too quick for any clock he had. So in Padua he slowed falling down. He let balls roll down long, gently sloping ramps, where the same kind of motion takes several seconds, and measured that instead. You met those ramps in Chapter 1.

Later, in the books he wrote as an old man, he described an experiment that he could only do in his head. Set two ramps facing each other, like a letter V, and let a ball roll down one. It rolls up the other and climbs to very nearly the height it started from, falling a little short because of the rubbing. Make the second ramp shallower. The ball has to travel further along it to climb back to its starting height, and it does. Make the ramp shallower still, and the ball rolls further still. Now lay the second ramp perfectly flat. The ball can never climb back to its starting height, however far it rolls. So, Galileo reasoned, it never stops.

:::fig c2_two_ramps() | Galileo's thought experiment. Left: the ball climbs back to nearly its starting height. Middle: a shallower ramp, and it travels further to get there. Right: a flat ramp, where the starting height can never be regained, so the ball rolls on. The dashed line is the starting height.

#### Step 1: Follow the trend to its end

Look at what kind of argument that is. Galileo never watched a ball roll for ever; no one can. He watched a *trend*. Every time he made the ramp shallower the ball went further, and the only thing spoiling the pattern was friction. Reasoning about where a trend points once you remove the thing that spoils it is a thought experiment, and it is only trustworthy when every step has been checked against real ramps first.

You can compare the same trend on a counter. Slide an ice cube over a folded cloth and then across a wet steel thali kept flat beside it. Keep a hand at the far edge and wipe up the water afterwards. The cube usually travels farther on the thali: less rubbing means slower stopping. Follow the trend to its end: take the rubbing away entirely, and the stopping takes for ever. An air-hockey table comes close: with the fan on, a cushion of air lifts the puck clear of the table and it glides from end to end at very nearly constant speed.

#### Step 2: A rule in two halves

In 1644 the French philosopher René Descartes wrote the result down as a law of nature in his *Principles of Philosophy*: a body left to itself keeps moving at the same speed and in a straight line. Read that sentence slowly, because there are two claims in it. The direction does not change. The speed does not change either. People find the first half much easier to believe than the second. When Halloun and Hestenes tested 478 university students in 1985, 84 per cent agreed that a body with no force on it moves in a straight line, but only 30 per cent agreed that its speed stays the same. Half a law is a very common thing to believe.

In 1687 Isaac Newton made the whole law the first of his three laws of motion, in the book known as the *Principia*. In today's words: every body keeps its state of rest, or of steady motion in a straight line, unless forces acting on it compel it to change that state. Galileo had seen it first, with his ramps, and Newton never said so. Notice two things about the wording. A body at rest is covered as well as a moving one: rest is just the steady speed that happens to be zero. And the law is exact. A body left alone does not "tend" to keep moving, as if it might wear out eventually. It keeps its velocity *exactly*, speed and direction both, for as long as the total force on it is zero.

#### Step 3: What inertia is, and what it is not

The behaviour the law describes has a name: ==inertia==, the tendency of a thing to stay at rest or to keep moving in a straight line at a steady speed. Every object has it, parked or moving. How much it has is measured by its mass, the quantity of matter in it. Try to shove a basketball and it goes; shove a boulder the same way and it barely moves. A kilogram of kurmura and a kilogram of steel bolts have the same mass and the same inertia, though one fills a bag and the other fits in your fist. Mass is the same on Earth, in orbit and on the Moon. It counts stuff; it is not a pull.

Now the trap. People say "the katori kept sliding because of its inertia", as if inertia were a push that keeps things going. Swap the word for a made-up one: "the katori kept sliding because of its movability". The sentence says exactly as much, which is nothing. Inertia is not a force. It is the name for the fact that motion continues until a force changes it. The explanation of why the katori kept sliding is that *nothing had stopped it yet*: the floor's friction was small, so it took a while. Name the forces, or their absence. Never let the word do the explaining.

#### Step 4: The lurch

Now the question this law answers best. A local train is slowing into a station, and everyone standing lurches towards the front. What pushed them? Watch from the platform first. Before the brakes, every passenger is moving at the train's speed. Then the brakes grip, and the train slows. What slows the passengers? Only what touches them. The floor touches their feet, and the floor is part of the train, so friction drags their feet to a slower speed. Nothing is touching their heads and shoulders. Those parts keep the speed the train had a moment ago, exactly as Galileo's ball would, and from the platform you watch a whole compartment of people tilt forward.

Now step inside and feel it. From in here it genuinely feels as if something shoves you forward, and if you shut your eyes you would swear to it. Physicists call that feeling a fictitious force: inside anything that is speeding up, slowing down or turning, everything seems to be pushed the other way. Apply the test from the story of the arrow. What object is doing the pushing? There is none. The feeling is real; the force is not. It is the first law seen from inside a carriage that is changing its velocity while your body carries on with its own.

**How far ahead does your head get?** (The train numbers are guesses; measure your own.)
A local train at 60 km/h is moving at 16.7 m/s. Suppose it brakes to a stop in 20 s. It loses 16.7 ÷ 20 = 0.83 m/s of speed every second.
Your feet, gripped by the floor, slow with the train. If nothing slowed your head at all, after one second it would be ½ × 0.83 × 1² = 0.42 m ahead of where the floor has carried your feet. After two seconds, 1.7 m.
Your head is not free, of course. Your legs and back pull it along, and that pull is the lurch you feel. Grab the pole and your arm does the pulling instead.

:::careful
> "A moving thing keeps moving, but it slowly runs down on its own."

The law has no "slowly runs down" in it. Every real thing stops because a real force stops it: friction from the floor, the drag of the air. The test is not to watch for a ball that rolls for ever, which is impossible, but to watch the stopping fade exactly as the friction fades. The cloth, wet thali and air cushion let you compare stopping as rubbing is reduced. Remove the last of the friction and you remove the last of the stopping.
:::

:::home The tray that stops
Put a small toy car, or a ₹5 coin standing on its edge, in the middle of a flat tray or a hardback book. Hold it level, walk across the room at a steady pace, then stop sharply. The toy carries on towards the front of the tray; the coin topples forward. Nothing pushed them. The tray stopped and they did not. Now walk, and start sharply instead of stopping: they slide backwards, towards you, which is the same thing as being pressed into your seat when a plane takes off. Clear the floor first.
:::
