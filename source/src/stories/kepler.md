:::scene Hven, 1576

In 1576 the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe began building an observatory called Uraniborg on Hven, a small island in the strait between Denmark and Sweden. There were no telescopes yet; Galileo would not point one at the sky for another thirty-three years. Tycho measured positions with great sighting instruments and his own eyes, and he did it night after night for about twenty years: the Sun, the Moon, and above all the planet Mars.

What made Tycho different was not the instruments. It was his refusal to stop. Astronomers before him had taken a few good measurements and fitted a theory to them. Tycho measured everything, over and over, and his positions disagreed with the published tables of where the planets ought to be.

:::scene Prague, 1600

In February 1600 a young German mathematician called Johannes Kepler arrived in Prague to work with Tycho, who had moved there. The two did not have long together. Tycho died in October 1601, and Kepler ended up with the measurements, twenty years of them. (You may read that Tycho was poisoned. That is speculation; his remains were examined in 2010 and the result settled nothing.)

Kepler set out to find the true path of Mars.

#### Step 1: everyone knew it was a circle

Astronomers had always drawn the planets' paths as circles, or as circles carried round on other circles. A circle was the perfect shape and the heavens were supposed to be perfect. Nobody seriously asked whether the shape was right; they asked how to arrange the circles.

Kepler began the same way. He built the best circular orbit for Mars that he could, with every allowance the old methods permitted, and compared it with Tycho's positions.

#### Step 2: eight minutes of arc

The best circle missed. At some points Mars was eight minutes of arc away from where the circle said it should be.

How big is that? Astronomers measure positions in the sky as angles. A full turn is 360 degrees, and each degree is 60 minutes of arc. The full Moon is about 31 minutes of arc across. Eight minutes is about a quarter of the Moon's width.

:::work
Full Moon's width: 0.52 degrees = 0.52 × 60 = 31 minutes of arc.
Kepler's gap: 8 minutes of arc.
8 ÷ 31 = 0.26, about a quarter of a Moon's width.
:::

That is a small gap. Earlier astronomers would have lived with it, because measurements before Tycho were not good enough to argue with. But Kepler knew how carefully Tycho had worked, and he made a decision that is the whole story: he trusted the measurements more than he trusted the circle. If Tycho's Mars would not sit on a circle, then Mars did not move on a circle.

#### Step 3: the shape that fitted

After years of calculation Kepler found the shape. It is an ==ellipse==: the curve you get with two pins, a loop of string and a pencil. Push two pins into a board, loop the string loosely round both, pull it tight with the pencil and draw. Wherever the pencil goes, its distance to one pin plus its distance to the other adds up to the same total, the length of the string. The two pin positions are the ellipse's *foci*, a word Kepler himself gave them.

For a planet, the Sun sits at one focus. The other focus is empty space.

Here is the part most drawings get wrong. Mars's orbit is only very slightly oval. Its nearest distance to the Sun is 0.83 of its farthest, and drawn to scale the orbit looks like a circle with the Sun placed a little off centre, by about 9% of the orbit's radius. That off-centre Sun, not any visible squashing, is what Tycho's eight minutes had been pointing at. Kepler was lucky in his planet, too. Of the planets known then, only Mercury's orbit is more oval than Mars's; had he started with Venus or Earth, whose orbits are far rounder, Tycho's data would never have revealed the shape.

Right: Mars's orbit drawn to scale. It is almost a perfect circle; the clue was the Sun, sitting at one focus, about 9% of the way out from the centre.

#### Step 4: three laws

Kepler published the ellipse in 1609, in a book called *Astronomia Nova*, the New Astronomy, together with a second discovery. A planet does not move at a steady speed. It hurries when it is near the Sun and dawdles when it is far away, in such a way that a line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Ten years later, in 1619, he added a third rule linking a planet's year to its distance: the square of the time it takes to go round is proportional to the cube of its average distance from the Sun. Together these are ==Kepler's laws==.

:::work
**The third law, with Mars.** Measure distances in Earth–Sun distances (1 for Earth) and times in years. Then (years)² = (distance)³.
Mars takes 1.88 years to go round. So 1.88² = 3.53 = distance³, and the distance is the cube root of 3.53, about 1.52.
Mars is about 1.52 times as far from the Sun as Earth is: 1.52 × 150 million km ≈ 228 million km. Kepler could find that from a calendar and arithmetic, with no measuring tape to the planet.
:::

#### Step 5: how, but not why

Kepler's laws describe. They tell you what the planets do, to the limit of Tycho's eyes, and they say nothing about why. The why arrived seventy-five years later, when Edmond Halley asked Isaac Newton what path a body would take under a pull that weakens with the square of the distance, and Newton answered: an ellipse. The gravity story tells that part. Kepler's ellipses are the measurements that Newton's law had to explain, and did.

:::careful
> "Planets go round the Sun in long squashed ovals."

Only in diagrams, which exaggerate so that you can see the shape. Mars's orbit is a circle to the eye, and Earth's is rounder still. The real signature of an ellipse is not squashing; it is the Sun sitting off centre, at a focus.
:::

:::careful
> "The Sun is at the centre of the ellipse."

It is not. It sits at one of the two foci, the pins. The centre of the ellipse is empty, and so is the other focus. For Mars the Sun is about 9% of the orbit's radius away from the centre.
:::

:::home Draw Mars
Push two drawing pins into the flap of a cardboard box, 2 cm apart. Make a loop of thread about 22 cm round, hook it over both pins, pull it tight with a pencil and draw all the way round, keeping the thread taut. You have drawn Mars's orbit to scale: an ellipse so round that its far ends are only about half a millimetre short of a circle. Now move the pins 8 cm apart, keep the same loop, and draw again; that is the squashed shape the textbook pictures show. Finally put both pins in the same hole, and the ellipse becomes a circle.
:::
