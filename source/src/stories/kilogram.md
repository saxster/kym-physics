:::scene Sèvres, near Paris, 20 May 2019

In a basement vault outside Paris, under three nested glass bell jars, behind a door that needs three separate keys held by three separate people, sits a small cylinder of metal. It is 39 millimetres high and 39 millimetres across, about the size of a golf ball, made of nine parts platinum to one part iridium. It was made in London in 1879 by the firm of Johnson Matthey, and in 1889 the first General Conference on Weights and Measures chose it as the kilogram. Not *a* kilogram. The kilogram. Its official name is the International Prototype Kilogram; everyone calls it Le Grand K.

For 130 years its mass was one kilogram by definition. It could not be wrong, because it was the thing that "kilogram" meant. Every kilo of rice in Crawford Market, every weight on every chemist's balance, every satellite's mass traced back through a chain of comparisons to that cylinder. Six sister copies sat beside it in the vault. Countries held their own copies, the United States number 20, India number 57, and brought them to Sèvres about every forty years, in 1889, 1948 and 1989, to be compared with the original.

#### The problem

The comparisons showed something unsettling. The copies and the original were drifting apart. Over a century the original seemed to have lost about 50 micrograms, fifty millionths of a gram, relative to the average of its copies: roughly the mass of a fingerprint. "Seemed", because by definition it could not change. If Le Grand K had lost mass, then every kilogram in the world had lost mass with it, and nothing could tell you which had moved.

Nobody found a convincing cause. The copies might have gained from their surroundings; the original might have worn or absorbed something. It had its own cleaning ritual, rubbed with chamois leather soaked in ether and alcohol, steamed with twice-distilled water, then left for a week or more to settle, after which it slowly gained mass again, about a microgram a month at first. A unit that depends on how recently somebody wiped it is not a good unit.

:::work
**How much is 50 micrograms?** 50 µg is 0.000 05 g, and a kilogram is 1,000 g, so the drift is 0.000 05 ÷ 1,000 = 5 × 10⁻⁸ of the whole: fifty parts in a thousand million.
A kitchen scale that reads to 1 g would need 1 ÷ 0.000 05 = 20,000 such drifts piled up before it noticed. For rice, nothing. For the laboratories that compare the constants of nature to ten decimal places, everything.
:::

#### The fix

The second had already stopped being a fraction of a day and become a count of vibrations of a caesium atom, in 1967. The metre had gone from a fraction of the Earth's size, to a platinum bar, to the light of a krypton lamp, to the distance light travels in a fixed fraction of a second, in 1983. The kilogram was the last unit still defined by an object.

In November 2018, at Versailles, delegates from sixty countries voted to change it, and the change came into force on 20 May 2019, World Metrology Day, the anniversary of the treaty of 1875 that had created the whole system. The new rule fixes a number of nature instead of a lump of metal. The number is the ==Planck constant==, written *h*, and it is now exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ kilogram metre² per second. That number links the energy of a packet of light to the light's frequency, which is Chapter 15, and it is the same in every atom in the universe. Its units contain a kilogram. With the second and the metre already fixed, fixing *h* fixes the kilogram too. An instrument called a Kibble balance can then weigh an object against an electric force that is measured in terms of *h*, and any well-equipped laboratory on Earth can make its own kilogram from the rule, without a visit to a vault. On the same day the ampere, the kelvin and the mole were pinned to constants of nature as well.

:::fig s_kilogram_rule() | Since 2019 the kilogram has been defined by the fixed Planck constant. One way to realise it is a Kibble balance: in one mode a current supports a mass, and in a separate mode motion produces a voltage. Combining the measurements eliminates the magnetic-field and coil-length factor. The two modes are not simultaneous.

"The redefinition of the kilogram may seem like a small change, but it will have an enormous impact on science," said Ian Robinson of Britain's National Physical Laboratory, who had spent years building one of the balances. He also said this: "There will be no change to the mass scale currently used in trade and industry." A kilo of rice is still a kilo of rice. Le Grand K is still in its vault, under its three bell jars. It is now just a very well-made lump of metal whose mass can, for the first time, be measured.

:::careful
> "My weight is 40 kg."

Kilograms measure mass, the amount of stuff in you, which is the same in Mumbai, on the Moon and in orbit. Weight is the Earth's pull on that stuff, a force, and it is measured in newtons: about 390 N for 40 kg of you, and about 65 N on the Moon. Chapter 3 keeps the two apart. The cylinder in the vault was never about how hard the Earth pulls; it was about how much is there.
:::
