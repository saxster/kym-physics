:::scene Glasgow, 1761

Joseph Black lectured on chemistry at Glasgow, and in 1761 he showed his students something that everyone had seen and nobody had thought about. Take a bowl of ice that has just begun to melt, stand a thermometer in it, and leave it in a warm room. The ice melts, slowly, for hours. Warmth is plainly flowing in from the room, because the ice is turning to water. And the thermometer does not move. It sits at the melting point, 0 °C in our units, until the last sliver of ice is gone, and only then does it begin to climb.

Black never properly published what he made of this; it survives in the notes of his lectures. His argument was simple and strong. If melting ice took only a little heat, the first warm day of spring would turn every snowfield into a flood within the hour. Instead the snow lingers for weeks, melting a little each day. Melting, he concluded, must swallow a great deal of heat without showing any of it on a thermometer. He called that heat *latent*, which means hidden.

#### Step 1: a thermometer does not measure heat

Temperature is how fast the particles of a thing are jiggling, on average. Heat is energy flowing from something warmer to something cooler. Most of the time the two go together: pour energy into a pan of water and the particles jiggle faster, so the thermometer rises. Melting is the great exception, and it shows that temperature and heat are different things. Energy can go in while the temperature stands still.

#### Step 2: where the energy goes

In ice, the water particles are locked into a pattern, each held in place by its attraction to its neighbours. To become liquid, they have to be pulled loose from that pattern. That takes energy, and the energy goes into loosening the grips, not into making the particles move faster. So the thermometer, which reads only the speed of the jiggling, sees nothing happen. The energy is still there, stored in the new, looser arrangement. That stored energy is the ==latent heat==.

The same thing happens again, on a far larger scale, at the ==boiling point==. To turn liquid water into vapour, the particles must be pulled completely clear of one another, and that costs much more.

#### Step 3: how much

Melting one kilogram of ice at 0 °C takes about 334 kJ. That is as much energy as it would take to warm the same kilogram of water from 0 °C all the way to about 80 °C. Boiling one kilogram of water at 100 °C takes about 2256 kJ, nearly seven times as much as melting it. Put the two together with the ordinary warming in between, and you get the heating curve for a kilogram of ice taken from the freezer to steam.

:::work
**One kilogram, from −20 °C ice to steam**
Warming the ice to 0 °C: about 42 kJ.
Melting it, at a steady 0 °C: 334 kJ.
Warming the water from 0 to 100 °C: about 419 kJ.
Boiling it, at a steady 100 °C: 2256 kJ.
Total: about 3050 kJ, and three-quarters of it went into the two flat stretches.
:::

:::fig c8_heating_curve() | One kilogram of ice, from −20 °C to steam, drawn to scale. The temperature climbs, pauses at the melting point while 334 kJ go into melting, climbs again, and pauses at the boiling point while 2256 kJ go into boiling. On the flat stretches energy flows in and the thermometer does not move. That is Black's hidden heat.

The flat stretches are why ice is such a good way to keep a drink cold. Three small ice cubes, 6 g each, dropped into a 250 g glass of soda at 20 °C, bring it down to about 13 °C, and nearly all of that cooling comes from the melting, not from the ice being cold to begin with.

#### Step 4: it comes back out

Hidden heat is not gone. When water freezes, the 334 kJ per kilogram come back out as warmth; when vapour condenses, the 2256 kJ come back. We think of condensation as a cold thing, because we see it on cold glasses, but it *warms* whatever it forms on: the drops on the outside of a glass of iced water are handing energy to the glass and speeding up the melting inside. Fruit growers in cold places spray their orchards with water before a frosty night, because as the water freezes on the fruit it gives out latent heat and keeps the fruit near 0 °C instead of letting it drop below.

#### Step 5: the sweat on your arm

Evaporation at skin temperature costs even more than boiling: about 2430 kJ for every kilogram of sweat, nearly 8% more than at 100 °C. That is the hidden heat your body spends to cool you, and it is the whole working of the matka on the kitchen counter. Black's ice and a Mumbai summer are one piece of physics.

:::careful
> "The heat disappears while the ice melts, and comes from nowhere when the water freezes."

Nothing disappears. The energy that melts the ice is stored in the loosened arrangement of the liquid's particles, and it is paid back, every joule of it, when they lock into a pattern again. A thermometer cannot see it, because it only reads how fast the particles move, not how they are arranged.
:::

:::home Watch the thermometer refuse
Fill a steel tumbler with ice cubes and a little water, stir, and read a kitchen thermometer every minute. It stays at 0 °C while the ice shrinks: for ten minutes, twenty, as long as any ice is left, even though the warm room is pouring energy in the whole time. Note the minute the last sliver vanishes. From then on the reading climbs steadily. You have just repeated Black's lecture.
:::
