:::scene London, 24 November 1803

Thomas Young, thirty years old, let a narrow sunbeam into a darkened room. Into the beam he held a thin card, about a thirtieth of an inch wide (less than a millimetre), edge-on, so that the light passed on both sides of it. On the wall behind, inside the card's shadow, where it should have been darkest, he saw fine stripes: bright, dark, bright, dark. The light that had passed on the two sides of the card was meeting behind it, and where it met it was adding up in some places and cancelling in others.

That was a large claim to hang on a few stripes, because a century earlier Isaac Newton had argued that light is a stream of tiny particles, and Newton's word still carried enormous weight. Two streams of particles cannot cancel each other. Two waves can. If you have seen pictures of this experiment with light going through two narrow slits, that is a later tidy-up for textbooks; the thing Young did was hold a card in a sunbeam.

#### Step 1: the thing only a wave can do

Drop two stones into still water and watch where the two sets of ripples cross. Where a crest from one meets a crest from the other, the water lifts twice as high. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel and the water lies flat. The pattern of doubled and flattened places is ==interference==, and nothing but a wave can make it. Throw two balls at a wall and you get two thumps, never silence.

So if light shows interference, light is a wave. That single idea, that light travels as waves and does what waves do, is the ==wave theory of light==, and the stripes in the shadow were its first direct evidence.

#### Step 2: Young's card

Behind the card, light from its two edges overlaps. On the line straight behind the middle of the card, the two paths are equal, so the waves arrive in step, crest with crest, and that line is bright. Move a little to one side and one path is longer than the other. When it is longer by half a wavelength, a crest arrives with a trough and the two cancel: dark. Longer by a whole wavelength, and crests meet crests again: bright. So the shadow fills with stripes, one for every extra half-wavelength of path difference.

Light's wavelength is tiny, about 400 to 700 nanometres, which is some 1,800 waves to the millimetre for green light. That is why nobody had noticed before. In the textbook version, with two slits half a millimetre apart and a wall one metre away, the bright stripes come about a millimetre apart; Young's card gave stripes of a similar size. You need a small bright source, a dark room and patience, and Young had all three. From the spacing of the stripes, the wavelength of the light can be worked out.

#### Step 3: round the corner

The stripes raise a question. If light goes in straight lines, how did any of it get into the shadow at all? The answer is an idea from Christiaan Huygens, who did the arithmetic for the speed of light: every point on a wave's crest acts as a small new source of waves spreading out in all directions. Behind a wide opening the little waves add up to a wave going straight on, which is why light seems to travel in straight lines. At an edge they spill round the corner. This bending of waves round edges is ==diffraction==, and how far a wave bends depends on its wavelength. Sound waves are centimetres to metres long, so sound bends freely round the corner of a building and you hear traffic you cannot see. Light's waves are a thousandth of a millimetre, so light bends by amounts you never notice, until you hold something less than a millimetre wide in a sunbeam.

:::scene Paris, 1818

The decisive scene came fifteen years later. The French Academy of Sciences offered a prize for the best account of diffraction, and a young engineer, Augustin Fresnel, submitted a complete wave theory of light, with the mathematics to go with it. On the jury sat Siméon Poisson, a formidable mathematician and a particle man. Poisson worked through Fresnel's equations looking for a flaw, and found what he took to be a fatal one. Take a small round disc, light it with a single point of light, and look at its shadow. Fresnel's theory, Poisson showed, predicts a bright spot right at the centre of the shadow, where the shadow ought to be darkest of all. Absurd, said Poisson. The theory must be wrong.

François Arago set the experiment up and looked. There was the spot: a point of light in the middle of the shadow, exactly where the wave theory said it should be. Fresnel won the prize in 1819. Poisson was never convinced, and the spot is now called both the ==Poisson spot== and the ==Arago spot==, which is a fair reward for an honest wrong prediction and an honest look. It later turned out that Maraldi had seen the spot decades earlier without anyone grasping what it meant.

#### Step 4: why the spot is there

Picture the disc with its rim, and the point on the wall exactly behind its centre. Every point on the rim is the same distance from that one point, because the disc is round. So the little waves that spill round the rim all travel the same distance to reach the centre of the shadow, and they arrive in step, crest with crest, and add up: a bright spot. Move away from the centre and the distances from the different parts of the rim no longer match, so the waves mostly cancel and the shadow is dark, as shadows should be. Poisson's "absurd" spot is nothing but the roundness of the disc, read through Huygens's little waves.

:::fig s_arago_spot() | Poisson's prediction, Arago's observation. A small round disc, lit by one point of light, casts a shadow on the screen. The waves spilling round the rim have the same distance to travel to the centre of the shadow from every point on the rim, so they arrive in step and add up to a bright spot. Not to scale.

The same adding and cancelling paints a soap bubble. Light reflected from the outer surface of the thin film meets light reflected from the inner surface, and for each thickness of film some colours add up and others cancel, so the colours swirl as the film thins. Just before the bubble pops, the top goes black: the film there is so thin that the two reflections cancel for every colour at once.

:::careful
> "So Newton was simply wrong. Light is a wave, not particles."

Hold that thought loosely. These experiments show that light spreads and interferes exactly as a wave does, and that is true. In Chapter 15 you will meet experiments showing that light also arrives in packets, one at a time, which is not what a simple wave does. Both are true, and physicists learned to keep both.
:::

:::home Waves you can see
Blow soap bubbles in sunlight and watch the colours swirl, then watch one bubble closely until it pops: the top goes dark first. Tilt an old CD or DVD under a lamp and look at the rainbow on it. That rainbow is not a prism's sorting but diffraction at the thousands of fine tracks on the disc, which act as thousands of tiny edges, all spilling light round themselves in step. After rain, look for the rainbow colours in a thin film of oil on the road: the same adding and cancelling as the bubble.
