:::scene A kitchen in Mumbai, May

On the kitchen counter stands a matka, a round pot of unglazed clay, and in the heat of May the water inside it is cool. Not fridge-cold, but cool enough that a glass of it is a relief. Nobody plugged it in. Nobody put ice in it. The pot simply stands there, slightly damp on the outside, and does its work.

Then the monsoon arrives. The same pot, the same water, the same counter, and the water is barely cooler than the tap. People say the matka has "stopped working". It has not stopped doing anything. The air around it has changed, and the matka's trick depends on the air.

#### Step 1: the fastest leave

The particles in a glass of water are not all moving at the same speed. Most jiggle at some middling pace; a few are much faster. At the surface, the fastest ones occasionally move quickly enough to break away from their neighbours and escape into the air as vapour. That is evaporation, and it needs no boiling. It happens at any temperature, from a puddle after the rain to the sweat on your arm.

Here is the part that matters. The particles that leave are the *fast* ones. So the average speed of those left behind falls, and a falling average speed is a falling temperature. Evaporation cools whatever is left behind. The amount is large: each gram that escapes from warm water carries off about 2.4 kJ, which is as much energy as it would take to warm that gram by nearly six hundred degrees if it could stay liquid. This is ==evaporative cooling==, and it is the only part of the matka's trick that is about the pot.

#### Step 2: a pot that leaks on purpose

Fired clay is full of tiny pores. Water seeps through them from the inside to the outer surface, where it evaporates, and every gram that leaves takes its 2.4 kJ from the water that is left. A glazed pot of the same clay cannot sweat and cools nothing.

:::work
**How much water must a matka sweat?**
To cool 10 litres (10 kg) of water by 10 °C, the water must give up 10 × 4186 × 10 = 418,600 J.
Each gram evaporated carries away about 2430 J.
So about 418,600 ÷ 2430 ≈ 170 g must seep out and evaporate: a little less than a cupful, over the day.
:::

Your body runs the same system. A resting person gives out about 120 W of warmth, and to carry that away by sweating alone you would need to evaporate about 3 g of sweat every minute. On a dry day you hardly notice it happening, because the sweat is gone as soon as it forms. On a humid day it stays on your skin, and that is the clue to the whole puzzle.

#### Step 3: damp air pushes back

Evaporation is not one-way traffic. While fast particles leave the water, some of the vapour particles already in the air come back and rejoin it. At any given temperature, the air just above a water surface can only have so much vapour in it before the two flows balance: as many particles returning as leaving. Beyond that point, no more water evaporates, however long you wait.

How close the air is to that balance point is its relative ==humidity==. At 50%, the air has half the vapour it could have at that temperature, and evaporation runs briskly. At 100%, there is no net evaporation at all. People say the air "holds" water like a sponge, and that is a handy way to talk, but nothing is holding anything. It is a balance between particles leaving and particles returning.

On a Mumbai afternoon in May the humidity is typically around 65%. In July it is typically around 81%, and the air is already close to the balance point. The matka's water can still seep to the surface; it just cannot leave fast enough to do much cooling.

#### Step 4: the floor

So an evaporating surface cools, and damp air slows the cooling. Where does it stop? It stops when the thin layer of air right next to the wet surface has cooled and dampened to its own balance point. The temperature of the surface at that moment is the ==wet-bulb== temperature, named for the way it is measured: a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in wet cloth and fanned. No wet cloth, no sweating skin and no matka can go below it. It is the floor.

The floor depends on both the air temperature and the humidity, and it can be calculated. Here are three afternoons.

:::work
**Three afternoons, three floors**
Mumbai, May: air 34.3 °C, humidity 65%. Floor: 28.7 °C. The most a matka can cool is 34.3 − 28.7 = 5.6 °C.
Mumbai, July: air 29.4 °C, humidity 81%. Floor: 26.7 °C. The most it can cool is 2.7 °C.
A dry inland town: air 38 °C, humidity 20%. Floor: 21.3 °C. The most it can cool is 16.7 °C.
:::

:::fig s_wetbulb() | The air temperature and the wet-bulb floor for three afternoons. In May a Mumbai matka can get at most about 5.6 °C below the air; in July only about 2.7 °C, because the damp air has almost no room for more vapour. In a dry town the floor is nearly 17 °C below the air, which is why a matka there makes water that feels truly cold. The afternoon values are typical ones for Mumbai, and a real pot stays a little above its floor.

So May gives the matka about twice the room that July does, and even May is poor beside a dry town. A real pot never quite reaches its floor, because energy also leaks in from the warm room through the clay. Measured matkas drop their water by anywhere between 5 and 15 °C depending on the air; one measurement in humid Kolkata managed 7 °C.

#### Step 5: why the fan helps, and what it cannot do

Stand a wet-wrapped tumbler in front of a fan and it cools faster. Not because the fan's air is cold: it is at the room's temperature. The fan sweeps away the damp layer next to the cloth and brings drier air in to take its place, so evaporation keeps going. A fan on dry skin in a 30 °C room cannot take you below 30 °C. Moving air alone never cools anything below its own temperature. Evaporation can, and that difference is the whole secret of the matka.

:::careful
> "The fan makes the air colder, so I feel cool."

The air from the fan is at exactly the room's temperature. What the fan does is carry away the damp air next to your skin so that your sweat evaporates faster, and it is the evaporation that cools you, to a floor set by the humidity. Switch the fan off, the damp layer builds up again, evaporation slows, and the cooling fades.
:::

:::home Measure the floor yourself
Take a kitchen thermometer and wrap a scrap of cotton cloth, soaked in water, around its tip. Hold it in front of a fan and watch the reading fall, then settle. That lowest steady reading is the wet-bulb temperature of the air in your kitchen. Now read a dry thermometer beside it. The gap between the two is a measure of how dry the air is, and it is the most any matka in that room can hope to cool its water. Do it once in May and once in July and write both pairs of numbers down.
:::
