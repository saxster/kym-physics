:::scene Thumba, Kerala, 21 November 1963

On the coast of Kerala, beside a fishing village called Thumba, stood the church of St Mary Magdalene. In 1963 it became the first building of India's space programme. The church was the control room and the place where the rockets were put together, with the rockets laid out about where the altar had been. The story goes that parts arrived by bicycle and by bullock cart. On 21 November 1963 a small American-made research rocket, a Nike-Apache, rose from beside the church: India's first rocket launch.

The scientist who organised it was Vikram Sarabhai. The weather instruments on the site were Anna Mani's: that same year she built the meteorological tower at Thumba for Sarabhai, as part of her life's work of giving India weather instruments of its own.

A rocket beside a church raises an obvious question. A car pushes on the road. A swimmer pushes on the water. What does a rocket push on, once it is high above Thumba in thinning air, and later in space, where there is nothing at all?

#### Step 1: The quantity of motion

Start with the quantity Newton called the "quantity of motion" and we call ==momentum==: a body's mass multiplied by its velocity. A 0.16 kg cricket ball at 25 m/s has 0.16 × 25 = 4 kg·m/s of it. Two things about momentum matter here. First, it has a direction, the direction of the velocity, so momentum to the left and momentum to the right can add up to nothing. Second, it belongs to the moving thing. In the story of the arrow you saw that a moving thing carries no force; what it carries is momentum, and only a force can change it. A big force, or a small force acting for a long time, changes it a lot.

#### Step 2: Push, and you are pushed

Now bring in the third law. When two things push on each other, the forces are equal and opposite and last for exactly the same time. So the momentum one gains, the other loses, in equal measure. Add the two together before and after, and the total has not changed. That is the conservation of momentum: for a group of objects that nothing outside is pushing on, the total momentum stays the same, whatever they do to each other inside the group.

The catch is in "nothing outside is pushing on". A single cricket ball's momentum changes all the time, because the bat and the Earth and the air push on it. Take the ball, the bat, the batter and the Earth together, and the total is constant. Momentum never appears or disappears; it only moves between things, and if you have lost track of some, you have drawn the boundary of your group too small.

:::work
**Throwing a bag on a skateboard** (the inputs are round guesses).
You stand on a skateboard, at rest; you and the board together have a mass of 40 kg. You throw a 4 kg school bag forward at 5 m/s.
The bag's momentum: 4 × 5 = 20 kg·m/s, forward.
Before the throw the total was zero, and nothing outside pushed, so the total is still zero. Your momentum must be 20 kg·m/s *backward*: 40 × v = 20, so v = 0.5 m/s. You roll gently backward.
Every jump you make does the same to the Earth. Leaving the ground at 2 m/s with a mass of 40 kg, you give the Earth 80 kg·m/s the other way. Its mass is 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, so it recoils at 80 ÷ (5.97 × 10²⁴) = 1.3 × 10⁻²³ m/s. The Earth jumps when you do; it just does not jump far.
:::

#### Step 3: A rocket is a machine for throwing

That is the whole secret. A rocket pushes on nothing outside itself. It carries its own load of stuff to throw, the hot gas made by burning its fuel, and it throws that gas backward out of its tail as fast as it can. The rocket pushes the gas backward; the gas, by the third law, pushes the rocket forward. The rocket gains forward momentum exactly as fast as the gas gains backward momentum. Nothing else is needed. Not air, not ground, not a launch pad. A rocket works *better* in empty space than at Thumba, because air only gets in the way of the gas coming out.

:::fig s_rocket_momentum() | A rocket climbs by throwing gas downward. The rocket pushes the gas down; the gas pushes the rocket up, equally hard. The momentum the gas gains downward equals the momentum the rocket gains upward: a small mass at a very high speed one way, a large mass at a low speed the other. The air plays no part.

The gas is light and the rocket is heavy, so the gas must leave very fast for the rocket to gain much: small mass times big speed balancing big mass times small speed, just as the school bag balanced you. That is why rocket exhaust comes out in a roaring jet rather than a gentle puff. And because the rocket is throwing away part of itself, it gets lighter as it climbs, and the same push moves what is left more and more easily.

Sixty years after Thumba, on 23 August 2023, the lander of Chandrayaan-3 came down over the Moon's south polar region with its engines pointing at the ground, throwing gas downward to slow its fall, and touched down at 18:04 Indian time. The Moon has no air for an engine to push against. It did not need any. The gas pushed the lander, and the lander settled onto the dust at the spot later named Shiv Shakti Point.

#### Step 4: The balloon rocket

You have a rocket in the house. Blow up a balloon and let go without tying it. The air rushes out one way and the balloon shoots off the other. The common explanation is that the escaping air hits the room and bounces the balloon along; that is Aristotle's old mistake in a new costume. The balloon's stretched rubber squeezes the air out backward, and the air pushes the balloon forward as it leaves. The air that has already left is beside the point. It is the air on its way out, pushing on the balloon as it goes, that does the work.

:::careful
> "The rocket pushes against the air, or against the launch pad, to lift off."

It pushes against its own exhaust, and against nothing else. If it needed air, it would stop working at the top of the atmosphere, and it does not; it works better there. If it needed the pad, it could not steer or speed up once airborne, and it can. The thing being pushed on is the gas, which the rocket brought with it.
:::

:::home A rocket on a string
Thread a long piece of string through a drinking straw and tie the string tight across a room, between two chairs, with the straw free to slide. Blow up a balloon, pinch its neck shut, and tape the balloon to the straw with the neck pointing along the string. Let go. Watch which way the air goes and which way the balloon goes. Then try a smaller balloon, or the same balloon blown up less: less gas to throw, less momentum gained. Predict before each run how far it will get.
:::
