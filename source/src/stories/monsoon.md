:::scene London, 1686

In 1686 Edmond Halley, the astronomer who a year later would pay to print Newton's *Principia*, turned his attention from the sky to the wind. Over the Indian Ocean, sailors knew, the wind blows from the sea towards the land for half the year and from the land towards the sea for the other half, and the half when it blows in from the sea brings the rain. The ==monsoon== fed half the world, and nobody could say what drove it. Halley proposed an answer that is still the one most books give. To understand it, start with something you can feel on Marine Drive any afternoon.

#### Step 1: the breeze you can feel

In the late afternoon in Mumbai a cool wind comes in off the Arabian Sea. It comes because land and sea do not warm equally in the same sunshine. Warming a kilogram of water by one degree takes about 4186 J; warming a kilogram of dry sand by one degree takes only about 800 J. For the same sunshine, the sand warms about five times as much. By midday the ground is hot and the sea is barely changed.

#### Step 2: why warm air goes up

The air sitting over the hot ground warms, and warm air expands, so a cubic metre of it is lighter than a cubic metre of the cooler air around it. The cooler, denser air pushes the warm air upwards, exactly as water pushes a floating orange up to the surface in Chapter 6. Warm air does not rise by itself; it is pushed up by denser air. Cooler air from over the sea flows in along the ground to take its place, warms in turn, and is pushed up too. High up, the air drifts back out to sea and sinks. The result is a loop, a ==convection current==, and the moving fluid carries energy with it. That carrying of energy by a moving fluid is ==convection==.

The flow along the ground, from sea to land, is the ==sea breeze==. At night the land cools faster than the sea, the loop turns the other way, and a gentle wind blows out to sea: the land breeze.

:::fig c8_sea_breeze() | The sea breeze, by day. The same sunshine warms the land about five times faster than the sea. The air over the land warms and is pushed up by the denser air around it; cooler sea air flows in underneath; the loop closes high up. At night the land cools faster, and the whole loop reverses.

#### Step 3: Halley's leap

Halley's idea was to make the picture bigger. Take the whole of Asia as the land and the whole Indian Ocean as the sea, and let the year stand in for the day. Through the summer the land heats up more than the ocean, the air over the land is pushed upwards, and moist air flows in off the sea for months, not hours, bringing the rain with it. In winter the land cools faster than the ocean, and the whole system turns round: dry air flows out from the land to the sea. The monsoon, in Halley's picture, is a sea breeze the size of a continent, and the wind turns around twice a year for the same reason it turns around twice a day at Marine Drive.

It is a beautiful idea, and for three hundred years it was the explanation.

#### Step 4: measuring it

An idea about the wind cannot be tested without instruments to measure the wind, the rain, the temperature and the sunshine, in thousands of places, year after year. In India the person who made that possible was Anna Mani. She trained in meteorological instruments in London and joined the India Meteorological Department in 1948. She standardised the drawings for about a hundred of them so that India could make its own, set up a network of stations measuring the Sun's radiation for the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58, began measuring ozone in 1960, built the meteorological tower at Thumba for Vikram Sarabhai's rocket launches in 1963, and retired as a Deputy Director General in 1976. When the Department announces that the monsoon has reached Mumbai, around 11 June in a normal year, it is reading instruments that follow her drawings.

#### Step 5: the challenge

:::scene Bengaluru, 2021

Numbers are what allow a beautiful idea to be tested, and tested it was. In 2021 Sulochana Gadgil and her colleagues argued that Halley's giant sea breeze fails several tests when it is compared with what the instruments actually record. In their picture, the monsoon rains are better understood as the seasonal movement of a band of rain that circles the Earth near the equator. In the northern summer that band shifts north and sits over India; in winter it moves south again. The land-versus-sea heating is still there, but it is no longer the main character.

:::fig s_monsoon_two_ideas() | Two pictures of the monsoon, both schematic. Left, Halley's: the land heats more than the sea in summer, the air over it is pushed up, and moist air flows in from the ocean; in winter the flow reverses. Right, the 2021 challenge: a band of rain that circles the Earth near the equator moves north over the land in July and south again in January. Real coastlines and winds are far more complicated than either drawing.

The argument is not finished, and this book will not pretend it is. Nobody disputes the sea breeze on Marine Drive. What is disputed is whether the monsoon is a giant one. You are reading this in the middle of an unsettled scientific question, which is the ordinary condition of science: an idea that fitted for centuries, numbers that fit less well than people thought, and a better idea being argued over right now.

:::careful
> "Warm air rises because heat rises."

Heat does not rise; it is not a thing that can rise. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, and the denser air pushes it up, just as water pushes up anything less dense than itself. Take away the surrounding cooler air and warm air would go nowhere. The orange floating in Chapter 6 and the sea breeze on Marine Drive are the same physics.
:::

:::home Land and sea on a windowsill
Fill two identical steel tumblers, one with dry sand or soil and one with water, to the same depth, and stand them side by side in direct sunshine for an hour. Read a kitchen thermometer pushed into each. The sand will be far warmer. Then move both into the shade and read them every ten minutes: the sand cools fast, the water slowly. You have just made the land and the sea, and the reason the breeze turns around at night.
:::
