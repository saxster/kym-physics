:::scene Mumbai, any clear evening

Just after sunset, low in the west, a thin curve of light hangs over the sea: a new crescent Moon. A week later it is a half-disc high in the evening sky. A week after that a full Moon rises in the east as the Sun sets in the west. Then it shrinks again, rising later each night, until it is gone and the cycle starts over.

Ask people what the dark part of the crescent is and about four in ten will say it is the Earth's shadow. It is a sensible guess. It is also wrong, and finding out why teaches you more about the Moon than any list of phase names.

#### Step 1: half lit, always

The Moon makes no light of its own. It is a ball of rock in sunlight, and like any ball in sunlight one half of it is lit and the other half is dark. That is true every hour of every day. The Moon is never more or less than half lit. What changes is how much of the lit half is turned towards you.

#### Step 2: the orange and the lamp

You can see the whole cycle in a minute. Take an orange into a dark room with one lamp on. Hold the orange at arm's length. Now turn slowly on the spot, keeping the orange in front of you.

When you face the lamp, the orange is between you and the light and you see its dark side: that is new Moon. Turn a quarter of the way round and you see one half lit, one half dark: first quarter. Turn until the lamp is behind you and the orange is fully lit: full Moon. Keep turning and the lit part shrinks back through a half and a thin curve to nothing. At every moment half the orange is lit; you simply see it from a different angle. Your head stood for the Earth, and not once did its shadow touch the orange.

That is all the ==phases of the Moon== are: one lit half, viewed from different angles as the Moon goes round the Earth once a month. A phase thinner than a half is a ==crescent==; fatter than a half is ==gibbous==; the Moon is *waxing* while the lit part grows and *waning* while it shrinks.

:::fig s_phases_ring() | Seen from above: sunlight comes from the left, and the Moon at every point on its orbit is lit on the half that faces the Sun. Below each position is the shape we see from Earth. Not to scale: the Moon is really about 30 Earth-diameters away, which is why Earth's shadow almost never reaches it.

#### Step 3: why it is not Earth's shadow

Two facts kill the shadow idea. First, Earth's shadow points directly away from the Sun, like your own shadow on a sunny afternoon. At first quarter the Moon stands a right angle away from the Sun in the sky, nowhere near where the shadow points. A thin crescent is even closer to the Sun's side of the sky, which is why you see it in the west just after sunset. The dark part of the crescent is simply the Moon's own night side, turned towards you.

Second, when Earth's shadow *does* fall on the Moon, we have a name for it: a ==lunar eclipse==. It can happen only at full Moon, when the Moon is on the far side of Earth from the Sun, and even then it happens only now and then. If phases were Earth's shadow, the full Moon would be the dark one, not the bright one.

#### Step 4: the month

The Moon goes once round the Earth relative to the stars in 27.3 days, but the cycle of phases takes a little longer, 29.5 days, because in that time Earth has moved some way round the Sun and the Moon needs a couple of extra days to catch up with the new direction of the sunlight.

:::work
Each day the Moon moves 360° ÷ 29.5 ≈ 12° further round the sky relative to the Sun.
Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so it takes 24 × 60 × 12 ÷ 360 ≈ 49 extra minutes to bring the Moon back over the horizon.
That is why the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, and why the full Moon, which sits opposite the Sun, rises at sunset and stands highest at midnight. The first-quarter Moon rises around noon.
:::

#### Step 5: eclipses, and why they are rare

An eclipse is a shadow, which is exactly what Aryabhata said in the year 499. A ==solar eclipse== is the Moon's shadow falling on Earth; it can happen only at new Moon, when the Moon is between us and the Sun. A lunar eclipse is Earth's shadow falling on the Moon, at full Moon.

So why isn't there an eclipse every month, at every new and full Moon? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° compared with Earth's orbit round the Sun. Most months, at new Moon, the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun in the sky and its shadow misses Earth; at full Moon it passes above or below Earth's shadow. The tilt sounds small, but at the Moon's distance it lifts the Moon as much as 34,000 km off the line, and Earth is only 6,371 km in radius. The shadow misses by a long way. Only twice a year, when the line where the two orbits cross points towards the Sun, do new and full Moons line up well enough for eclipses, and those few weeks are called eclipse seasons.

:::fig s_eclipse_tilt() | Side view: the Moon's orbit is tilted to Earth's orbit, so in most months the full Moon passes above or below Earth's shadow and the new Moon's shadow misses Earth. The real tilt is 5°, exaggerated here to 15° so you can see it; at the Moon's distance 5° is a miss of up to 34,000 km.

One more coincidence makes solar eclipses so dramatic. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and also about 400 times farther away, so the two look almost the same size in our sky, about half a degree each. The Moon can just cover the Sun. During a lunar eclipse the Moon does not go black but a deep coppery red, because some sunlight bends through Earth's atmosphere into the shadow, the same bending that makes sunsets orange in Chapter 11.

One warning that is not optional. Never look at the Sun, eclipse or no eclipse: not with your eyes, not through sunglasses, not through anything dark. Even a brief look can damage your eyes. The safe way to watch a partial solar eclipse is a pinhole: a 1 mm hole in a card, with the Sun's image projected onto a second card held behind it.

:::careful
> "The dark part of the Moon is the Earth's shadow."

Earth's shadow points straight away from the Sun. A crescent Moon is on the *Sun's* side of the sky; the shadow is nowhere near it. The dark part is the Moon's own night. Earth's shadow does reach the Moon now and then, and that is a lunar eclipse, which happens only at full Moon.
:::

:::careful
> "The far side of the Moon is the dark side."

The Moon keeps the same face towards Earth, so there is a far side we never see from here. But it is lit for half of every month, like every other part of the Moon: at new Moon the far side is in full sunlight. There is a far side. There is no permanently dark side.
:::

:::home The orange and the lamp
Find a dark room with a single lamp, or a phone torch propped on a shelf. Stand about two metres from it, hold an orange at arm's length a little above your head so that your own shadow does not fall on it, and turn slowly on the spot. Watch the lit part of the orange: new, crescent, half, gibbous, full, and back again. Stop at first quarter and check where the lamp is: a right angle away from the orange, exactly as the real first-quarter Moon is a right angle from the Sun.
:::
