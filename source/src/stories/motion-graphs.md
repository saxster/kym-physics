:::scene A corridor in a Mumbai flat, this afternoon

Stick a strip of masking tape on the floor every metre along the corridor, eight of them. A friend stands at the end with a phone stopwatch and calls out the seconds. You walk: steadily for four metres, then stop for two seconds, then walk faster for the last four metres. The whole thing takes eight seconds. Now turn that walk into a picture.

#### Step 1: walk it, write it

Every time your friend calls a second, note which tape you are nearest. You get a short table: at 0 s you were at 0 m, at 4 s at 4 m, at 6 s still at 4 m, at 8 s at 8 m. That table *is* the motion. Everything else is a way of looking at it.

#### Step 2: plot it

Time goes along the bottom, always. Distance walked goes up the side. Mark the four points and join them. You have a ==distance–time graph==, and its shape already tells the story: a line sloping up, then a flat stretch, then a line sloping up more steeply.

#### Step 3: read the slope

The ==slope== of a line (at school it is called the ==gradient==) is how much it rises for each step along. On this graph, rising means metres and stepping along means seconds, so the slope is metres per second: a speed. Steeper means faster. Flat means stopped.

:::work
**Reading the three slopes**
First stretch: rose 4 m in 4 s, so the speed was 4 ÷ 4 = 1 m/s.
Flat stretch: rose 0 m in 2 s, so the speed was 0.
Last stretch: rose 4 m in 2 s, so the speed was 4 ÷ 2 = 2 m/s.
:::

A straight line means a steady speed. A line that *curves* means the speed is changing. When Galileo rolled balls down a ramp (Chapter 1) the distance grew with the square of the time, and his graph would bend upward, steeper and steeper, because the ball kept getting faster. On a curved graph, the speed at any instant is the slope of the straight line that just touches the curve there.

#### Step 4: the other graph

Now plot the speeds you just read, against time. One metre per second for four seconds, zero for two, two for two. This is a ==velocity–time graph==, and it looks like a set of steps. Its trick is the opposite of the first graph's. There the slope gave you speed. Here the *area* under the line gives you distance, because a speed in metres per second, multiplied by a time in seconds, is a distance in metres.

:::work
**Reading the areas**
First step: 1 m/s × 4 s = 4 m.
Flat at zero: 0 m.
Last step: 2 m/s × 2 s = 4 m.
Total: 8 m, which is exactly where the top graph ended.
:::

:::fig s_walk_graphs() | The same walk as two graphs. Above, distance against time: the slope of each straight stretch is the speed, 1 m/s, 0 and 2 m/s. Below, velocity against time: the area under each step is the distance covered, 4 m, 0 and 4 m, adding up to the 8 m where the top graph ends. The dashed lines mark the moments the walk changed.

#### Step 5: three traps

The graph is not a map. The horizontal axis is time, so a line that curves does not mean you turned a corner; the corridor is dead straight. Keep saying "time" to yourself as you read along the bottom.

Two lines crossing mean two walkers at the same place at the same time. They do not mean the same speed. More than a third of students say they do, and it is an easy slip, because the lines look as if they meet "as equals". The speeds are the slopes, and at a crossing the slopes are usually different.

Walking back is where distance and position part company. Distance walked only ever adds up, so a distance–time graph never comes down. A graph of your *position* along the corridor does come down when you turn round, with a downhill slope, as the walk in Chapter 1's figure shows. Check which kind of graph you are reading before you read it.

:::careful
> "The line on the graph curves, so the path must have curved."

The bottom axis is time, not distance across the floor. A curve means the speed was changing; a straight line means it was steady. Someone walking in a perfectly straight corridor but speeding up draws a curved line. Someone walking round a bend at a steady pace draws a straight one.
:::

:::home Draw your own
Lay out tape marks every metre in a corridor or along a compound wall, and have a friend call the seconds from a phone stopwatch. Walk the pattern above and plot it on squared paper. Then walk a new pattern without telling your friend what it will be, plot it, and see whether they can read your walk back from the graph alone: where you stopped, where you hurried. Finally, start as slowly as you can and speed up smoothly: watch the line bend.
:::
