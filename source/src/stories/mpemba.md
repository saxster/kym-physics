:::scene Magamba Secondary School, Tanzania, 1963

The class was making ice cream. You boil milk, stir in sugar, let the mixture cool, and put it in the freezer. Erasto Mpemba, a Form 3 student, put his in while it was still hot. And his froze first, before the mixtures of classmates who had patiently let theirs cool.

He said so. The class laughed. His physics teacher told him, "You were confused. That cannot happen." Everybody knows that hot water takes longer to freeze than cold water, because it has further to cool. Mpemba had seen what he had seen, and he did not let it go.

#### The lecturer who listened

A few years later, at Mkwawa Secondary School in Iringa, a physicist from University College in Dar es Salaam, Dr Denis Osborne, came to give a talk. Afterwards Mpemba asked him a question. If you take two equal amounts of water, one at 35 °C and one at 100 °C, and put them in a freezer, the hot one freezes first. Why?

Osborne thought the boy must be mistaken. But he went back to his laboratory and tried it, and it happened. In 1969 the two of them published a short paper together in the journal *Physics Education*, with the title "Cool?", Mpemba by then a student at a college of wildlife management. They had put 70 ml of water in beakers on a block of foam in the icebox of an ordinary refrigerator, starting at different temperatures, and timed how long each took to begin freezing. Water starting at 25 °C took longest. Water starting near 90 °C took much less. They checked that the hot water had not simply boiled away, and that dissolved air was not the answer. Osborne called the tests crude and asked for better ones. He got fifty years of them.

#### An old puzzle

It turned out to be an ancient observation. Aristotle wrote that water which has been warmed freezes more quickly. Francis Bacon wrote that "slightly tepid water freezes more easily than that which is utterly cold". Descartes had a theory about it. Joseph Black, in 1775, compared water that had been boiled with water that had not. Nobody had settled it, and the schoolboy's name went on it because he was the one who refused to be laughed out of it.

#### Why it is so strange

Here is why physicists found it hard to believe, and it is a good piece of reasoning. Temperature is one number. Hot water cooling towards freezing has to pass through the cold water's starting temperature on its way down. If, at that moment, the hot water were the same as the cold water in every other way, it could not possibly win: from there on the two would be identical, and the hot one would be behind by however long it took to get there.

So if the effect is real, the hot water must arrive at that temperature *different* in some way that matters. There are candidates. Some of it has evaporated, so there is less to freeze. It has been stirred by currents as it cooled, while the cold water sat still. It holds less dissolved gas. It has melted the frost under its container, so it sits in better contact with the cold shelf. Its molecules may be arranged a little differently. Each of these has been proposed; none has been shown to be the answer.

#### The argument

In 2016 two physicists at Cambridge, Henry Burridge and Paul Linden, measured the time for water to cool to 0 °C with great care and found that the dramatic effects reported earlier vanished. Worse, they found that a thermometer sitting a single centimetre higher or lower in the water could produce a false effect all by itself, because the top of a cooling cup is not at the same temperature as the bottom. Their verdict was that there is "no evidence to support meaningful observations of the Mpemba effect", though they noted that the 1969 result was too large to be explained by that mistake.

In 2020, Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer showed the effect cleanly, not in water but in the simplest system they could build: a single tiny glass bead, held in water by laser light, nudged thousands of times from a "hot" start and a "warm" start. The hot start settled faster. So something like the effect is real in some systems, and understood there. For water, the honest answer in 2022, from a journalist who asked all of them, was: "Whether hot or cold water freezes faster remains unknown."

This book promised to say plainly what nobody knows. This is one of those things.

:::careful
> "Hot water freezes faster than cold. Everyone knows that."

Nobody knows that. Careful experiments mostly fail to find it, and the ones that find it do not agree about why. The equally confident "that was disproved years ago" is also too strong: it has been found, cleanly, in systems that are not water. The true statement is the uncomfortable one: for water, after fifty years, it is unsettled.
:::

:::home A fair test
Take two identical cups and put exactly the same amount of water in each, say 100 ml, one from the cold tap and one from the warm tap. Put them side by side on the same shelf of the freezer at the same moment and look every ten minutes for the first skin of ice. Then do it again with the cups' positions swapped. You will probably not see the effect, and that is not the point. The point is what Burridge and Linden learned: anything that differs between the two cups except the starting temperature, their position, their shape, how deep a thermometer sits, can produce a result that looks like physics and is not.
:::
