:::scene Florence, 1643

In the 1640s everyone who built pumps knew an annoying fact, and nobody could explain it. A pump will lift water out of a well, but only to a height of about ten metres. Make the pipe longer and the water simply stops rising, as if ten metres were the end of its rope.

In Florence, Evangelista Torricelli tried the problem with a heavier liquid. He filled a tube with mercury, turned it upside down into a dish of mercury and opened the end. The mercury ran down the tube, but not all the way. It stopped when the column stood about 76 centimetres above the mercury in the dish, and above it, at the top of the tube, was a space with nothing in it. In a letter dated 11 June 1644, Torricelli wrote the sentence this story is named for: *Noi viviamo sommersi nel fondo d'un pelago d'aria.* We live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air.

#### Step 1: What holds the mercury up

The first thought almost everyone has is that the empty space at the top is *sucking* the mercury up. Torricelli's thought was the opposite, and it is the heart of the matter. Empty space has nothing in it, so it has nothing to pull with. What there is plenty of is air, pressing on the open surface of the mercury in the dish. We live at the bottom of a deep sea of air, and like water at the bottom of a sea, the air at the bottom is under pressure from all the air above it. That ==atmospheric pressure== pushes down on the dish and so pushes mercury *up* the tube, and it keeps pushing until the weight of the column in the tube balances it. Then it stops. The height of the column is a measure of how hard the air is pushing: Torricelli had invented the ==barometer==.

:::fig s_torricelli() | Torricelli's tube. The air presses on the open mercury in the dish and pushes mercury up the tube until the column's own weight balances the push, at about 76 cm. Above the column is a space with almost nothing in it, which pulls on nothing: the column is held up from below, not from above. The same push would hold up 10.3 m of water, which is why the water tube on the right is drawn with a break in it.

#### Step 2: Weighing the air

Now the pump makes sense. Mercury is 13.6 times as dense as water, so the air that can hold up 76 cm of mercury can hold up 13.6 × 0.76 = 10.3 m of water, and no more. A pump does not pull water up a pipe. It takes air out of the top of the pipe, and the air pressing on the well pushes the water up, until the column weighs as much as the air can hold. Ten metres is the end of the air's rope, not the water's.

You can also weigh the air. The pressure at sea level, about 101,000 newtons on every square metre, means that the column of air standing on a one-centimetre square, all the way to the top of the atmosphere, weighs about 10 newtons: it has a mass of about a kilogram. On a square metre that comes to about ten tonnes. Ten tonnes of air stands on every square metre of Marine Drive, and on every square metre of you. You are not crushed because the air pushes on every surface in every direction, up and sideways as well as down, and because the air and fluids inside your body push outward just as hard. Air pressure only hurts when it is unbalanced, which is the whole lesson of Step 4.

#### Step 3: Up the mountain

:::scene Puy de Dôme, 19 September 1648

If the air is an ocean with weight, then on a mountain there should be less of it above you, and the barometer should read lower. In France, Blaise Pascal wanted the test done and was too ill to climb, so on 19 September 1648 his brother-in-law Florin Périer carried a mercury tube up the Puy de Dôme, a mountain near Clermont. Périer did something that every good experiment since has copied: he left a second, identical tube at the bottom, in Clermont, with a priest watching it all day. On the summit the mercury stood about three inches, some 8 cm, lower than it had at the bottom. Back in Clermont, the tube that stayed behind had not moved all day. So the drop was not the weather and not the time of day. It was the mountain.

:::work
**How much air did Périer climb above?** Eight centimetres of mercury is a pressure of 13,600 × 9.8 × 0.08 ≈ 10,700 newtons per square metre.
Sea-level pressure is about 101,300, so the barometer had fallen by 10,700 ÷ 101,300 ≈ 10.5 per cent. Roughly a tenth of the ocean of air was below Périer's feet.
:::

#### Step 4: The horses

:::scene Regensburg, 1654

Otto von Guericke had built a pump that could take the air *out* of a vessel. In Regensburg in 1654, or a little later (the accounts disagree), he fitted two copper hemispheres together, pumped the air out of the inside, and hitched teams of horses to the two halves. The horses could not pull them apart. How many horses depends on who is telling the story: a letter of 1657 says twelve, most popular accounts say sixteen, and one says thirty. Say "teams of horses" and you are safe.

Nothing inside the ==Magdeburg hemispheres== was holding them together, because there was almost nothing inside. The air *outside* was pushing in, on every square centimetre of copper, with nothing inside to push back. If the hemispheres were half a metre across (a round guess; the real size is not in this book's sources), the area the air pushes on is about 0.2 m², and 0.2 × 101,000 ≈ 20,000 newtons, the weight of about two tonnes, holds the halves together. Let the air back in through a valve and the two halves come apart with no effort at all.

:::fig s_hemispheres() | The Magdeburg hemispheres. With the air pumped out there is nothing inside to push outward; the air outside pushes inward on every part of the surface, and the horses have to beat that whole push. For hemispheres half a metre across the push is roughly 20,000 newtons, about the weight of two tonnes.

#### Step 5: Nothing sucks

Every one of these stories looks, at first, like something *pulling*: the empty space pulling mercury up the tube, the pump pulling water up the pipe, the empty hemispheres pulling each other shut, your mouth pulling a drink up a straw. In every case the truth is a push from the side with more air. A ==vacuum== is a space with nothing, or almost nothing, in it, and nothing cannot pull. When you drink through a straw you lower the air pressure inside your mouth, and the ordinary air pressing on the surface of your drink pushes the drink up to fill the gap. Even a perfect vacuum in your mouth could lift water only 10.3 m; nobody drinks through a three-storey straw. Fill a glass to the brim, lay a card on it, turn it over above the sink and the card stays: the air pushes up on a card 10 cm square with about 1,000 newtons, the weight of a hundred kilograms, against a few newtons of water. ==Suction== is not a force. It is the name people give to a push they cannot see.

:::careful
> "The straw sucks the drink up into my mouth."

You cannot pull on a liquid with empty space. What you do is remove some air from your mouth, so that the air pushing on the drink in the glass is pushing harder than the air left in your mouth. The drink moves from the harder push towards the softer one. Nothing pulls; the room's air does the pushing, down on the glass and up the straw.
:::

:::school
The word "suction" does not appear in your IGCSE syllabus. In an exam, explain that the air pressure outside is bigger and pushes. The syllabus does have Δp = ρgΔh: that is Torricelli's column, with a deeper liquid giving a bigger pressure.
:::

:::home The card and the straw
Over the sink: fill a glass to the brim with water, lay a stiff, wet postcard flat on top so that it seals, hold the card in place with your palm and turn the glass upside down. Take your hand away. The card stays. Then dip a straw in a glass of water, put a fingertip over the top, and lift it out: the water stays in the straw until you lift your finger and let air in at the top. Name the pusher each time.
:::
