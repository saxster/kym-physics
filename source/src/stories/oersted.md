:::scene Copenhagen, 21 April 1820

On 21 April 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted was giving a lecture in Copenhagen with a battery and a wire on the bench, and a compass lying nearby. When he connected the wire so that a current flowed, the compass needle twitched. It moved so slightly that the audience did not notice. Ørsted did.

Over the following weeks he tried it again and again. A wire carrying a current, laid over a compass, pushed the needle aside. Reverse the current, and the needle swung the other way. Take the current away, and it settled back to north as if nothing had happened. In July he published what he had found, in four pages of Latin, and sent them to scientists across Europe.

Why did a twitch matter so much? For two hundred years, since Gilbert kept them apart in *De Magnete*, electricity and magnetism had been two separate subjects. Rubbed amber lifted paper; lodestones lifted iron; neither did anything to the other. Now a current, which is charge in motion, had moved a magnet. The two subjects were one.

:::scene Paris, September 1820

The four pages reached Paris at the end of the summer. On 4 September François Arago told the Académie des Sciences about the experiment, and on 11 September he repeated it in front of the members. One of them was André-Marie Ampère. A week later, on 18 September, Ampère presented his first work on what he named electrodynamics, and in that week he had already made the leap that turned the discovery inside out. If a current acts like a magnet, he proposed, then perhaps every magnet is a bundle of currents: the magnetism of a lodestone or a compass needle is itself caused by electric currents circulating inside it. He also gave a rule, pictured as a little man lying along the wire, for working out which way a nearby needle must turn. Two centuries later that is still, in essence, how physics sees it.

#### Step 1: moving charge, not charge

A charged balloon does nothing to a compass. You can hold a rubbed comb against a compass case all afternoon and the needle will not stir. Let the charge *move*, as a current in a wire, and the needle turns. That is the first half of electromagnetism in one sentence: magnetism comes from charges that are moving.

#### Step 2: the shape of the field around a wire

Map the field around a long straight wire with a compass and you find circles. The field lines loop round the wire, with no beginning and no end, and a needle anywhere on a circle points along it. Point the thumb of your right hand along the current and your curled fingers show the way the field goes; reverse the current and the circles run the other way, which is why Ørsted's needle swung the other way.

The field is weaker farther from the wire. Its strength is B = μ₀I/(2πr): double the distance, half the field.

Ørsted's batteries gave small currents, which is why his needle only twitched.

#### Step 3: from a wire to a magnet

Wind the wire into a coil and the circles around each turn add up. Sprinkle iron filings around a current-carrying coil and you get the same two-ended pattern you get around a bar magnet: a north end, a south end, loops between. Slide an iron bar inside and the field is far stronger, because the iron's own tiny magnets line up with it. That is an electromagnet, and it is a magnet only while the current flows. Switch off, and the pull mostly vanishes; reverse the current, and north and south swap.

Ampère's leap closes the circle. Why is a fridge magnet a magnet, with no battery attached? Because its electrons behave like tiny magnets, and in some materials they line up. Physicists sometimes draw this as electrons spinning or orbiting like little loops of current. Both pictures are crude, and the people who draw them say so; the true account is quantum, and comes later. But the one-line summary holds: every magnetic field, including the Earth's and the fridge magnet's, comes from moving charge.

#### Step 4: a test that fools almost everyone

Lay two wires side by side and send currents along both in the same direction. Do they push apart, as two like charges would, or pull together? They pull together. Currents in the same direction attract; currents in opposite directions repel. "Likes repel" is a rule for charges, not for currents, and it is one of the first things Ampère worked out.

:::careful
> "Electricity and magnetism are the same thing."

Not quite. A charge sitting still has no magnetic effect on a compass. A *moving* charge does. The honest sentence is: magnetism comes from charges that are moving, and, as Chapter 13 will show, a changing magnetic field pushes charges in turn.
:::

:::careful
> "Field lines are something that streams out of the wire."

They are a drawing. Each line records the direction a compass would point at that spot. Nothing flows along them, and no particle rides them like a rail.
:::

Ørsted had shown that a current moves a magnet. The obvious next question, whether a magnet can be made to move charge and drive a current, took eleven more years and another man at a bench in London.
