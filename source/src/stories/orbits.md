:::scene Cambridge, 1687

In 1687 Isaac Newton published the *Principia*, the book in which the fall of an apple and the orbit of the Moon became one rule. A second account of the same ideas, *The System of the World*, was printed in 1731, four years after his death, and it contains a drawing that has been copied into physics books ever since: a mountain, impossibly tall, with a cannon on top.

Nobody has ever fired that cannon. It is a thought experiment, and it is still the clearest explanation of an orbit anyone has given.

#### Step 1: the cannon on the mountain

Put the mountain so high that there is no air up there to slow anything down, and fire the cannon exactly sideways. After the ball leaves the barrel only one thing acts on it: gravity, pulling it down. The ball curves towards the ground and lands some way off.

Fire harder. The ball travels further before it lands. Harder again, and further again. Now notice something about the ground. Earth is round, so as the ball travels, the ground beneath it curves away. A slow ball reaches the ground long before that matters. But a fast enough ball falls towards the ground at exactly the rate the ground curves away beneath it. It falls, and keeps falling, and never gets any nearer the surface. It goes all the way round and arrives back at the mountain from behind. That is an ==orbit==: a fall that keeps missing.

At Earth's surface the speed that does this is about 7.9 km every second; call it 8 km/s. Fire faster than that and the ball swings out into a longer loop, an ellipse, coming back round to the mountain each time. Fire at about 11 km/s and it never comes back at all: it has escaped.

:::work
**How fast is 8 km/s?**
The Bandra–Worli Sea Link is 5.6 km long.
At 7.9 km/s the cannonball would cross the whole bridge in 5.6 ÷ 7.9 = 0.7 seconds.
:::

:::fig s_newton_cannon() | Newton's cannon. Four balls fired sideways from the same mountain top, with no air. Too slow and they curve down to the ground; at the right speed the ground curves away as fast as the ball falls and it goes round for ever; faster still and it swings out into a longer loop. The dashed line is where a ball would go if gravity were switched off. Each path is the true orbit shape under an inverse-square pull, drawn to scale with the Earth; only the mountain is impossibly tall.

#### Step 2: nothing holds a satellite up

The cannonball shows what every satellite is doing. Nothing holds it up. No engine pushes it along. Once it is above the air there is nothing to slow it, so it keeps its sideways speed, and gravity keeps bending its path round. It is falling, continuously, and continuously missing. The hard part of space travel is not staying up; it is reaching that sideways speed in the first place.

The International Space Station, the ==ISS==, is a cannonball of this kind: about 400 km above the ground, moving at about 7.7 km/s, once round Earth every 92 minutes or so, which is fifteen or sixteen sunrises a day. Even at 400 km there is a whisper of air, which is why very low orbits slowly decay; but that is friction's doing, not gravity's.

One more thing the cannon tells you: the ball's mass never came into it. A heavy ball and a light ball fired at the same speed follow the same path, just as the two coins in Chapter 4 landed together. Every satellite at the same height goes at the same speed, whether it is a one-kilogram cube or the 420-tonne station.

#### Step 3: why the astronauts float

Here is the puzzle the whole of Chapter 5 is built around. Gravity at the height of the station is not zero. Work it out: the station is 6,371 + 400 = 6,771 km from Earth's centre, against 6,371 km for you.

:::work
Pull at the station compared with the ground = (6,371 ÷ 6,771)² = 0.885.
Gravity up there is about 89% as strong as in your kitchen.
:::

So why do the astronauts float?

Think about a lift whose cable has snapped. The lift falls; you fall with it; the floor drops away from under your feet at exactly the rate you fall, so it stops pressing on them. Let go of a pen and it hangs beside you, because it is falling too. For as long as the fall lasts, you float. Gravity has not gone anywhere. Gravity is doing the pulling.

The station is a lift whose cable has snapped, except that it is moving sideways at 7.7 km/s, so it falls *around* Earth and the fall never ends. Everyone and everything inside is falling together. Nothing presses on anyone's feet. And the press of the floor on your feet is the thing you actually feel as weight: take it away and you feel ==weightless==, with gravity at 89% all the while.

:::school
At school, weight means the gravitational force on a mass. In that language an astronaut on the ISS still *has* weight, about 89% of her weight on the ground. What she has lost is the feeling of it, because nothing is holding her up. Say "feels weightless" and you are right in both languages.
:::

#### Step 4: your phone can feel it

The free phyphox app reads your phone's motion sensor. Open "Acceleration (with g)" and lay the phone on a table: it reads about 9.8 m/s². That is not gravity it is sensing; it is the table pushing up. The live free-fall trace in Chapter 5 shows what happens next, without risking your phone: during a short fall the sensor reading drops to about zero. For a third of a second your phone is exactly where the astronauts are: falling, with nothing pushing on it, weightless with gravity at full strength.

:::careful
> "There's no gravity up there. That's why they float."

Then what bends the station's path into a circle fifteen or sixteen times a day? Gravity at 400 km is 89% of ground strength. The astronauts float because they and the station are falling together, not because the pull has stopped. The lift with the snapped cable is the honest picture.
:::

:::careful
> "Satellites stay up because they're above the air."

Being above the air only stops them slowing down. What keeps them up is sideways speed: about 7.7 km/s at 400 km, enough to keep missing the ground. Take that speed away and a satellite would fall straight down like a dropped stone, air or no air.
:::

:::home The weightless phone
The live trace in Chapter 5 lets you inspect this experiment without dropping a phone. For a physical test, ask an adult first and use only a phone they agree can be dropped onto a thick cushion. Install phyphox and open "Acceleration (with g)". Put the phone flat on a table and read about 9.8 m/s². Now hold it about 40 cm above a soft pillow or a folded blanket, start recording, and let it drop. Look at the graph: the reading falls to almost zero for about a third of a second, then jumps as the pillow catches it. That dip is free fall, the same thing an astronaut feels all day. Keep the drop small and the landing soft.
