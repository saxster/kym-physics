:::scene Kusumapura, Patna, 499 CE

In 499 CE, in Kusumapura near modern Patna, a twenty-three-year-old astronomer and mathematician named Aryabhata finished a short work in Sanskrit verse called the *Aryabhatiya*. We know his age because he wrote it into a verse of the book: twenty-three years had passed since his birth when that age of the world reached 3,600 years, which places his birth in 476 CE. Some historians date the final text nearer 510 CE, and its arguments survived across India in commentary after commentary.

#### The river shore that slides backwards

Anyone who watches the night sky for an hour sees the stars drift steadily from east to west. To almost every astronomer of the ancient world, the explanation seemed obvious: the Earth sits still in the middle, and the dome of the sky spins around it once a day.

Aryabhata offered a different picture, and he explained it with a boat. When a person in a boat moves forward down a river, he wrote, the stationary trees and stones on the bank appear to move backwards in the opposite direction. In the same way, the stationary stars appear to drift westwards from Lanka, at the equator, because the Earth itself turns towards the east.

The boat analogy separates what your eyes see from what is actually moving. Watching the stars rise and set cannot by itself tell you whether the sky turns around a still Earth or the Earth turns inside a still sky. Both models give the same view from the ground. You do not feel the Earth's spin for the same reason you do not feel a steady glide in a boat or a local train cruising at constant speed: as you saw in Chapter 2, your body detects changes in velocity, not steady motion itself.

#### Shadows in place of Rahu

Aryabhata applied the same geometry to eclipses. Popular tradition blamed eclipses on Rahu, a demon head that swallowed the Sun or the Moon. Aryabhata wrote that the Moon shines by sunlight and that eclipses are shadows. When the Earth stands between the Sun and the Moon, the Earth casts a long cone of shadow into space, and the Moon grows dark as it passes through it. When the Moon passes directly between the Sun and the Earth, the Moon covers the Sun and casts its own shadow onto the ground. From the widths and angles of those shadow cones, astronomers could calculate in advance when an eclipse would start and how long it would last.

#### What he claimed, and what he did not

Because Aryabhata was right about the turning Earth and the geometry of eclipses, modern websites often credit him with claims he never made. He did not propose a heliocentric solar system with the Earth orbiting the Sun; his planetary model was still geocentric, using epicycles, smaller circles riding on larger circles, to track the planets. Even later Indian astronomers, including Brahmagupta, rejected his rotating Earth, asking why tall buildings and birds in flight were not left behind. Direct mechanical proof that the ground turns beneath us waited until Léon Foucault's pendulum in Paris in 1851. Nor is there any historical basis for the "99.4 per cent accuracy" figures repeated online. What Aryabhata actually did at twenty-three needs no exaggeration: he looked at the turning sky and saw a moving boat.
