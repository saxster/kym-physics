:::scene Ujjain, about 1150

Around 1150, at the age of thirty-six, the mathematician and astronomer Bhaskara II (often called Bhaskaracharya, "Bhaskara the teacher") finished his four-part work, the *Siddhanta Shiromani*, or *Crown of Treatises*. Born around 1114 in Vijjadavida in the Deccan region of India, he headed the astronomical observatory at Ujjain, the city that served for centuries as the prime meridian of classical Indian astronomy.

#### Four books in verse

The *Siddhanta Shiromani* was written in Sanskrit verse so that students could memorise the rules, accompanied by Bhaskara's own prose explanations and worked examples. Its first two parts, the *Lilavati* (on arithmetic and geometry) and the *Bijaganita* (on algebra), became the standard mathematics textbooks across India for centuries. The *Lilavati* poses its problems as riddles about swarms of bees, flocks of swans, and lotus flowers blown sideways in a pond, turning geometry and quadratic equations into stories.

The third and fourth parts, the *Grahaganita* and the *Goladhyaya*, turn to the motions of the planets and the sphere of the sky and Earth.

#### Why a round Earth looks flat

In the *Goladhyaya* (*Chapter on the Sphere*), Bhaskara tackled a question every student asks when first told that the Earth is a ball: when we walk outside and look across a field, the ground looks flat. How can we be standing on a sphere?

Bhaskara answered with scale and geometry. Draw a huge circle, he wrote, and look at just a hundredth part of its circumference. That short arc curves so gently that to the eye it looks like a straight line. A person standing on the Earth sees only a tiny fraction of its circumference at once, far less than a hundredth part of the globe, and so the ground appears flat to us even though the whole Earth is a sphere.

He also addressed the puzzle that Brahmagupta had discussed five centuries earlier: if the Earth is a sphere hanging in space without a pillar underneath it, why does it not fall, and why do people and oceans on the opposite side not drop off into the sky? Bhaskara argued that if the Earth fell simply by being heavy, an arrow shot into the sky and the Earth itself would fall together, and one would have to ask which way "down" even points in open space. Instead, the Earth holds things to its surface by its own attractive power, so that people standing on opposite sides of the globe each feel "down" as towards the Earth's centre beneath their own feet, and nobody is upside down.

#### Instruments and honest history

In the final chapters of the *Goladhyaya*, Bhaskara described the instruments (*yantras*) of an astronomer's observatory: armillary spheres of rings to model the sky, vertical rods (*shanku*) to cast noon shadows for latitude and time, water clocks, and a sighting board called the *phalaka-yantra* that he designed to measure the Sun's altitude.

One caution is needed when reading about Bhaskara online. Non-scholarly websites often quote a verse on attraction and claim that Bhaskara discovered the law of gravity. As with Brahmagupta, that confuses a qualitative statement with a mathematical law. Bhaskara wrote in the *Siddhanta* tradition that the Earth draws heavy things in the air down to its surface, but he gave no inverse-square formula and no link between falling bodies and planetary orbits, which he still modelled with epicycles. What he gave his readers was a clear, geometric explanation of life on a giant sphere.
