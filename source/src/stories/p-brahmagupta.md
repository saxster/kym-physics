:::scene Ujjain, 628 CE

In 628 CE, at the age of thirty, the mathematician and astronomer Brahmagupta completed his treatise *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, the *Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma*. Born in 598 CE, likely at Bhillamala in Rajasthan, he worked in the astronomical tradition of Ujjain in central India, the city through which Indian astronomers drew their prime meridian of longitude.

#### Fortunes, debts, and zero

Today Brahmagupta is remembered first for mathematics. Before him, zero was used mostly as an empty placeholder in positional numbers, so that you could tell 205 apart from 25. In the *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, Brahmagupta treated zero as a number in its own right and wrote down explicit rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers, which he called "debts" (*rina*), alongside positive numbers, which he called "fortunes" (*dhana*).

His rules read like modern algebra in everyday words. The sum of two fortunes is a fortune, and the sum of two debts is a debt. The sum of a fortune and an equal debt is zero. Subtracting a debt is the same as adding a fortune, and the product of two debts is a fortune. Only at division by zero did his rules falter, a trap that took centuries more to untangle. Every time you use a negative sign in Chapter 1 to tell a westward velocity from an eastward one, or a downward acceleration from an upward one, you are using Brahmagupta's bookkeeping.

#### The Earth's nature to attract

Brahmagupta also asked why things fall, and why people and oceans stay on a round Earth instead of dropping off. Around 1030 CE, four centuries after the *Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, the scholar Al-Biruni travelled through India, learned Sanskrit, and wrote a survey of Indian science. In it, Al-Biruni reported Brahmagupta's statement on falling bodies: heavy things fall to the Earth by a law of nature, for it is the nature of the Earth to attract and to keep things, as it is the nature of water to flow.

To someone standing on a spherical Earth, "down" means towards the Earth's centre. People on the opposite side have their feet pointed towards the same centre. Brahmagupta was arguing that objects do not fall toward some universal bottom of space; they are drawn toward the Earth itself from every side around the globe.

#### The gap between a word and a law

Because of Al-Biruni's report, some accounts claim that Brahmagupta discovered the law of gravity a thousand years before Isaac Newton. That confuses two very different steps in science.

Saying that the Earth's nature is to attract heavy things is a qualitative idea, a sentence in words. It has no mathematics for how the pull changes with distance, and no link to planetary orbits. In fact, Brahmagupta rejected Aryabhata's rotating Earth, arguing that if the Earth spun, stones and birds would not come straight back down to the spot below them.

A thousand years later, Newton did not merely say that the Earth attracts the apple. He showed that if the pull weakens with the square of the distance from the Earth's centre, then at the Moon's distance of 60 Earth radii the pull is weaker by 60² = 3,600, predicting an acceleration of 0.00272 m/s² that matches the Moon's orbit. Brahmagupta stated a true physical intuition about the round Earth, and that idea waited ten centuries for a mathematical law.

In Chapter 5 you will see how Newton turned the qualitative idea of attraction into the inverse-square law that links falling stones to the orbits of moons and planets.
