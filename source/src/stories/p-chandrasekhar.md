:::scene Bombay, July 1930

On 31 July 1930, nineteen-year-old **Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar** boarded the steamship SS *Pilsna* at Ballard Pier in Bombay, bound for Venice and a graduate scholarship at Cambridge. In his trunk he carried **Ralph Fowler**'s 1926 paper explaining how burnt-out stars called **white dwarfs** support themselves against gravity. During the voyage across the Arabian Sea, working in pencil on deck, Chandrasekhar added one piece of physics Fowler had left out: **Albert Einstein**'s speed limit for fast-moving electrons. His notepad calculation showed that no white dwarf heavier than about 1.4 solar masses could hold itself up.

#### From Madras to the *Pilsna*

Born in Lahore on 19 October 1910 (his uncle was **C. V. Raman**), Chandrasekhar grew up in Madras and studied at Presidency College, where he published his first paper in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society* at eighteen and won a scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge.

Fowler had explained how white dwarfs like **Sirius B** pack the mass of the Sun into a sphere the size of the Earth, a million times denser than water. **Wolfgang Pauli**'s exclusion principle forbids two electrons from sharing the same quantum state; squeezed together, the electrons are forced into high momentum states, creating a quantum **electron degeneracy pressure** that resists gravity even at zero temperature.

#### Adding Special Relativity

On the *Pilsna*, Chandrasekhar worked out how hard the core of a white dwarf is squeezed as you add mass. In a white dwarf as heavy as the Sun, the fastest electrons move at speeds approaching the speed of light c.

In non-relativistic mechanics, kinetic energy grows with momentum squared (E = p² / 2m), stiffening the gas enough to halt gravity at any mass. In special relativity, however, no electron can outrun c, so energy grows only in proportion to momentum (E ≈ pc). That softens the pressure law (P ∝ ρ⁴/³ instead of ρ⁵/³), matching the scaling of gravity itself. Above the **Chandrasekhar limit** (about 1.44 solar masses), electron degeneracy pressure fails and the star must collapse further.

#### The Ambush in Burlington House

:::scene London, 11 January 1935

After four years refining the exact equation of state at Cambridge, twenty-four-year-old Chandrasekhar presented his full curve of white-dwarf masses and radii to the Royal Astronomical Society in London.

Speaking immediately after him, **Arthur Eddington** attacked the relativistic formula as *"stellar buffoonery"*. *"I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way,"* Eddington declared, objecting to the unchecked collapse that would leave behind what physicists later called a **black hole**.

Although Bohr, Pauli, and Dirac privately agreed that Chandrasekhar's mathematics was right, none challenged Eddington in public before astronomers. Chandrasekhar wrote up his stellar theory in a 1939 book, moved to the University of Chicago, and spent the next five decades writing the definitive treatises on radiative transfer, hydrodynamic stability, and black holes, receiving the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics fifty-three years after his voyage from Bombay.
