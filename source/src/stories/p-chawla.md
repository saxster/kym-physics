:::scene Karnal, 1976

On summer evenings in the town of **Karnal** in Haryana, the Chawla family slept on string *charpoys* on the flat roof of their house, looking up at the Milky Way and the occasional bright point of an artificial satellite gliding from horizon to horizon. By day, whenever her brother drove her past the Karnal Aviation Club, fourteen-year-old **Kalpana Chawla** pressed against the fence to watch single-engine Pushpak trainer aeroplanes lift off from the grass strip, and pestered her father until he took her up for a flight in a cockpit.

#### The Only Girl in the Wind-Tunnel Lab

Born in Karnal on 17 March 1962, the youngest of four children of **Banarsi Lal Chawla** and **Sanyogita Chawla**, who had crossed from Pakistan during the 1947 Partition, she chose her own name, *Kalpana* ("imagination"), on her first day at Tagore Baal Niketan school. Her bedroom walls were covered with drawings of biplanes and jets.

When she decided in 1978 to study aeronautical engineering at **Punjab Engineering College** in Chandigarh, professors tried to steer her toward electrical engineering or medicine. Out of a hundred students admitted that year, only seven were women, and Kalpana was the first and only woman in the Aeronautical Engineering department. She organised fluid-mechanics study clubs and graduated at the top of her department in 1982.

#### Simulating Airflows and Ground Effect

Moving to the United States in 1982, Chawla earned a master's degree in aerospace engineering at the University of Texas at Arlington and a PhD in 1988 at the University of Colorado Boulder. At **NASA Ames Research Center** in California, she specialised in **computational fluid dynamics** (**CFD**), programming supercomputers to solve the equations of airflow around vertical and short take-off aircraft such as the Harrier jump-jet. When a hovering jet blasts exhaust downward against a runway, the rebounding air curls into low-pressure vortices beneath the wings so that the higher-pressure air above pushes the aircraft down; Chawla wrote numerical simulations to map those three-dimensional flows while earning pilot licences for aeroplanes and gliders.

#### Weightless Aboard *Columbia*

:::scene In orbit, November 1997

Selected as a NASA astronaut in December 1994, Chawla lifted off from Florida on 19 November 1997 aboard the Space Shuttle *Columbia* on mission **STS-87**, becoming the first Indian-born woman in space.

Orbiting 280 kilometres above the Earth at 7.7 kilometres per second, the shuttle circled the globe every ninety minutes. Inside, the crew floated weightless not because gravity had disappeared (Earth's pull at 280 km is still 92 per cent as strong as on the ground in Karnal), but because both shuttle and crew were in continuous **free fall** around the curve of the Earth.

In January 2003 she returned to orbit on **STS-107**, running more than eighty microgravity experiments in fluid physics, combustion, and crystal growth. On 1 February 2003, sixteen minutes before landing, *Columbia* broke apart during atmospheric re-entry after superheated air entered a wing leading edge damaged at launch. Chawla died with her six crewmates, and India renamed its meteorological satellite series **Kalpana-1** in her memory.
