:::scene Darjeeling, 1940

High on the Singalila ridge along the Bengal-Nepal border, at **Darjeeling** (2,130 m) and **Sandakphu** (3,660 m), **Bibha Chowdhuri** packed light-tight wooden boxes holding glass plates coated with photographic emulsion. Some batches sat in the thin mountain air under the open sky; others lay beneath twenty centimetres of water or paraffin wax to slow incoming cosmic rays. Carried back to the Bose Institute in Calcutta, developed, and examined millimetre by millimetre through a binocular microscope, the emulsions revealed tiny strings of blackened silver grains: the curving footprints of unstable subatomic particles from space called **mesotrons** (now known as **muons** and **pions**).

#### The Only Woman in the MSc Class

Born in Calcutta in 1913 into a Brahmo Samaj family where her father was a physician and her aunt had founded a girls' school, Bibha Chowdhuri studied physics at the Rajabazar Science College of the University of Calcutta, graduating in 1936 as the only woman in her MSc physics class.

She persuaded **Debendra Mohan Bose**, who had succeeded his uncle **Jagadish Chandra Bose** as director of the Bose Institute, to let her join his cosmic-ray group and took charge of exposing and reading the mountain plates.

#### Measuring Particle Mass with a Microscope

In 1935 **Hideki Yukawa** had predicted that atomic nuclei are held together by a middleweight particle about two hundred times heavier than an electron. Before giant post-war accelerators existed, the only source of such particles was the **cosmic rays** striking Earth's upper atmosphere.

While cloud chambers captured only brief snapshots, photographic emulsions acted as continuous traps. Between 1939 and 1942, Bose and Chowdhuri published four papers in *Nature* turning microscopic tracks into quantitative detectors. As a charged particle ploughs through gelatin emulsion, it ionises silver bromide crystals and leaves a trail of silver grains. Slower, heavier particles scatter more often and leave closer-spaced grains than fast, light ones. By calibrating their Ilford half-tone plates against known proton tracks and comparing exposures at Darjeeling and Sandakphu, Chowdhuri and Bose measured the grain spacing and scattering angles along curved tracks and calculated a mesotron mass of roughly 160 to 220 electron masses.

#### Wartime Limits, Manchester, and TIFR

In their 1941 and 1942 *Nature* papers, Chowdhuri and Bose noted that wartime supply restrictions prevented them from obtaining fuller-tone emulsions with denser silver grains. After the war, **Cecil Powell** in Bristol used those improved Ilford nuclear emulsions with the same grain-counting method to distinguish the pion from the muon and won the 1950 Nobel Prize, crediting Bose and Chowdhuri's 1941 method in his 1959 book.

Chowdhuri completed her PhD on cosmic-ray air showers in **Patrick Blackett**'s laboratory at Manchester, and in 1949 **Homi Bhabha** recruited her as the first woman scientist at the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research** (**TIFR**) in Bombay. She later studied deep underground muon bundles in the **Kolar** gold mines. In 2019 the International Astronomical Union named the star **HD 86081** **Bibha** ("beam of light") in her honour.
