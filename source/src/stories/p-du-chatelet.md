:::scene Cirey, France, 1740

In the 1730s, a woman in France could not enrol at the University of Paris, attend meetings of the Royal Academy of Sciences, or enter the Paris cafés where mathematicians gathered to argue about physics (when Gabrielle Émilie Le Tonnelier de Breteuil, Marquise du Châtelet, was turned away at the Café Gradot, she had a man's suit tailored and walked back in). To do serious research, she and her partner, the writer Voltaire, renovated her family's château at Cirey-sur-Blaise in north-eastern France, equipping it with twenty thousand books and a laboratory.

#### The argument over moving force: mv or mv²?

In 1740 du Châtelet published *Institutions de physique* (*Foundations of Physics*), begun as a textbook for her teenage son after she found that no French book explained the newest physics clearly.

In the final chapter of the *Institutions*, she entered a fierce dispute in European mechanics. Everyone agreed that a heavier or faster object is harder to stop, and that a moving object carries some "force of motion." Followers of René Descartes and Isaac Newton said the true measure of motion is mass times velocity, *mv* (what we now call **momentum**). Double a ball's speed, and its *mv* doubles. Followers of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz argued instead for *vis viva* ("living force"), which goes with mass times velocity *squared*, *mv*² (twice what we now call **kinetic energy**, ½*mv*²). Double a ball's speed, and its *mv*² grows by 2² = 4.

#### Brass balls in soft clay

How do you test *mv* against *mv*² by experiment? Du Châtelet pointed to the experiments carried out in Leiden in 1722 by the Dutch physicist Willem 's Gravesande. He had dropped solid brass balls from different heights onto soft potter's clay and measured the dents they left behind.

Suppose you want a ball to hit the clay at *twice* the speed. Because a falling ball's speed grows with the square root of the drop height (*v* = √(2*gh*)), dropping a ball from *four* times the height makes it hit the clay at *twice* the speed. If the effort needed to stop the ball went with *mv*, the twice-as-fast ball would sink twice as deep into the clay. Instead, 's Gravesande found that a ball hitting at twice the speed made a dent **four times** as deep; a ball hitting at three times the speed (from nine times the height) made a dent **nine times** as deep.

Du Châtelet did not claim to have invented the clay experiment or to have "proved" the law alone, but she built 's Gravesande's measurements into a clear, public argument for *mv*². Today we know that both sides had hold of a real physical quantity: momentum (*mv*) measures how a force acts over **time**, whereas kinetic energy (½*mv*²) measures how a force acts over **distance**, such as the depth of a dent in clay.

#### Translating the Principia against the clock

From 1745 onwards, du Châtelet translated Newton's Latin *Principia* into French, adding an algebraic commentary that recast Newton's geometry in modern calculus. Discovering in 1748 that she was pregnant at forty-two, an age then perilous for childbirth, she worked late into the night to finish the manuscript, posted the parcel to the royal library days before giving birth, and died six days later in September 1749. Her two-volume translation remains the standard French edition of Newton's *Principia*.
