:::scene The Royal Institution, London, 1831

Michael Faraday was born in 1791 in Newington Butts, south London, the son of a blacksmith who had moved to the city looking for work. The family was poor, and Michael's schooling ended at thirteen with little more than reading, writing, and basic arithmetic.

#### Seven years in a bookshop

At fourteen he became an apprentice to a French bookbinder and bookseller named George Riebau on Blandford Street. For seven years Faraday stitched leather covers onto other people's books, and after hours he read what he was binding: the electricity article in the *Encyclopædia Britannica* and Jane Marcet's *Conversations on Chemistry*. With pocket pence he bought glass vials and zinc discs to repeat the experiments at the back of the shop.

In 1812 a customer gave the twenty-year-old apprentice tickets to hear the chemist Humphry Davy lecture at the Royal Institution. Faraday wrote out three hundred pages of neat notes with hand-drawn diagrams, bound them in leather, and sent the book to Davy, asking for any job in science. When a laboratory assistant was dismissed in 1813, Davy hired Faraday in his place.

#### The spinning wire and the iron ring

In April 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted in Copenhagen found that an electric current in a wire makes a nearby compass needle twitch: electricity produces magnetism.

On 3 and 4 September 1821, in his basement laboratory at the Royal Institution, Faraday set a vertical magnet in a dish of conducting mercury (never touch mercury yourself) and hung a free wire above it. When current flowed through the wire, the wire circled continuously around the magnet. By turning an electric current and a magnet into continuous rotation, Faraday had built the world's first electric motor.

Then he asked the reverse question: if electricity makes magnetism, can magnetism make electricity? For ten years he tried placing magnets beside coils of wire and saw nothing. The answer came on 29 August 1831. Faraday wound two separate coils of insulated copper wire around opposite sides of a heavy iron ring. When he connected the first coil to a battery, a compass needle attached to the second coil jumped for an instant and settled back to zero. When he disconnected the battery, the needle jumped the opposite way. A steady magnet makes no current; only a **changing** magnetic field induces a current in a wire. Within weeks he showed that pushing a bar magnet in and out of a hollow coil, or spinning a copper disc between the poles of a magnet, generates a continuous current. Every power-station generator and street transformer in Mumbai descends from those experiments.

#### Lines of force and the Christmas Lectures

In 1836 Faraday built a twelve-foot wooden cube covered in metal foil, stepped inside with sensitive charge detectors while sparks struck the outside, and found not the least electrical influence inside: the **Faraday cage**.

Because he had no university algebra, Faraday pictured the space around magnets and charges as filled with curved **lines of force**, like the patterns iron filings trace on a card over a magnet. James Clerk Maxwell later turned Faraday's lines into the equations of electromagnetism, praising Faraday's physical reasoning as a mathematical method of the highest order.

Remembering his own youth, Faraday founded the Royal Institution's Friday Evening Discourses and, in 1825, its Christmas Lectures for children. In Chapter 12 and Chapter 13 you will trace his circuits, his motor, his iron ring, and his field lines.
