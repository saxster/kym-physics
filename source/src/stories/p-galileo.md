:::scene Padua, 1604

Drop a stone from head height and it hits the floor in less than half a second. In 1604, when Galileo Galilei taught mathematics at Padua in northern Italy, no clock could measure half a second. To discover the mathematical rule of falling bodies, Galileo had to slow falling down without changing its nature.

#### Strings, balances, and the habit of testing

Galileo was born in Pisa in 1564. His father, Vincenzo Galilei, was a lutenist and music theorist who tested ancient claims by experiment. An old legend said that Pythagoras had discovered musical intervals from the weights of blacksmiths' hammers and that doubling the weight pulling a string raises its pitch by an octave. Around 1589 in Florence, Vincenzo hung weights on lute strings and showed that an octave requires four times the weight, not twice.

Young Galileo shared that habit. In 1586, aged twenty-two, he wrote *La Bilancetta* (*The Little Balance*), pointing out that Vitruvius's tale of Archimedes checking King Hiero's crown by bathtub overflow would need impossible precision, and designing a hydrostatic balance to weigh metals in water instead (Chapter 6).

As for the Leaning Tower of Pisa, that tale comes only from Galileo's late-life secretary Vincenzo Viviani; Galileo never describes dropping balls from the tower himself. Instead, he gave a sharp thought experiment: tie a heavy stone and a light stone together. If the light stone really fell slower, it would drag back on the heavy stone and make the pair fall *slower* than the heavy stone alone; yet the tied pair is heavier than either one, so by the same rule it should fall *faster*.

#### Ramps and the odd-number rule

At Padua, Galileo replaced vertical dropping with a smooth wooden groove tilted at a gentle angle. A bronze ball rolling down a slight incline is pulled by gravity just like a falling ball, only much more slowly.

In a reconstruction by historian Stillman Drake, the story goes that Galileo tied movable gut frets across the ramp like lute frets and sang a steady beat, sliding the frets until the ball clicked over one fret on each beat. Whether he timed the rolls by beats or by weighing water pouring from a thin pipe into a cup, his measurements revealed a clear rule. In the first beat of time, the ball rolled 1 unit of distance; in the second beat, 3 units; in the third, 5; in the fourth, 7. Add those odd numbers up (1, 1 + 3 = 4, 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16) and the total distance from the start grows as the square of the time (*d* ∝ *t*²). That meant the ball's speed grew by the same amount in every equal beat: constant acceleration.

#### Four moons around Jupiter

:::scene Venice, August 1609

In May 1609 Galileo heard of the new Dutch spyglass. Grinding his own lenses, he showed an eight-to-nine-power telescope to the Venetian Senate from the bell tower of San Marco on 25 August 1609. Turning a stronger telescope on the sky on 7 January 1610, he saw three tiny "stars" beside Jupiter; a fourth soon appeared, and by 15 January he realised they were four moons orbiting Jupiter (*Sidereus Nuncius*, March 1610). Critics had argued that a moving Earth would lose its Moon, yet here was Jupiter moving across the sky with four moons in tow.

In his final book, *Two New Sciences* (1638), written under house arrest near Florence, Galileo brought together his work on motion, ramps, and projectiles. You will meet his work across Chapters 1, 2, 4, 9, and 10.
