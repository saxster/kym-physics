:::scene London, 1684

In August 1684, the twenty-seven-year-old astronomer Edmond Halley travelled from London to Cambridge to ask Isaac Newton a question that had stumped Robert Hooke and Christopher Wren: if the Sun pulls on a planet with a force that weakens with the square of the distance, what shape of path will the planet follow?

#### A fish book and a masterpiece

Born in London in 1656, Halley had never been content to stay indoors. At twenty, before finishing his degree at Oxford, he sailed to St Helena in the South Atlantic and spent a year charting 341 southern stars that European astronomers could not see from home.

When Halley asked his orbit question in Cambridge in 1684, Newton answered at once: an ellipse. Asked how he knew, Newton replied that he had calculated it years earlier, rummaged through his papers, and could not find the proof. He promised to write it out afresh and send it to London.

What arrived a few months later led Halley to coax a full three-volume book out of the secretive professor. Yet the Royal Society had no money left to print it. The Society had just spent its funds printing an expensive illustrated volume called *De Historia Piscium* (*The History of Fishes*) by Francis Willughby, which sold so poorly that the Society paid Halley's clerk salary partly in unsold copies of the fish book. So Halley paid to print Newton's *Principia* (1687) out of his own pocket, edited the text, calmed Newton down when Hooke quarrelled with him, and saw the book through the press.

#### The comet of 1531, 1607, and 1682

Once Newton's laws were in print, Halley applied them to comets, which people had assumed were one-off visitors travelling on straight lines or open curves. Computing the orbits of twenty-four comets, Halley noticed that three bright comets, seen in 1531, 1607, and 1682, shared almost identical paths around the Sun at intervals of 75 to 76 years.

They were not three comets, he concluded, but one comet on a long elliptical orbit, due back around late 1758. Halley died in 1742, aged eighty-five. As 1758 approached, three French mathematicians, Alexis Clairaut, Joseph-Jérôme Lalande, and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, spent months calculating how the gravitational pulls of Jupiter and Saturn would delay the comet, predicting perihelion around mid-April 1759. On Christmas night, 25 December 1758, a farmer and amateur astronomer named Johann Georg Palitzsch near Dresden spotted the comet returning on schedule.

#### The first theory of the Indian monsoon

In 1686 Halley also published the first meteorological map of the trade winds and the first physical theory of the Indian monsoon. Because land heats up faster in summer sunshine than the ocean does, he argued, warm air over Asia rises and cooler, moist air from the Indian Ocean flows in to replace it, bringing the monsoon rains; in winter the flow reverses.

For three centuries Halley's "giant sea breeze" was the textbook explanation of the monsoon. In recent decades, meteorologists such as Sulochana Gadgil and her colleagues have shown that the real monsoon is driven even more by the seasonal northward shift of the equatorial rain belt (the Intertropical Convergence Zone) than by a simple land-sea breeze alone. Even where later data revise it, Halley's paper started the physics of the monsoon.
