:::scene Cairo, about 1015

Hasan Ibn al-Haytham, known in Latin Europe as Alhazen, was born around 965 CE in Basra, in modern Iraq, and spent much of his working life in Cairo. Between about 1011 and 1021 he wrote his seven-volume *Kitab al-Manazir*, the *Book of Optics*. How he got the quiet years to write it is a famous tale: the story goes that he promised Caliph al-Hakim he could control the flooding of the Nile, saw upriver that the task was impossible, and feigned madness so the Caliph would keep him under house arrest rather than execute him. Historians treat the madness story with caution as a legend, but the experiments in his book are real.

#### Do your eyes send out beams?

For more than a thousand years before Ibn al-Haytham, Greek and Arab thinkers had argued over how we see. Mathematicians such as Euclid and Ptolemy taught the **extramission** theory: your eyes send out invisible rays, like feelers, that touch an object and report back. That idea still tempts us today; in psychology studies, most children and a majority of adults sketch something leaving the eyes when asked to draw how vision works.

Ibn al-Haytham overturned the eye-beam theory with observations that anyone can check. First, if you look at a very bright light, your eyes hurt. If you stare at a bright window and close your eyes, a coloured afterimage lingers in the dark for seconds afterwards. Why would a beam going *out* of your eye hurt your eye or leave an imprint behind? Something from outside is clearly coming *in* and acting on the eye. Second, when you walk outside at night and open your eyes, you see the distant stars at once. How could a substance from your tiny eye rush out and fill the whole sky to the stars in an instant?

#### Every point sends out rays

Instead, Ibn al-Haytham argued that luminous bodies such as the Sun or a candle flame send light out in straight lines from every point on their surface, and non-luminous objects reflect the light that hits them in every direction. You see your hand or this page only when some of that reflected light enters the pupil of your eye. He recognised that light enters through the lens of the eye and passes to the optic nerve, though how the retina forms a focused image was worked out only centuries later.

#### The dark room and the pinhole

To test whether light travels in straight lines and whether crossing rays mix, Ibn al-Haytham used a darkened room (*bayt al-muzlim*, the ancestor of the *camera obscura* or pinhole camera). Hang several candles in a row outside a dark room with a single tiny hole in the shutter. On the wall opposite the hole, an image of each candle appears, upside down and in reversed order: the right-hand candle makes a spot on the left, and the left-hand candle makes a spot on the right.

Block one candle outside, and only its matching spot on the wall vanishes. Uncover it, and the spot returns. That simple experiment proved two things at once: light from each candle travels in a straight line through the hole to its own place on the wall, and when light beams cross inside the tiny hole, they pass right through one another without tangling or mixing their colours.
