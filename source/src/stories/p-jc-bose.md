:::scene Calcutta, 1895

In 1895, at Calcutta Town Hall, the physicist Jagadish Chandra Bose stood before an audience chaired by the Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal, Sir Alexander Mackenzie, and triggered an electric spark in a small metal box. Invisible electromagnetic waves travelled about 75 feet across the building, passed through walls and the Lieutenant-Governor's own body in between, and tripped a detector that rang a bell and set off gunpowder.

#### Three years without taking a rupee of pay

Born in 1858 in Munsiganj in Bengal (now in Bangladesh), Bose studied at St Xavier's College in Calcutta before travelling to England, where he learned physics at Cambridge under Lord Rayleigh. When he returned to India in 1885, the colonial education service appointed him Professor of Physics at Presidency College in Calcutta, but on the reduced "Indian tier" of pay: two-thirds of the salary paid to a European professor, cut in half again because his appointment was marked officiating.

Bose protested in the quietest way possible. He taught all his classes every day, and he refused to touch his salary cheque. For three years he worked without taking a rupee of pay, until the college authorities relented, granted him the full rate, and paid all three years of arrears at once.

#### Shrinking the waves to five millimetres

In 1888 Heinrich Hertz in Germany had confirmed James Clerk Maxwell's prediction by generating electromagnetic waves in the laboratory. But Hertz's waves were metres long, about the length of a dining table. Try to study how a wave reflects from a small mirror or bends through a prism when the wave is three metres long, and the wave washes right around your apparatus.

Working in a twenty-four-foot room next to a bathroom at Presidency College, with no government grant and the help of a skilled local tinsmith, Bose built spark transmitters that generated electromagnetic waves as short as **5 millimetres**, at a frequency of about 60 GHz (3 × 10⁸ m/s ÷ 0.005 m = 60 × 10⁹ Hz). Because his waves were tiny, his whole apparatus could fit into a carrying case. He invented pyramid-shaped **horn antennas** to collect the waves, lenses of sulphur to focus them, wire-grid polarisers, and even a stack of *Bradshaw's Railway Timetable* with tin-foil pages interleaved to twist the waves' polarisation just as crystals twist visible light.

#### The coherer and the patent story

To detect his millimetre waves, Bose built a spiral-spring **coherer** using fine contacts of iron with a drop of mercury, and later semiconductor crystals such as galena (lead sulphide). When a wave arrived, the contact's electrical resistance dropped and clicked a receiver. In January 1897, at Lord Rayleigh's invitation, Bose demonstrated his compact millimetre-wave apparatus at the Royal Institution in London.

You will often read that Bose refused to patent any of his inventions. The true story has an extra layer. Bose disliked commercial secrecy and published his designs openly so anyone could use them, including Guglielmo Marconi. However, two of Bose's friends, the American philanthropist Sara Bull and the Irish writer Sister Nivedita, persuaded him to sign a patent application for his galena crystal detector. US Patent 755,840 was filed in 1901 and granted in 1904, though Bose never enforced it commercially and allowed it to lapse. Today the 60 GHz band he opened in Calcutta is used in high-speed wireless links, radar, and radio astronomy.
