:::scene Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1912

At the start of the twentieth century, astronomers could measure the distances to only the nearest stars by watching them shift slightly as the Earth orbits the Sun (parallax). Beyond a few hundred light-years, that shift becomes too tiny to measure from the ground. Nobody knew how large our Milky Way galaxy was, or whether the spiral patches in the sky were small gas clouds inside our galaxy or whole island universes millions of light-years away. The astronomer who found the yardstick to answer both questions could not choose her own research topic or use the observatory's telescopes at night.

#### Glass plates from Peru

Henrietta Swan Leavitt was born in Massachusetts in 1868 and educated at what became Radcliffe College. After an illness left her with progressive hearing loss, she joined the Harvard College Observatory in the 1890s, first as a volunteer and later as a staff member.

At Harvard, Director Edward Pickering hired a group of skilled women known as "computers" (because a computer was then a person who computed) to measure and classify stars on glass photographic plates, paying them a third to a quarter of what men in equivalent posts earned. Leavitt's assignment was to find **variable stars**, stars whose brightness rises and falls over days or weeks, on plates taken at Harvard's southern station at Arequipa in Peru.

To spot a variable star among thousands of tiny black silver dots on a glass negative, Leavitt laid a transparent positive copy of one night's plate directly over a negative plate from a different night. A steady star left a neat white ring around a dark core; a star that had brightened or dimmed stood out to her practised eye.

#### A swarm of bees at the same distance

In 1908 Leavitt published a list of **1,777 variable stars** in the Magellanic Clouds, two misty companion galaxies in the southern sky. Among sixteen of those stars for which she could measure the cycle time, she noticed a hint of a rule: the brighter ones took longer to complete each pulse from bright to dim and back.

Normally, seeing a bright star in the sky tells you nothing about its true luminosity: a dim bicycle lamp ten metres away looks brighter than a lighthouse ten kilometres away. You cannot tell whether a star looks bright because it is truly luminous or merely close.

Leavitt saw that the Small Magellanic Cloud solved that trap. Because all the stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud belong to the same distant cluster, they all sit at nearly the same distance from Earth, like a swarm of bees across a field. Any star in that cloud that *looks* four times brighter on the plate really *is* giving out four times more light.

In 1912, using **25 Cepheid variable stars** in the Small Magellanic Cloud, Leavitt plotted their brightness against the logarithm of their period and drew a smooth, tight line: brighter Cepheids always pulse more slowly.

#### Measuring the universe

Once other astronomers calibrated the distance to a few nearby Cepheids, Leavitt's **period-luminosity law** became a cosmic measuring tape: time how many days a distant Cepheid takes to blink, read off its true brightness, and compare that with how faint it looks to find its distance. Harlow Shapley used her law to measure the Milky Way, and from 1923 to 1929 Edwin Hubble used Cepheids in distant galaxies to show that the universe is vast and expanding. Leavitt died in 1921, four years before a Swedish mathematician wrote to nominate her for the Nobel Prize.
