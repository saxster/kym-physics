:::scene Woolsthorpe, 1666

In the summer of 1665, plague broke out in England, and Cambridge University closed. A twenty-two-year-old student named Isaac Newton packed his notebooks and went home to his family's manor house at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire, where he spent the next two years with his calculations and experiments.

#### Two prisms in a darkened room

At that time, most scientists believed that white sunlight was the purest, simplest kind of light, and that when a glass prism threw a rainbow onto a wall, the glass itself was staining the white light to create the colours.

Newton tested that belief in a darkened room by cutting a small hole in his window shutter and holding a triangular prism in the sunbeam. Across the room, the prism threw an elongated band of colours, from red at one end to violet at the other. Then came what he called his *experimentum crucis*, the decisive test at the crossroads. He placed a board with a small hole in it so that only a single colour, say blue or red, could pass through, and sent that single colour through a *second* prism.

If a prism worked by adding colour to light, the second prism should have spread the single colour into a fresh rainbow. Instead, the blue ray bent further and stayed blue; the red ray bent less and stayed red. White sunlight was already a mixture of all the colours, and the glass simply sorted them out because violet bends more strongly in glass than red does. He published this result in a letter to the Royal Society on 6 February 1672.

#### The apple and the sixty Earth radii

At Woolsthorpe, Newton also thought about gravity. Sixty years later, in 1726, he told his biographer William Stukeley over tea under some apple trees that the idea of gravitation had come to him earlier in just such a setting, "occasion'd by the fall of an apple, as he sat in contemplative mood." There really was an apple tree at Woolsthorpe, though the cartoon version where the apple hits his head is an invention; a Royal Society librarian calls the story "an 18th-century sound bite."

The question was genuine: how high does the Earth's pull reach? It stays just as strong on the highest mountain top. What if the same pull reaches sixty times farther than the Earth's surface, all the way to the Moon?

By Newton's first law of motion, a Moon with no force on it would fly off along a straight line. Instead, the Moon's path bends continually around the Earth. Newton guessed that Earth's pull weakens with the square of the distance from the Earth's centre. At the Moon's distance of about 60 Earth radii, the pull per kilogram should be weaker than at the ground by 60² = 3,600. An apple near the ground accelerates at 9.8 m/s² and falls 4.9 m in its first second. Divided by 3,600, the Moon's acceleration towards the Earth should be 0.00272 m/s², dropping about 1.36 mm below a straight line in one second and 4.9 m in one minute. Comparing that prediction with the Moon's 27.3-day orbit, Newton found they agreed "pretty nearly."

#### The Principia

Newton kept the calculation in his papers until Edmond Halley visited him in Cambridge in August 1684 and urged him to publish. Three years later came the *Principia* (1687), uniting the three laws of motion and universal gravitation.
