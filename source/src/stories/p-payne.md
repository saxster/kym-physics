:::scene Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1925

In the brick library of the Harvard College Observatory, twenty-five-year-old **Cecilia Payne** sat surrounded by thousands of glass photographic plates, each striped with the dark barcode of a star's spectrum. For two years she had measured the strengths of those spectral lines and fed them into the thermal ionisation equation published in Calcutta by **Meghnad Saha**. Common metals from silicon to iron came out in roughly the same relative proportions as in the crust of the Earth. Hydrogen and helium did not. According to her equations, hydrogen was roughly a million times more abundant in the stars than astronomers believed.

#### A Ticket to Eddington's Lecture

Born in Wendover, Buckinghamshire on 10 May 1900, Cecilia Payne entered Newnham College, Cambridge on a scholarship in 1919. That December she attended a lecture in Trinity College by **Arthur Eddington** on the May 1919 solar eclipse expedition that had confirmed **Albert Einstein**'s general relativity. She wrote the lecture out from memory that night and switched her main study to physics.

At the Cavendish Laboratory she attended lectures by **Ernest Rutherford** and **Niels Bohr**, sitting alone in the front row by regulation as the only woman in the advanced course. Because Cambridge did not grant degrees to women until 1948, and Britain offered her little prospect beyond schoolteaching, she won a fellowship in 1923 to work under **Harlow Shapley** at the Harvard College Observatory.

#### Decoding the Glass Archive

Harvard's women computers, led by **Williamina Fleming** and **Annie Jump Cannon**, had classified more than two hundred thousand stellar spectra into the sequence O, B, A, F, G, K, M. Yet leading astronomers, including Eddington and **Henry Norris Russell**, assumed that a vaporised Earth would show the same spectrum as the Sun, and that the Sun's strong iron and calcium lines meant the stars were made mostly of heavy elements.

Combining Cavendish atomic physics with Cannon's plate archive, Payne applied Saha's equation across eighteen elements to establish the first accurate stellar temperature scale. She also saw why solar hydrogen lines looked deceptively modest: at the Sun's surface temperature of 5,800 K, fewer than one hydrogen atom in a hundred million sits in the second energy level (n = 2) needed to absorb visible Balmer light. For the Balmer lines to appear as strong as they do when almost all the hydrogen sits unseen in the ground state, the star had to consist overwhelmingly of hydrogen.

#### The Caveat Added Under Pressure

When Payne sent her draft thesis, *Stellar Atmospheres*, to Henry Norris Russell in January 1925, he warned her that a hydrogen-dominated universe was *"clearly impossible"*. Payne published all of her data tables and abundance figures in her 1925 dissertation, earning Radcliffe College's first astronomy PhD, while adding a reluctant sentence stating that the enormous hydrogen and helium abundances were *"almost certainly not real"*.

Four years later, Russell arrived at the same result by an independent calculation and confirmed in 1929 that Payne had been right all along. The astronomer **Otto Struve** called her 1925 monograph *"undoubtedly the most brilliant Ph.D. thesis ever written in astronomy"*. In 1956 **Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin** became the first woman promoted to full professor in Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences and the first woman to chair a Harvard department.
