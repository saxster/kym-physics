:::scene Calcutta, February 1928

In a darkened room at 210 Bowbazar Street, **C. V. Raman** and his research student **K. S. Krishnan** directed a beam of sunlight, narrowed by a telescope lens and filtered through violet glass, into flasks of purified benzene and sixty other liquids. Looking through a second green filter that blocked the incoming violet beam, they still saw a faint, shifted glow. On the evening of 27 February 1928, Raman looked through a direct-vision spectroscope and saw the shifted colour separated from the incident line by a dark gap. The next morning, 28 February, they photographed the spectrum with a small quartz spectrograph.

#### The Accountant After Hours

Born on 7 November 1888 near Tiruchirappalli, **Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman** entered Presidency College in Madras at fourteen and published his first optics papers in *Philosophical Magazine* while still an undergraduate. Because research posts for Indian scientists scarcely existed under colonial rule, he sat the Indian Finance Department examination in 1907, placed first, and went to Calcutta as an Assistant Accountant General.

Riding a tram home from work, Raman spotted the signboard of the **Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science** (**IACS**) at 210 Bowbazar Street. For the next decade he worked double hours: in the Bowbazar laboratory from half past five until quarter to ten each morning, at his Finance Department desk all day, and back in the laboratory until late at night. In 1917 he gave up his high civil-service salary to take the Palit Chair of Physics at the University of Calcutta.

#### The Tabla's Black Patch

An ordinary circular drumhead vibrates at frequencies that do not form whole-number ratios (1 : 1.59 : 2.14 : 2.30), so it gives an unpitched thud. Working with **Sivakali Kumar**, Raman studied the Indian *mridangam* and *tabla*, whose makers weight the centre of the skin with a layered black paste called *syahi* (iron filings, charcoal, and rice starch). Sprinkling fine sand on the drumhead to trace its nodal lines, Raman and Kumar showed in *Nature* on 15 January 1920 that the heavy central patch slows the inner membrane just enough to pull the vibration modes into five clean harmonic overtones (1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5).

#### Pocket Instruments at Sea

:::scene Mediterranean Sea, September 1921

Sailing home from England aboard the SS *Narkunda*, Raman climbed onto deck with a pocket spectroscope and a Nicol polarising prism.

Lord Rayleigh had explained the blue sky by molecular scattering, yet argued that the blue sea was merely the reflection of the sky in water. Using his Nicol prism at Brewster's angle to cancel the surface reflection, Raman saw the sea glow an even richer indigo from below. By the time the ship docked in Bombay, he had posted a *Nature* letter showing that water molecules themselves scatter sunlight.

#### The New Radiation

Back in Calcutta, Krishnan recorded in his diary between 5 and 28 February 1928 that every pure liquid they tested showed a weak polarised glow of altered wavelength, which Raman and Krishnan discussed on 9 February as the **Raman-Krishnan effect**. Roughly one scattered photon in ten million gives a packet of energy to a molecule's vibration or takes one away, emerging with a shifted colour that serves as a fingerprint of its chemical bonds. The discovery won Raman the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics, and India celebrates 28 February as National Science Day.
