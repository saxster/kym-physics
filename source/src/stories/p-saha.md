:::scene Calcutta, 1920

At the University College of Science in Calcutta, twenty-six-year-old **Meghnad Saha** puzzled over a question that had stalled astronomers for decades. When starlight is spread out through a prism, cooler yellow stars like the Sun show strong dark absorption lines of calcium and iron, whereas hotter white and blue stars show strong lines of helium and hydrogen and almost no metals. Most astronomers assumed different stars were made of different proportions of chemical elements. Working with paper, pencil, and German journals delayed by the First World War, Saha proved that the stars were not changing their ingredients; they were changing their temperature.

#### From Seoratali to Presidency College

Meghnad Saha was born on 6 October 1893 in the village of Seoratali near Dacca (now Dhaka), the fifth child of a village grocer. Because the nearest English middle school lay ten kilometres away in Simulia, a local doctor, **Ananta Kumar Das**, boarded the boy free of charge in his own home while Saha walked between villages and won a scholarship to Dacca Collegiate School.

Expelled from that school in 1905 after joining a silent student boycott during the British governor's visit, Saha continued at a private school and entered Presidency College in Calcutta in 1911. There he studied alongside **Satyendra Nath Bose** and learned under **Prafulla Chandra Ray** and **Jagadish Chandra Bose**. Recruited as young lecturers at Calcutta in 1916, Saha and S. N. Bose taught themselves quantum theory and relativity from German papers and in 1920 published the first English translation of **Albert Einstein**'s and **Hermann Minkowski**'s papers on relativity.

#### The Ionisation Equation

Reading **Walther Nernst**'s physical chemistry alongside **Niels Bohr**'s atomic model, Saha saw a bridge between thermodynamics and spectroscopy. Chemists knew how furnace heat splits molecules into atoms in reversible equilibrium. What if the extreme heat inside a stellar atmosphere strips electrons off atoms according to the same thermodynamic laws?

In four papers drafted in Calcutta in 1919 and 1920, beginning with *"Ionization in the Solar Chromosphere"* in *Philosophical Magazine*, Saha derived his **thermal ionisation equation**. It links the fraction of ionised atoms to the gas temperature, the electron pressure, and the atom's **ionisation potential**, the energy required to knock an electron free.

Suddenly the Harvard stellar sequence (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) made sense as a single temperature scale. In a cool 3,000 K star, hydrogen atoms sit in their ground state and cannot absorb visible Balmer light. Around 10,000 K, collisions boost hydrogen into its second level so the Balmer lines blaze across the spectrum. Above 25,000 K, nearly every hydrogen atom has already lost its electron (H⁺), so the lines fade again. Working with **Alfred Fowler** in London in 1920 and 1921, Saha verified these predictions against laboratory spectra.

#### Rivers and Calendars

Returning to Calcutta in 1938 after fifteen years at Allahabad, Saha founded the **Institute of Nuclear Physics** and was elected to Parliament in 1952. Having grown up amid Bengal's river floods, he wrote the hydrological plan for the **Damodar Valley Corporation** and chaired the committee that reformed the Indian national calendar.
