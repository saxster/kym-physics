:::scene Dacca, 4 June 1924

In his study at the University of Dacca, thirty-year-old **Satyendra Nath Bose** enclosed a four-page typescript with a brief covering letter to **Albert Einstein** in Berlin. *"Respected Sir,"* Bose wrote, *"I have ventured to send you the accompanying article for your perusal and opinion. I am anxious to know what you think of it. You will see that I have tried to deduce the coefficient 8πν²/c³ in Planck's Law independent of the classical electrodynamics."* If Einstein thought the paper worth publishing, Bose asked whether he would arrange for its translation into German for the *Zeitschrift für Physik*. *"Though a complete stranger to you, I do not feel any hesitation in making such a request. Because we are all your pupils though profiting only by your teachings through your writings."*

#### The Classroom Puzzle

Born in Calcutta on 1 January 1894, the son of an East Indian Railway accountant, Bose topped every university mathematics examination at Presidency College, just ahead of his classmate and friend **Meghnad Saha**. Together the two young lecturers taught themselves modern physics and translated Einstein's relativity papers into English in 1920.

In 1921 Bose moved to the new University of Dacca as Reader in Physics. Lecturing to postgraduate students in 1924 on **Max Planck**'s blackbody radiation law, he was dissatisfied with every textbook proof. Planck's formula assumed energy comes in discrete quanta of size E = hf, yet to count how many radiation modes exist at each frequency, every derivation fell back on classical electromagnetic waves before bolting the quantum hypothesis on at the end. Bose wanted a derivation that treated light as particles from the first line.

#### Two Identical Coins

Counting how packets of light share energy across tiny phase-space cells of volume h³, Bose noticed why classical probability failed. Toss two distinct coins, a rupee and a paisa, and there are four equally likely outcomes: (H, H), (H, T), (T, H), and (T, T).

If two light quanta of the same frequency have no private labels at all, however, then "first photon in cell A, second in cell B" and "second in A, first in B" are the exact same physical state. Counting only how many particles sit in each cell gives three equally likely arrangements (two in A, two in B, or one in each). From this new rule of counting, Planck's radiation law dropped out in four pages without a single reference to classical wave electrodynamics.

#### The Postcard from Berlin

After *Philosophical Magazine* ignored his manuscript, Bose posted it to Einstein on 4 June 1924. On 2 July, Einstein sent a handwritten postcard to Dacca calling Bose's paper *"a most important contribution"*. Einstein translated the paper into German himself, published it in the August 1924 *Zeitschrift für Physik* with a praising note, and extended Bose's counting rule to massive atoms to predict the **Bose-Einstein condensate**.

After working in Europe with **Marie Curie** and meeting Einstein in Berlin, Bose returned to teach in Dacca and Calcutta and championed teaching science in Bengali. In 1945 **Paul Dirac** named every particle that obeys Bose's statistics and shares a single quantum state, including photons and gluons, a **boson**.
