:::scene Delft, 1586

In 1586, in the Dutch town of Delft, the Flemish engineer and mathematician Simon Stevin and his friend Jan Cornets de Groot climbed with two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, to drop them from a height of 30 feet (about 9.1 metres) onto a wooden board below. Tradition places their drop at the tower of the Nieuwe Kerk, the New Church of Delft.

#### Testing a two-thousand-year-old claim

Everyone who had studied university natural philosophy knew what was supposed to happen. According to Aristotle, heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones in proportion to their weight. If a ten-times-heavier lead ball fell ten times faster, it would hit the board when the lighter ball had fallen only 3 feet, and a listener below would hear two distinct bangs separated by a long gap.

Stevin and de Groot let the two lead balls go together. Down on the wooden board below, the two balls struck so closely that their impacts sounded like a single thud.

Most people have heard the story of Galileo Galilei dropping weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa. Yet that story comes only from a biography written long afterwards by Galileo's last pupil, Vincenzo Viviani, and Galileo himself never mentioned doing it. Stevin actually performed the drop with de Groot and published the result in Dutch in 1586, in *De Beghinselen der Weeghconst* (*The Principles of the Art of Weighing*), three years before Galileo arrived to teach at Pisa.

Why did Stevin use lead balls and a wooden board? In air, a wide, light sheet of paper is slowed by air resistance much more than a compact metal ball. Choosing two dense spheres of lead kept air drag tiny compared with weight, and dropping them onto a sounding board let the human ear judge whether they landed together.

#### The wreath of spheres on a ramp

In the same 1586 book, Stevin solved another puzzle: how hard do you have to pull to hold a heavy load still on a ramp?

He drew a triangular ramp with a gentle slope on the left and a steep slope, half as long, on the right. Over the triangle he draped a closed loop of fourteen identical, evenly spaced balls on a string, like a wreath (*clootcrans*). Four balls lie along the gentle slope and two along the steep slope, while eight hang symmetrically underneath.

If the four balls on the gentle side did not balance the two on the steep side, the loop would slide round by itself, placing four new balls on the left and two on the right in the exact same arrangement, and spin forever in perpetual motion. Because a loop of weights cannot drive itself forever, it must hang still. Cut away the symmetrical hanging piece at the bottom, and the four balls on the gentle ramp must balance the two balls on the steep ramp. Stevin put this diagram on the cover of his book with the Dutch motto *Wonder en is gheen wonder*: "Wonder is no wonder." Once you see the mechanism, the mystery vanishes.

#### Decimal fractions for everyone

Born in Bruges in 1548, Stevin worked as a bookkeeper before designing windmills, sluices, and fortifications in the Netherlands. In 1585 he published a short booklet called *De Thiende* (*The Tenth*), which introduced decimal fractions (0.1, 0.01, 0.001) to everyday European arithmetic so surveyors and merchants could calculate without awkward fractions.
