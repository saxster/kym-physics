:::scene Khodad, near Pune, 1996

Rising out of fields of sugarcane and onions around **Khodad**, eighty kilometres north of Pune, stand thirty parabolic dishes, each 45 metres across, wide enough to hold a cricket pitch inside its rim. Look up through one of the bowls against the blue Deccan sky, however, and you see clouds right through the reflector. Rather than heavy steel plates, **Govind Swarup** and his engineers wove the dishes of the **Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope** (**GMRT**) out of thin stainless-steel wire mesh stretched over rope trusses like a bicycle wheel. To metre-long radio waves from distant galaxies, that wire grid looks like a solid mirror; to the monsoon wind, it is an open screen through which the air blows with barely a push.

#### From Thakurdwara to Sydney and Stanford

Born on 23 March 1929 in Thakurdwara in Uttar Pradesh, Govind Swarup completed his MSc at Allahabad University in 1950, trained in solar radio astronomy under **Joseph Pawsey** in Sydney on a Colombo Plan fellowship, and earned his doctorate at Stanford University in 1961. When Swarup and three fellow Indian radio astronomers abroad wrote to **Homi Bhabha** offering to return and build a radio astronomy group at home, Bhabha cabled back within days inviting them to the **Tata Institute of Fundamental Research** (**TIFR**) in Bombay, which Swarup joined in 1963.

#### Tilting a Telescope with a Hill

To track a star across the sky as the Earth spins once every twenty-four hours, an equatorially mounted telescope must have its rotation axle pointed parallel to the Earth's axis, tilted at an angle equal to the observatory's geographic latitude. Tilting a 500-metre steel structure to 40° or 50° in Europe or North America is prohibitively expensive.

Swarup noticed that southern India sits only 11° north of the equator. If he laid a long cylindrical radio reflector along a natural hillside sloping north-to-south at 11°, its axle would automatically stand parallel to the Earth's rotation axis. A single mechanical drive turning the trough east to west could then track radio sources across the sky for ten hours a day at low cost.

In 1965 Swarup found the exact 11.4° north-south hill slope he needed near **Ooty** (11.4° N latitude) in the Nilgiris. Completed in 1970, the 530-metre **Ooty Radio Telescope** (**ORT**) measured the structures of hundreds of distant radio galaxies by timing the exact moments when the Moon passed in front of them.

#### The Bicycle-Wheel Dish

In the 1980s Swarup proposed building the world's largest telescope at longer **metre wavelengths** (150 MHz to 1,420 MHz), the band that detects redshifted 21 cm signals from neutral hydrogen gas in the early universe and millisecond pulsars.

Because a wave cannot pass through holes much smaller than its wavelength, a one-metre radio wave reflects cleanly off a wire mesh with centimetre openings. Swarup invented the **SMART** design (*Stretch Mesh Attached to Rope Trusses*): 0.55 mm stainless-steel wire mesh pulled into a parabola by tensioned steel ropes like bicycle spokes. Completed in 1996 near Pune, the thirty-dish GMRT array spans 25 kilometres and serves astronomers worldwide.
