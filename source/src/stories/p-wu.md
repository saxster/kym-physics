:::scene Washington, December 1956

In a low-temperature basement at the National Bureau of Standards in Washington, **Chien-Shiung Wu** and four cryogenic physicists watched the count rate from a thin crystal of radioactive **cobalt-60** cooled to 0.003 degrees above absolute zero (0.003 K). A strong magnetic field lined the spinning cobalt nuclei up like tiny tops turning the same way. If nature's laws were indifferent between left and right, as every textbook assumed, the beta-decay electrons shooting out of those nuclei would fly off equally toward the north and south poles of the magnet. Instead, most of the electrons shot out opposite to the nuclear spin. Nature's mirror had cracked.

#### From Mingde School to Columbia

Chien-Shiung Wu was born on 31 May 1912 in Liuhe near Shanghai, where her father founded the **Mingde Vocational Continuing School for Girls** so that local girls could learn mathematics and science. Boarding at the Suzhou Women's Normal School from age eleven, she graduated in physics from National Central University in Nanjing in 1934 and sailed to California in 1936 to earn her PhD at Berkeley under **Ernest Lawrence** and **Emilio Segrè**.

At Columbia University during the Second World War, her work on radioactive **xenon-135** explained why the first plutonium reactor at Hanford kept shutting down as xenon swallowed slow neutrons. After the war she became the world authority on **beta decay** (in which a nucleus turns a neutron into a proton while emitting an electron and an antineutrino), using ultra-thin radioactive films to confirm **Enrico Fermi**'s beta-decay spectrum.

#### The Question from Lee and Yang

In the spring of 1956, Columbia theorist **Tsung-Dao Lee** and **Chen-Ning Yang** of Princeton visited Wu's office in Pupin Hall. They suspected that **parity conservation** (the rule that the mirror image of any physical process behaves identically to the original) had never been tested in the weak nuclear interaction governing beta decay.

Wu saw how to test it: align the spins of cobalt-60 nuclei in a magnetic field and check whether beta electrons emerge equally along and opposite to the spin axis. To stop thermal jiggling from scrambling the nuclear spins, the crystal had to be cooled below 0.01 K.

#### Cancelling the Voyage Home

Wu and her husband, **Luke Chia-Liu Yuan**, held tickets on the *Queen Elizabeth* for their first trip home to China in twenty years. Wu cancelled her passage, partnered with **Ernest Ambler**'s cryogenic team in Washington, and commuted by train from Columbia all autumn.

Over Christmas 1956, whenever the team reversed the magnetic field, the electron count flipped with it. In a mirror, a spinning nucleus reverses its sense of rotation while an outgoing electron's path does not; the asymmetry proved that the weak interaction distinguishes left-handed from right-handed. Lee and Yang received the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics; Wu was omitted despite twenty-three nominations, and later won the first Wolf Prize in Physics in 1978.
