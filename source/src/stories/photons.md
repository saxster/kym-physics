:::scene Bern, 1905

In 1905 Albert Einstein was twenty-six and worked in the patent office in Bern, examining other people's inventions. That year he sent four papers to the journal *Annalen der Physik*. One was about the jiggling of pollen grains in water, one about space and time, one about the formula E = mc². The first of the four, received on 18 March and printed on 9 June, was about light, and it said something that most physicists found harder to accept than relativity. Light, it proposed, is not delivered smoothly. It arrives in packets.

When the Nobel committee finally gave Einstein the Physics prize for 1921, the citation named this work, on what is called the photoelectric effect, as the one specific discovery it was rewarding. It did not mention relativity.

#### Step 1: the puzzle

Shine light on a clean metal surface in a vacuum and, for some lights and some metals, electrons jump out of the metal. That is the photoelectric effect, and by 1905 careful measurements had shown four things about it that nobody could explain. First, for each metal there is a threshold: light below a certain frequency, that is, light that is too red, knocks out *no* electrons at all, however bright you make it. Second, light above the threshold knocks electrons out instantly, even when it is dim. Third, brighter light knocks out *more* electrons. Fourth, brighter light does not make them any faster; only bluer light does that.

If light were a smooth wave, a bright red lamp should pour energy into the metal until electrons boiled out of it. It does not happen. You can bathe the metal in red light all day and nothing emerges, then touch it with a faint violet glimmer and electrons fly.

#### Step 2: Einstein's packets

Einstein's answer was that light is delivered in packets, now called photons, and the energy of each packet depends only on the light's frequency:

:::eq
**E = h f**   (h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds)
:::

An electron takes the whole of one packet or nothing. To escape the metal it must pay an entry fee, the metal's binding energy, and keep the change as speed. So if one packet is worth less than the fee, no electron leaves, and piling up more packets does not help, because each electron is only ever handed one. That is the threshold. A packet worth more than the fee frees an electron at once, with the difference as kinetic energy. Brighter light means more packets, so more electrons, each with the same energy as before. Bluer light means bigger packets, so faster electrons. All four puzzles in one sentence.

:::work
**Violet light of wavelength 420 nm falls on calcium, whose binding energy is 2.71 eV.**
Each violet packet carries 2.95 eV. The electron pays 2.71 eV to leave and keeps 0.24 eV.
**A TV remote's infrared, wavelength 940 nm, falls on the same calcium.**
Each packet carries 1.32 eV, less than the 2.71 eV fee. No electron leaves, however bright the remote.
:::

Only packets above the line can free an electron; how many packets arrive makes no difference.

#### Step 3: so is light a wave or not?

It still travels like one. Light still spreads, bends round edges and makes interference fringes, as Young's card showed in 1803, and nothing in Einstein's paper undid that. What changed is how it *delivers* its energy: in packets, one at a time. Both are true at once, and the honest summary is simply that light travels like a wave and lands like a particle. If that sits uncomfortably, you are in good company; physicists took years to make peace with it.

The packets are so small that ordinary light seems smooth. A 100-watt bulb that puts a tenth of its power into yellow light sends out about 3 × 10¹⁹ packets every second. Nobody sees a flicker. The quantum shows itself only where one packet meets one electron.

#### Step 4: a letter from Dacca

:::scene Dacca, 4 June 1924

On 4 June 1924 a physics teacher at the University of Dacca, Satyendra Nath Bose, posted a letter to Einstein in Berlin. It began "Respected Sir". He had written a short paper about how to count packets of light, had sent it to the *Philosophical Magazine* in London, and had heard nothing back. He did not, he said, know enough German to have it published in a German journal himself; would Einstein read it, and, if he thought it worth anything, arrange for it to appear?

Einstein read it, translated it into German himself, sent Bose a postcard on 2 July saying so, and had it printed in August with a note of his own attached. Bose's way of counting turned out to describe not only light but a whole class of particles, which are now called bosons in his honour. A teacher in Dacca, with no answer from London, had put his name permanently into the vocabulary of physics.

:::careful
> "Light is a particle, not a wave."

It is neither word on its own. Light travels like a wave and delivers its energy in packets. Drop either half and you will get the wrong answer to some experiment.
:::

:::careful
> "Brighter light knocks out faster electrons."

Brighter light knocks out *more* electrons, each with the same energy as before. Only a higher frequency, bluer light, makes them faster. Below the threshold frequency, no brightness at all is enough.
:::

:::careful
> "The phone camera is seeing heat from the remote."

It is seeing light: near-infrared from a small LED in the remote, at a wavelength of about 940 nanometres, just beyond red. That is not the same as the warmth you feel from a hot cup, and the remote is not warm.
:::

:::home See the invisible
Point a TV remote at your phone's camera in a dim room and press a button while watching the screen. The remote's bulb, dark to your eye, flashes on the screen: the camera's sensor can see infrared at 940 nm that your eye cannot. If one camera shows nothing, try the other. Each flash is a stream of packets of about 1.3 eV, too small to free an electron from a metal and completely harmless.
:::
