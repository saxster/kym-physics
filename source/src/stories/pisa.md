:::scene Pisa, about 1590

You know the picture. A young professor climbs the Leaning Tower of Pisa with two balls, one heavy and one light. Below him stand the other professors, who believe Aristotle's rule that heavy things fall faster, and all the students. He lets go. One thud. Two thousand years of physics end in a couple of seconds.

It is a wonderful scene, and it almost certainly did not happen.

#### Where the story comes from

Every version of it goes back to one man. Vincenzo Viviani was Galileo's assistant and secretary in the last years of his life, when Galileo was old and blind. In 1654, twelve years after Galileo died, Viviani wrote a life of his master, and it was printed in 1717, seventy-five years after Galileo's death. In it he says that Galileo showed, by "repeated experiments made from the height of the Leaning Tower of Pisa in the presence of other professors and all the students", that weights of the same material fall at the same speed, whatever their size.

That is the only source. Nobody else who was in Pisa between 1589 and 1592, when Galileo taught there, mentions it. Galileo wrote a great deal about falling and never once mentions the tower. The same Viviani is also the only source for the story of Galileo timing a swinging lamp in Pisa cathedral with his pulse, and the lamp that hangs there now was put up after Galileo had left.

#### What Galileo actually wrote

Galileo did write about dropping two balls. He says he tested a cannonball against a musket ball and that they "did not quite fall together": the heavy one landed a little ahead. He was honest about it. The air holds the small ball back slightly, as Chapter 4 explains, so a real drop never gives the perfect single thud the legend wants.

His real weapon was not a drop at all but an argument, and it is one of the best in physics. Suppose, with Aristotle, that heavier things fall faster. Now tie a light ball to a heavy one with a string and drop the pair. The light ball, being slower, should hold the heavy one back, so the pair should fall more slowly than the heavy ball alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy ball alone, so it should fall faster. Both cannot be true. So the rule must be wrong, and the only way out is that all bodies fall alike. He did not need a tower for that; he needed a quiet room.

Most historians today think the tower drop was a thought experiment, or at most a private test. One serious scholar, Stillman Drake, thought it did happen, as a show for students, roughly as Viviani says. Either way, the old man telling the story to his young assistant may have remembered a demonstration that never quite took place.

#### Even the legend would have been messy

In the 1980s a historian, Thomas Settle, repeated the drop with real balls and found something odd: the lighter ball tended to pull ahead at the start. His explanation was that the hand holding the heavier ball tires and lets go a fraction later. A drop from the tower, done in front of a crowd, would have been a muddle of early releases and air resistance. Legends are tidier than experiments.

#### What really happened

In 1586, three years before Galileo arrived in Pisa, two men climbed about thirty feet up the tower of the Nieuwe Kerk in Delft, in the Netherlands. Simon Stevin, a mathematician, and his friend Jan Cornets de Groot dropped two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, onto a wooden board. "They fall together at the same time on the ground," Stevin wrote, and "this proves that Aristotle is wrong." He published it the same year. The Delft drop has its own page in this book, under Stevin's name, because it is the one that is documented.

#### How to weigh a story

You can test a historical story the way a physicist tests a claim: ask where it comes from. Who first told it? When? Were they there? Does the person it is about mention it themselves? The tower scores badly on every question: one teller, writing after the hero's death, who was not there, about a man who never mentioned it. Newton's apple scores better, since Newton told the story himself, though sixty years late. Stevin's drop scores best of all: he did it, wrote it down, and printed it within the year.

:::careful
> "Galileo proved that heavy and light things fall together by dropping them off the Leaning Tower."

The documented drop is Stevin's, at Delft, in 1586. Galileo's own test, by his own account, did not come out clean, and his real contribution was the argument with the tied balls and the ramps you meet in Chapter 1, which slowed falling down enough to measure it.
:::
