:::scene Near Dresden, Christmas night, 1758

On the night of 25 December 1758 a farmer called Johann Georg Palitzsch, who studied the sky in his spare time from his fields near Dresden, saw a faint comet. He was the first person on Earth to see it. Astronomers across Europe had been searching for it for months, because this comet had an appointment.

Edmond Halley, the friend who had badgered Newton into writing the *Principia*, had worked out that a comet seen before would come back on a long orbit, and had said it would return in 1758. By then Halley had been dead for sixteen years. The comet kept the appointment all the same, and it has been ==Halley's comet== ever since.

#### Step 1: three people and a mountain of arithmetic

Halley's prediction was not quite enough on its own, because a comet does not feel only the Sun. On its way out and back it passes the giant planets Jupiter and Saturn, and each of them tugs at it, speeding it up or slowing it down a little. Those tugs shift the date of its return. Newton's law tells you exactly how big each tug is at each moment, but there is no neat formula for adding them all up over decades. The only way is to calculate the path step by small step.

In Paris, three people did that: the mathematician Alexis Clairaut, the astronomer Jérôme Lalande, and Nicole-Reine Lepaute, who did a great share of the calculating. Over months of work they predicted that the comet would pass closest to the Sun around the middle of April 1759. Palitzsch saw it on Christmas night, three and a half months before that closest pass, as it came in.

A comet's path is a very long ellipse, far longer than any planet's. It spends most of its time crawling through the cold far reaches of its orbit, then rushes through its closest pass to the Sun in a few weeks. Every nudge along the way shifts when that rush arrives.

#### Step 2: a nudge is a message

Now the idea. Each small tug of one body on another, pulling an orbit off the plain ellipse that Kepler would have drawn, is called a *perturbation*. The Paris team used perturbations in the obvious direction: they knew where Jupiter and Saturn were, so they could work out how the comet would be nudged. But you can run the arithmetic backwards. If something is being nudged and you can measure the nudge, you can work out where the nudger must be. A pull is a message about the thing that is doing the pulling.

That reversal is what found a planet.

:::scene Berlin, 23 September 1846

The planet Uranus was drifting off course. Year after year it strayed from the positions that the tables, worked out from Newton's law, said it should occupy. In Paris, Urbain ==Le Verrier== took the drift as a message. He supposed that an unseen planet beyond Uranus was tugging it, and he calculated where that planet would have to be to produce exactly the drift observed. Then he wrote the position in a letter and posted it to Johann Galle at the Berlin observatory.

The letter arrived on 23 September 1846. That same night Galle and his assistant Heinrich d'Arrest pointed their telescope where the letter said, and there, within about one degree of Le Verrier's position, was a planet nobody had ever knowingly seen. It was named ==Neptune==.

:::work
How close is one degree? The full Moon is about half a degree across.
Le Verrier's pen had put Neptune within about two Moon-widths of where it really was, in a sky that measures 360 degrees all the way round.
:::

Two footnotes belong to the story. In England, John Couch Adams had made a similar calculation, but his position was about 12° out, some two dozen Moon-widths, and it played no part in the discovery. And James Challis had actually seen Neptune twice while searching, and had not recognised it as a planet. Seeing is not the same as knowing what you have seen.

#### Step 3: why it mattered

Newton's law had been built to explain the apple and the Moon, and then the planets already known. Here it reached past everything it had been made for: it took a wobble in one planet and said *there is another one, there, look*. That is the strongest test a law of nature can pass, a prediction of something nobody has seen, and it is why Neptune is called the first planet discovered by mathematics.

It is also a warning against overconfidence. The law is simple; the sky is not. With two bodies, the Sun and one planet, Newton's law gives a perfect ellipse. Add a third and there is no exact answer at all, only step-by-step arithmetic that Lepaute and her colleagues did by hand for months and that computers now do in seconds. The Moon's own motion, pulled by both Earth and Sun, kept the best mathematicians of the 1700s busy for the whole century.

:::careful
> "Newton's law predicts the sky perfectly."

It predicts the sky *very well*, which is different. Every extra body adds tugs that can only be followed step by step; that is why the Paris team needed months for one comet, and why Uranus's tables went wrong in the first place: they left out a planet nobody knew about.
:::

:::careful
> "Halley discovered Halley's comet."

He never saw its 1758 return; he had died in 1742. What he did was recognise that a comet seen before would come back, and say when. The comet carries his name because he predicted it, not because he found it. A farmer near Dresden found it.
:::
