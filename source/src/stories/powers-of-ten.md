:::scene Juhu beach, Mumbai, any evening

Pick up one grain of sand. It is about a millimetre across: a thousandth of a metre. You are about a metre and a half tall. The Earth under your feet is about ten million metres across. The Sun is about a hundred billion metres away, and the Milky Way, faint overhead if the lights would let you see it, is about a thousand billion billion metres from side to side. Go the other way: a wave of light is about a millionth of a metre long, an atom of hydrogen a ten-billionth, and the proton at its centre a thousandth of that again.

Numbers like these cannot be held in the head as a string of zeros. Physicists hold them as *steps*, and each step is a ten.

#### Step 1: 10³

Ten multiplied by itself three times is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000, and it is written 10³. The little raised number counts the tens. So 10⁶ is a million, a one followed by six zeros, and 10² is a hundred. These are the ==powers of ten==.

They have one lovely property. To multiply two of them, you *add* the little numbers: 10³ × 10³ = 10⁶, because a thousand thousands is a million. That is why the prefixes you already know are powers of ten: kilo means ×10³, so a kilometre is 10³ metres.

#### Step 2: 10⁻³

A minus sign on the little number means divide instead of multiply. 10⁻³ is 1 ÷ 1,000 = 0.001, a thousandth: the sand grain, in metres. Milli means ×10⁻³; centi ×10⁻²; micro ×10⁻⁶; nano ×10⁻⁹. And 10⁰, no tens at all, is simply 1: you, roughly, in metres.

#### Step 3: writing a big number small

The mass of the Earth is about 5,970,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg. Nobody can read that, and nobody can check it. Written as 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg it is easy to read and easy to check: the power of ten says what scale you are on, and the number in front gives the detail. This way of writing, a number between 1 and 10 times a power of ten, is called ==scientific notation==; at school you may meet it as ==standard form==. It works for small numbers too: an electron's mass is 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

:::work
**A day and a year, in standard form**
A day: 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 s = 8.64 × 10⁴ s.
A year: about 365 days, so about 3.16 × 10⁷ s.
The powers tell you at once that a year is between a hundred and a thousand times a day (365 lies between 10² and 10³).
:::

#### Step 4: the zoom

Here is the step most people get wrong. Ten times bigger, and then ten times bigger again, is not twenty times bigger. It is a hundred times. Each step *multiplies*. Three steps is a thousand times, six steps a million. From a grain of sand to you is three steps. From a hydrogen atom to the Sun is nineteen. "Too small to see" is not one size: a bacterium and an electron are both invisible, and in mass they are fifteen steps of ten apart, more steps than separate you from the Sun.

:::fig c0_ladder() | A ladder of sizes from a proton to the Milky Way. Every mark is ten times the one before. The things on it are 36 steps apart from end to end; written out, that ratio would be a one followed by 36 zeros.

#### Step 5: a ruler made of tens

The ladder in the figure is drawn so that each step of ×10 has the same width. A ruler like that is a ==logarithmic scale==, and it is the only kind of ruler that can fit a proton and a galaxy on one page. Ordinary rulers add: each centimetre is one more. This one multiplies: each mark is ten times the last. It has one odd property: it never reaches zero, because below any mark there is always another, a tenth of it, and another below that, for ever. The scale for earthquakes works this way, and so does the decibel scale for loudness that you will meet in Chapter 9, where every 10 decibels is a sound ten times as intense.

:::careful
> "10⁶ is twice as big as 10³, because 6 is twice 3."

It is a thousand times as big. The little numbers are counts of tens, and three more tens means multiplying by ten three more times. A million is a thousand thousands, not two thousands.
:::

:::home A page you cannot measure
A single page of a notebook is too thin for a ruler. So measure a hundred of them pressed flat: about a centimetre, say, which is 10⁻² m. One page is then 10⁻² ÷ 10² = 10⁻⁴ m, a tenth of a millimetre. Now work out how many pages, stacked, would reach the top of your head: your height in metres divided by 10⁻⁴. For 1.5 m that is 15,000 pages, or 1.5 × 10⁴. Check it against the number of pages in a notebook and the thickness of the notebook.
:::
