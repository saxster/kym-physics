:::scene Cambridge, 1666

In the beginning of 1666, by his own account, Isaac Newton bought a triangular glass prism "to try therewith the celebrated phœnomena of Colours", as he put it in the old spelling. Everyone knew what a prism did. Hold it in sunlight and a patch of rainbow colours appears on the wall. The usual explanation was that pure white light had been stained on its way through the glass, the way clear water is coloured by passing through a tea bag.

Newton set up to look properly. "Having darkened my chamber, and made a small hole in my window-shuts" to let in a thin beam of sunlight, he placed the prism in the beam so that the light fell on the wall across the room. He expected a round patch of colour, because the hole was round and the beam that came through it was round, and the known rules of bending said a round beam should bend as a whole and stay round. Instead the patch was an oblong: 13¼ inches long and 2⅝ wide, about five times longer than it was broad, red at one end and violet at the other. The beam had not just been bent. It had been fanned out, and some of the light was bending more than the rest.

#### Step 1: two ideas and a test

There were two ways to explain the oblong. The first: the glass stains the light, adding colours as it goes, and somehow stretches it too. The second: the colours were in the sunlight all along, mixed together to look white, and each colour bends by a slightly different amount, so the prism sorts them out. The first idea was the one everyone believed. The second was Newton's.

How do you decide between them? Newton saw that the two ideas predict different things for a second prism. If glass stains light, then taking one colour out of the fan and sending it through a second prism should stain it again and change it. If glass only sorts, a single colour has nothing left to be sorted: it should bend, and come out the same colour it went in.

#### Step 2: the experimentum crucis

"I took two boards," he wrote, "and placed one of them close behind the Prisme at the window." The board had a small hole, so that only one colour of the fan passed through it. Further on stood a second board with a hole, and behind that a second prism. He turned the first prism a little to send each colour in turn through the holes.

The red came out of the second prism red, bent but otherwise unchanged. The blue came out blue, bent more than the red had been. No new colours appeared anywhere. He called it his ==experimentum crucis==, the decisive experiment, and his conclusion in the letter is the whole of the matter in one sentence: "Light it self is a Heterogeneous mixture of differently refrangible Rays." Refrangible was his word for how much a ray bends. ==White light== is not pure. It is every colour at once, and a prism adds nothing to it. It sorts.

#### Step 3: why glass sorts

Light slows down when it enters glass or water, and slowing at a slant swings the beam round, which is why it bends. What Newton found is that the slowing is not quite the same for every colour: violet slows a little more than red, so it bends a little more. The spreading of colours that comes from bending them by different amounts is ==dispersion==.

The differences are tiny. Water slows red light by a factor of 1.331 and violet by 1.342. Send a beam into water at 45° and the red continues at 32.09°, the violet at 31.80°: three-tenths of a degree apart. Glass does a little better, which is why Newton used it, and across a long room a small difference in angle grows into a wide band of colour on the far wall. That is also why a rainbow needs a whole sky of raindrops and a dark cloud behind it to show up at all.

#### Step 4: a rainbow is a million prisms

Sunlight enters a raindrop, is bent and sorted at the surface, bounces off the inside of the back of the drop, and is bent and sorted again on the way out, so the colours come back towards the Sun's side of the sky. That is why you only ever see a rainbow with the Sun behind you. Each drop sends one colour to your eye, and millions of drops at slightly different heights send you the whole band. The band is continuous; "seven colours" is a habit of naming, not a fact about the light.

Newton was not the first to study rainbows with care. Some three and a half centuries before him, Kamal al-Din al-Farisi and Theodoric of Freiberg, working separately, filled glass spheres with water to make giant raindrops and traced the light through them, building on the work of Ibn al-Haytham. Nobody before Newton, though, had shown that white light was a mixture.

#### Step 5: the letter and the quarrel

Newton wrote all this up in a letter dated from Cambridge on 6 February 1672, which was read to the Royal Society in London and printed. Then the arguing began. Other natural philosophers, Robert Hooke the loudest among them, disputed what the colours meant and what light was made of, and Newton came to dislike the quarrelling so much that he held back his full book on light for more than thirty years. The experiment itself was never seriously in doubt. Anyone with two prisms and a sunny day could repeat it, and they did.

:::careful
> "The prism puts the colours into the light."

Then a second prism would put in more, and it does not. A single colour sent through a second prism comes out the same colour, only bent. The colours were already in the sunlight, travelling together; the prism separates them because each bends by a slightly different amount. White light is a mixture that looks like nothing at all.

:::home A prism from the kitchen
On a sunny morning, stand a plain glass of water on a windowsill where the Sun shines through it, and look on the floor or the wall on the far side for a small band of colour, red at one edge and violet at the other. A round glass bends light the way a prism does. For a rainbow of your own, go out on the balcony with the Sun behind you and spray a fine mist from a plant sprayer: look into the mist, away from the Sun. A CD's rainbow looks similar but is made a different way, by diffraction at the fine tracks on its surface, which is a story of its own.
