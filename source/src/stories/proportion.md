:::scene A dark room in Mumbai, with a torch

Switch off the lights, stand a metre from a wall and shine a torch at it. There is a bright patch. Step back to two metres. The patch is bigger and dimmer. How much bigger, and how much dimmer? The answer to that small question turns out to be the answer to how gravity weakens with distance, how sound fades across a room, and why the Sun does not roast the Earth. Physics is full of questions of the form "if I double this, what happens to that?", and the answers come in only a few shapes.

#### Step 1: double it, double it

The simplest shape: double one thing and the other doubles too. A 1 kg bag of atta weighs 9.8 N; a 2 kg bag weighs 19.6 N. Walk at a steady pace for twice the time and you cover twice the distance. One quantity is ==proportional== to the other, and the relationship is called ==proportionality==. On a graph it is a straight line through the origin: nothing in, nothing out.

#### Step 2: double it, four times

The next shape is the ==square law==: double the input and the output goes up four times, because 2 × 2 = 4. Triple it and the output goes up nine times. You have met it already if you read the story of 's Gravesande's clay: a ball moving twice as fast has four times the energy of motion, and makes a dent four times as deep. Galileo's balls on their ramp obeyed a square law too: the distance rolled in 1, 2, 3 and 4 seconds went 1 : 4 : 9 : 16, so the gaps between the seconds went 1, 3, 5, 7. And a wave with twice the height carries four times the energy, which is why a big wave does far more than twice the damage of a small one.

#### Step 3: double it, halve it

Some pairs go the other way. Push a trolley with the same force and double its load: the acceleration halves. Share the same cake among twice as many people: each slice halves. The output is proportional to one *over* the input. Double, and you get a half.

#### Step 4: double it, a quarter

Now the torch. At one metre, the beam lights a patch one square wide. At two metres, the beam has spread sideways to twice the width and twice the height, so it lights four squares. The torch is sending out the same light, so each square now gets a quarter of what the single square got. At three metres, nine squares, each a ninth as bright. Twice as far, four times as weak: a square law turned upside down, the *inverse square*.

:::work
**The torch**
At 1 m: 1 × 1 = 1 square, full brightness.
At 2 m: 2 × 2 = 4 squares, each ¼ as bright.
At 3 m: 3 × 3 = 9 squares, each ⅑ as bright.
The same light, spread over an area that grows with the square of the distance.
:::

:::fig s_torch_patch() | A torch and three walls, at one, two and three metres. The lit patch is 1 square, then 4, then 9; the same light is spread over four and then nine times the area, so each square is a quarter and then a ninth as bright. Twice as far, four times as weak.

Anything that spreads out evenly from a point follows this law, because the surface it spreads over grows as the square of the distance. Sound does: twice as far from a speaker, a quarter of the intensity, because the same sound power is spread over a sphere four times the area. Gravity does, and that was Newton's great check in Chapter 5. The Moon is about 60 Earth-radii away, so Earth's pull on it should be 1 ÷ 60², or 1 ÷ 3600, of the pull at the surface: 9.8 ÷ 3600 ≈ 0.0027 m/s², and that is exactly the acceleration the Moon needs to stay on its orbit.

#### Step 5: the doubling test

To find which shape a pair of quantities follows, do one experiment: double the input and see what the output does. Doubles: proportional. Four times: square law. Halves: inverse. A quarter: inverse square. One doubling tells you the shape; a second doubling, to four times, confirms it. This test runs underneath every "law" in the book.

:::careful
> "Twice as far from the torch means half as bright."

A quarter. The beam spreads in two directions at once, across and up, so the patch grows in area as the square of the distance, and the same light has to cover four times the area. Half is the answer for things that spread along a line, like paint from a brush; light, sound and gravity spread in all directions.
:::

:::home The torch on the wall
In a dark room, shine a torch straight at a wall from exactly 1 m and mark the edges of the bright patch with two bits of tape. Step back to exactly 2 m and mark again. Measure both widths: the second should be about twice the first, so the patch has four times the area. The torch has not changed; the same light is spread four times thinner. If you have a phone with a light-meter app, hold it at the wall each time and see whether the reading drops to about a quarter.
:::
