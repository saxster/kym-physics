:::scene A hospital in Mumbai, any afternoon

A girl of twelve has a chest X-ray. It takes a fraction of a second; a machine clicks, and she goes home. On the way out she wonders: is she radioactive now? If she hugs her little brother, will some of it rub off on him? Should she keep away from the dog?

Asked questions like these, between 63 and 75 per cent of students in three separate studies said yes: things that have been near radiation become radioactive. The answer is no. The reason is worth more than the answer, because it tells you what radiation is.

#### Step 1: what the word means

"Radiation" means energy travelling outwards from a source. It is a wide word. The light from this page is radiation. So is the warmth from a cup of chai on your hands, the invisible flash from a TV remote that a phone camera can see, the radio waves that carry a cricket commentary, and the X-rays that made the picture of the girl's lungs. All of these are electromagnetic waves on the same ladder, from radio at the bottom to gamma rays at the top; the story *Light is electromagnetic* lays the ladder out. And light arrives in packets, as *Light in packets* explains, so a beam of radiation is a stream of packets, each carrying an amount of energy that depends on where it sits on the ladder.

#### Step 2: what makes some of it dangerous

Each packet of visible light carries between about 1.6 and 3.3 electron-volts of energy. The electrons that hold a molecule together are bound by about 10 electron-volts, and knocking an electron clean out of an atom, ionising it, takes anywhere from 10 to 1,000. So a visible packet cannot do it, and neither can infrared or radio, however many packets arrive: each electron only ever meets one packet at a time. Those lower rungs can harm you only by heating, through enormous numbers of packets acting together, which is what a microwave oven does to food.

Ultraviolet packets, X-rays and gamma rays carry more energy per packet than a molecule's bonds can withstand. They can break molecules and ionise atoms, which is why they are called ionising radiation, and why sunburn, X-ray shielding and radiation safety are all about the top of the ladder and not the bottom.

:::scene Paris, 1896

The top of the ladder has a second source besides hot things and X-ray tubes. In 1896 Henri Becquerel in Paris found that a lump of uranium ore, left on photographic plates wrapped in black paper, darkened them through the wrapping. Something was coming out of the uranium itself, with no sunlight and no electricity involved. The atoms' nuclei were unstable, and as each one rearranged itself it threw out a packet or a particle carrying around a million electron-volts, a hundred thousand times what an ordinary chemical change releases. A substance that does this is radioactive, and the behaviour is radioactivity. Three kinds of emission turned up: alpha particles, which are stopped soonest; beta particles, which travel farther; and gamma rays, which go farthest of all.

#### Step 3: being hit is not the same as carrying

Here is the heart of it. A diagnostic X-ray exposes the patient to a beam without leaving radioactive material in the body. The CDC distinguishes that exposure from contamination. Food treated with approved irradiation methods does not become radioactive either; the permitted kinds and energies are chosen for that purpose. These examples do not mean that every kind of radiation at every energy behaves the same way. Exposure can still harm tissue while it happens, which is why the amount and purpose of the beam matter.

Contamination is a different thing. It means radioactive *material*, dust or liquid whose own nuclei are unstable, on your skin, hair or clothes, or breathed in or swallowed. That material keeps emitting wherever it is, and it can be carried from place to place, washed off, or passed on. The X-rayed girl has been *exposed*; she carries nothing, so she can hug anyone she likes. A worker who gets radioactive dust on his sleeve is *contaminated*; the dust is the problem, and taking off the sleeve removes most of it.

:::fig s_exposure_vs_contamination() | Left: an X-ray beam passes through a person and is gone; afterwards nothing stays behind. Right: radioactive dust on a sleeve keeps emitting, and can be carried off or washed off. The first is exposure; the second is contamination.

One honest footnote. "Radiation never makes anything radioactive" is also too simple. Neutrons, which come from some nuclear reactions, *can* make the nuclei they hit unstable. That is precisely why neutrons are not used to irradiate food, and why the true rule is about which kind of radiation, not about radiation in general.

#### Step 4: how long it lasts

An unstable nucleus does not decay on a timetable. For any one nucleus the moment is random. But take a large number of them and the pattern is exact: in a fixed time, called the half-life, half of them will have decayed, whichever half that turns out to be. Wait another half-life and half the remainder go, and so on. After ten half-lives, about one part in a thousand is left.

:::fig s_halflife_c14() | Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. Every 5,730 years, half of what is left decays: 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%. Counting what remains in old wood or bone is how archaeologists date it.

Half-lives span an absurd range, from less than 10⁻²³ seconds to more than 10¹⁶ years. Carbon-14's is 5,730 years, which makes it a clock for things that were alive within the last fifty thousand years or so. Caesium-137's is 30.2 years. Uranium-238's is 4.5 billion years, about the age of the Sun, which is why there is still plenty of it in the rocks.

#### Step 5: the background you live in

You are being hit by ionising radiation now. Cosmic rays from space give the average person about 0.4 millisievert a year; the rocks and soil add more, up to ten times more in some places than others; the food you eat contains traces of radioactive atoms, so, in the words of one textbook, we are all a little radioactive. A twelve-hour flight to London adds about 0.02 to 0.03 millisievert, roughly three weeks' worth of the average cosmic dose, because the air above you is thinner. Airline crew pick up about 2 millisievert a year. None of this makes anyone radioactive. What matters is the dose: how much ionising energy was deposited, and in how much of you.

:::careful
> "Anything that has been near radiation becomes radioactive."

A diagnostic X-ray does not leave radioactive material in you. Radioactive *material* on you or inside you is contamination and keeps emitting. Some other exposures, such as neutron irradiation, can change nuclei themselves. The true rule depends on the kind and energy of radiation; exposure and contamination are different questions.
:::

:::careful
> "All radiation is dangerous."

Radio waves and visible light are radiation too. What matters is the energy per packet, which decides whether it can break molecules, and then the dose. A phone camera watching a TV remote is watching radiation, and it is completely harmless.
:::

:::home A half-life you can toss
Take 32 coins. Toss them all, remove every one that lands heads, and count what is left; write the number down. Toss the survivors, remove the heads again, and repeat until none remain. Each round is one half-life: about 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, 0, with wobbles, because for any one coin the result is chance while for many the halving is steady. That is exactly how a lump of radioactive material behaves, with years in place of tosses.
:::
