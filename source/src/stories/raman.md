:::scene The Mediterranean Sea, September 1921

The liner *Narkunda* is steaming east, bound for Bombay, and a physicist from Calcutta is standing at the rail looking at the sea through a small crystal. C. V. Raman is thirty-two and on his way home to India, and the Mediterranean around him is a deep, dark blue.

He knows the standard explanation. Lord Rayleigh, the English physicist who had explained why the sky is blue, had said that the sea is blue because it reflects the sky. Raman has a way to test that, right here at the rail. The crystal in his hand is a Nicol prism, a piece of mineral cut so that it lets through only light that is shaking in one direction, a polarising filter. Light reflected from the surface of water shakes mostly in one direction, so a Nicol prism held at the right angle can block the reflected sky almost completely. He turns the prism until the reflected sky is gone. If Rayleigh is right, the blue should go with it.

The sea is still blue. With the reflection removed, the colour remains, so it must be coming from inside the water. Raman writes it up on board, a short letter to the journal *Nature* called "The Colour of the Sea", and when the *Narkunda* docks at Bombay he posts it.

#### Step 1: how a crystal can delete a reflection

Light is a sideways shake. When a beam meets a water surface at a slant, the little that bounces off is mostly shaking parallel to the surface, side to side. At one particular slant, 53° from the vertical for water, which means looking down at the sea about 37° below the horizon, the reflected light is shaking side to side and nothing else. A filter set to pass only up-and-down shaking blocks it entirely.

What such a filter does not block is the light that comes up from inside the water. That light has been scattered about inside and shakes every which way, so the filter takes half of it and lets the rest through. Hold the filter right, then, and the reflected sky vanishes while the water's own light stays. The surface, by the way, reflects very little in the first place: of sunlight falling straight down onto calm water, only about 2 in every 100 parts bounce back. The rest goes in. Whatever the sea does with that light is what the sea's colour is made of.

#### Step 2: what the water does with the light

Raman's explanation was that the water does what the air does. Sunlight enters the sea, its molecules scatter a share of it, blue most of all, and some of that scattered light comes back up to your eye. The sea's blue and the sky's blue are cousins, both made by scattering; one is not a reflection of the other. His test did not say everything there is to say about the colour of the sea. It settled one thing: the blue is the water's own, and a question that had been argued about could now be argued with evidence.

It also set Raman thinking about scattering, and he went on thinking about it for seven years.

:::scene Calcutta, February 1928

At the Indian Association for the Cultivation of Science in Calcutta, Raman and his students had spent those years studying how liquids scatter light. By 1928 he had a young colleague, K. S. Krishnan, twenty-nine years old, and in February the two of them found something that nobody expected.

Shine light of one colour into a liquid, and look at the light scattered sideways. Almost all of it comes out the same colour it went in; that is the ordinary scattering of the sky and the sea. But a small fraction of it comes out a *different* colour, shifted a little, and the size of the shift depends on the liquid. Krishnan's diary records the work day by day from 5 February. On 9 February Raman said the discovery should be called the "Raman–Krishnan effect". On 28 February they announced it, and the diary breaks off in mid-sentence after that day. The world's books call it the ==Raman effect==, but Krishnan's name belongs in the story, and Raman himself put it there first.

In 1930 Raman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics "for his work on the scattering of light and for the discovery of the effect named after him". In India, 28 February is now ==National Science Day==.

#### Step 3: why a changed colour matters

Ordinary scattering is a catch and a re-send: the molecule shakes with the light and re-broadcasts the same colour. In the Raman effect something is exchanged. The light hands a little of its energy to the molecule and sets it vibrating, and light with a little less energy is a little redder; now and then an already-vibrating molecule hands energy back, and the light comes out a little bluer. (Why light's energy and its colour go together is a question for Chapter 15.) Each kind of molecule vibrates in its own ways, so each gives its own set of shifts. Measure the shifted colours, and you can tell what the liquid is made of without touching it. The sea had led Raman to a fingerprint for molecules.

:::careful
> "Raman discovered why the sky is blue."

That was ordinary scattering, which keeps the colour, and Rayleigh had explained it fifty years before. Raman showed that the sea's blue is its own, and then found the rare scattering that *changes* colour. And "the light scattered by a liquid changes colour" is wrong too: almost all of it keeps its colour, and only a tiny fraction shifts. The smallness of the fraction is why the discovery took seven years and a careful laboratory, and why nobody can see it at home.
:::

:::home Raman's test at the sea wall
Borrow a pair of polarised sunglasses and first check them on a phone or laptop screen: if the screen darkens as you slowly turn the lenses, they are polarised. (If nothing changes, they are only tinted and will not work.) Then, with an adult, go to Marine Drive on a bright day, look at the sea about a third of the way down from the horizon to your feet, and tilt your head slowly from one shoulder to the other. The glare on the water brightens and dims as you tilt. The sea's blue stays. You have just repeated Raman's experiment, with sunglasses instead of a crystal.
