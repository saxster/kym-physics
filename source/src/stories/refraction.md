:::scene Your kitchen, this afternoon

Drop a coin into an empty steel tumbler and look at it from above. Now pour in water, slowly, and keep looking. The coin seems to rise. The bottom of the tumbler looks nearer than it did, and the coin with it, though neither has moved a millimetre. Stand a pencil in a glass half full of water and look from the side: the pencil appears bent, or broken, exactly at the waterline.

Nothing has happened to the coin or the pencil. Something has happened to the light on its way from them to you. It has changed direction where it left the water, and your eye, which assumes light arrives in straight lines, has been fooled about where it came from.

The rule for how much light bends at a surface was written down in 984 by Ibn Sahl, and found again about six centuries later by Willebrord Snell in Leiden, around 1621, so that it carries Snell's name. Neither of them could say *why* light bends. That came later, and it is the simplest part of the story.

#### Step 1: light bends because it slows

Light travels more slowly in water than in air: three-quarters as fast, 2.25 hundred million metres a second instead of 3.00. Now picture a beam meeting the water at a slant. The side of the beam that reaches the water first slows down first, while the other side is still moving at full speed in the air. The fast side swings round the slow side, the way a lawnmower swings when one wheel runs off the path onto thick grass, and the whole beam turns towards the perpendicular to the surface. Leaving the water, the reverse happens: the side that reaches the air first speeds up first, and the beam turns away from the perpendicular. A beam that meets the surface head-on slows down but has no reason to turn, so it goes straight.

This change of direction at a boundary is ==refraction==, and light that has done it has been ==refracted==. The cause is the change of speed. Not weight, not density, not any pull from the water: speed.

How much a material slows light is its ==refractive index==, the speed of light in empty space divided by the speed in the material. Water's is 1.333, ordinary glass about 1.52, diamond 2.42. A bigger index means more slowing and more bending, which is why a diamond flashes and a glass of water only bends things a little.

#### Step 2: the rule

The rule Ibn Sahl and Snell found, ==Snell's law==, fixes the angles. For light passing from air into water, a beam arriving at 45° to the perpendicular continues at 32°; one arriving at 30° continues at 22°. At school you will meet it as a rule about sines, and it works for any wave that changes speed at a boundary: ripples running from deep water into shallow, sound passing from warm air into cold, light passing from air into glass.

#### Step 3: the coin

Light from the coin heads up to the surface at a slant and bends away from the perpendicular as it leaves the water. Your eye catches that bent ray and traces it straight back, as eyes do, to a point above and nearer than the coin. Every ray from the coin is bent the same way, so the whole coin appears to have risen. The coin has not moved. Its image has.

:::work
**How much does it rise?** Looking straight down, the water's depth appears divided by its refractive index.
A coin under 10 cm of water: 10 ÷ 1.333 = 7.5 cm. It looks 2.5 cm nearer than it is.
Under 8 cm of water: 8 ÷ 1.333 = 6.0 cm.
Looking in at a slant, it looks shallower still.
:::

Your eye traces the arriving ray straight back (dashed) to a place higher and nearer than the coin. The coin has not moved; its image has.

This is why a pond always looks shallower than it is, and why people who dive into unfamiliar water can hurt themselves on a bottom that looked further away. It is also why your legs look short when you stand in a swimming pool: the light from your feet bends on its way out, and your eye traces it back to feet that are not there.

#### Step 4: a curve makes a lens

At a flat surface every part of a beam bends by the same amount, so the beam stays a beam. Curve the surface, and different parts of the beam meet it at different slants and bend by different amounts. Shape the curve right, bulging outwards like a lentil, and all the rays of a parallel beam can be brought together at one point, the focus. A piece of glass or water shaped to do this is a ==lens==, and lenses are what the rest of this story is about.

A magnifying glass is a lens. Hold one in sunlight and it gathers the whole beam that falls on it into a point so bright and hot it can scorch paper, which is why you must never let a lens focus sunlight anywhere near an eye. Every part of the lens helps to form its image: cover half the lens and you still see the whole image, only dimmer. A drop of water bulges too. Put a drop on a sheet of clear plastic laid over newsprint and look through it: the letters beneath are magnified. You have made a lens out of water.

#### Step 5: the two lenses in your head

Your eye is a lens system, and it forms a real, upside-down image on the retina at the back, like the pinhole camera's picture on its screen but far brighter. The surprising part is which piece does the work. Most of the bending happens at the cornea, the clear front of the eye, because air to cornea is the biggest change of speed on the way in; the cornea does about two-thirds of the job. The lens inside, behind the pupil, does the fine tuning. Muscles around it make it fatter to focus on near things and let it flatten for far ones, and for a young eye the nearest comfortable point is about 25 cm away.

Open your eyes underwater and everything blurs. The cornea's index is 1.38, and water's is 1.33, so the change of speed at the cornea nearly vanishes and two-thirds of the eye's bending goes missing. A swimming mask puts a layer of air back in front of the eye and the bending returns. Glasses work on the same principle. A short-sighted eye bends light too strongly, so its glasses bend it slightly outwards first; a long-sighted eye bends too weakly, so its glasses add a little bending. Glasses change the light on its way in. They do not change the eye.

:::careful
> "The water pulls the light towards it, and that is why it bends."

Nothing pulls. Light slows down in water, and when a slanting beam slows on one side before the other it swings round. Leaving the water it speeds up and swings the other way. If it were a pull, light leaving water would bend the same way as light entering it, and it bends the opposite way.
:::

:::home The coin, the pencil and a drop
Put a coin in a steel tumbler and look at it from above as you pour in water: watch it seem to rise. Stand a pencil in a glass half full of water and look from the side, then from above, then from below the waterline, and notice where it looks bent and where it looks whole. For the lens, lay a piece of clear plastic (the lid of a food box works) over a page of small print, put one drop of water on it, and read through the drop. Then flatten the drop with a fingertip and read again: a flatter drop magnifies less. The curve is the lens.
:::
