:::scene Pisa, about 1583

The story goes like this. Galileo Galilei, nineteen years old and a student at the university, is sitting through a long service in the cathedral at Pisa. A lamp hanging from the ceiling on a chain has been left swinging, and Galileo watches it. The swings slowly die down, from wide arcs to small ones, and yet, as far as he can tell, each swing takes the same time as the one before. He has no watch, so he times the lamp against his own pulse. Wide swing or narrow swing: the same number of heartbeats.

It is a good story, and you should know that it is a legend. It comes from one source only, Vincenzo Viviani, who was Galileo's assistant in the last years of Galileo's life and wrote it down long afterwards, without a date. Galileo never told it himself. The lamp that hangs in the cathedral today was put up after Galileo had left Pisa.

Here is the true part. Galileo really did study swinging weights, and the rule he found is where this story starts. From there we go to a child on a swing, a singer and a wine glass, and two bridges that moved when they should not have.

#### Step 1: every swing has its own rhythm

Tie a key to a metre of thread, pull it a little to one side and let go. It swings across and back, and one complete swing, out and home again, takes about two seconds. A weight swinging like this is a ==pendulum==, and any motion that repeats itself in this way, back and forth about a resting place, is an ==oscillation==.

Now change things and watch what happens to the two seconds. Pull the key further to one side before you let go: it travels further, but each swing still takes about two seconds, because it moves faster. Tie on a heavier key: still two seconds. The mass does not matter at all. Only one thing changes the time, and that is the length of the thread. A thread four times as long swings twice as slowly; a thread a quarter as long swings twice as fast.

So a pendulum has a rhythm of its own, fixed by its length. Physicists count it as swings per second and call it the ==natural frequency==: the rate at which a thing oscillates when nothing is pushing it and nothing is holding it back. A pendulum has one. So does a playground swing, a guitar string, a wine glass, a tall building and a bridge. Anything that can wobble has a rhythm it wobbles at when left alone.

One honest footnote to the legend. "Wide swings take the same time as narrow ones" is nearly true. For small swings the time hardly changes at all. For big swings it grows a little: a swing out to 60° from the vertical takes about 7% longer than a tiny one. A pulse could never have noticed; a phone stopwatch can.

#### Step 2: pushing in time

Watch a parent pushing a small child on a swing. The swing has its own rhythm. If its chains are 2.5 m long, one swing out and back takes about 3.2 s. The parent does not shove. They give a small push each time the seat comes back to them, and the swings grow, and grow, until the child is flying.

Why does a small push do so much? Each push adds a little energy of motion to the swing. If the pushes come at the swing's own rhythm, every push lands just as the seat is moving away from the pusher, so every push speeds it up. The small additions add up, swing after swing.

Now try pushing at the wrong rhythm, say twice a second. Some pushes land while the seat is moving away and speed it up; others land while it is moving towards you and slow it down. The pushes cancel each other out and the swing never gets going. Push far too slowly and the seat just follows your hand.

Driving a thing at its own natural frequency, so that small pushes add up into a large motion, is ==resonance==. That is the whole idea. Everything else in this story is the same idea in different clothes.

:::work
**How often should the parent push?** Suppose the swing's chains are 2.5 m long (a guess for a park swing).
Time for one swing: T = 2π√(L ÷ g) = 2π × √(2.5 ÷ 9.8) = 3.2 s.
So: one push every 3.2 s, each time the seat returns. Pushes every 1.6 s, or every 6.4 s, land at the wrong moment half the time and fight each other.
:::

#### Step 3: a wine glass and a singer

Wet a finger and rub it slowly round the rim of a wine glass, and the glass sings a clear note. Your finger slips and catches, slips and catches, hundreds of times a second, and each catch is a tiny push. The glass answers at its own natural frequency; that one note is the sound of the glass resonating.

This is also how a singer can break a glass, and the trick is not loudness alone. The singer has to find the glass's own note and hold it, so that each ripple of sound arriving at the glass pushes it the way it is already moving. A loud wrong note leaves the glass unharmed; the right note, held steadily, can shake it until it shatters. (Not one to try at home: it takes a loudspeaker.)

How large the motion grows depends on friction. A swing with very little friction keeps adding up pushes for a long time before the losses catch up, so its peak is tall and narrow, and you must hit the rhythm almost exactly. More friction drains the energy faster, so the peak is lower and broader, and the rhythm matters less.

#### Step 4: two bridges

Soldiers crossing a bridge are ordered to break step. Hundreds of boots landing together at a steady rhythm are hundreds of small pushes in time, and if that rhythm happens to match the bridge's own, the pushes add up.

Something like that happened in London on 10 June 2000, when the Millennium Bridge, a new footbridge over the Thames, opened to crowds. The deck began to sway from side to side. People adjusted their steps to keep their balance, fell into step with the sway without meaning to, and their footfalls pushed it further. The bridge closed two days later and reopened in 2002 with dampers fitted: devices that drain away the energy of the sway faster than walkers can add it. More friction, lower peak.

The most famous bridge collapse of all is usually told as resonance, and it was not. In 1940 the Tacoma Narrows suspension bridge, in the state of Washington, twisted itself to pieces in a steady wind, and the film is shown in physics classes everywhere with the explanation that gusts of wind happened to arrive in time with the bridge's natural rhythm. The wind that day was not gusting. The deck twisted a little; the twist changed the way the steady wind pushed on it; the changed push twisted it further. Each twist fed the next, with no rhythm from outside at all. Engineers call that flutter, and it is the same thing that makes a flag flap in a steady breeze. Resonance needs a push that keeps time. Flutter makes its own.

:::careful
> "Resonance turns a small push into a huge force. It must be making energy from nothing."

It makes nothing. Each push adds a little energy of motion, and resonance only arranges the pushes so that they add up instead of cancelling. The energy in the child's enormous swing is the sum of all the parent's small pushes, less what friction has taken away. Stop pushing and the swing dies down.
:::

:::home Find the rhythm
Tie a key or a nut to about half a metre of thread and hold the top end between finger and thumb. Move your hand gently from side to side, a centimetre or two, at different rhythms. Most rhythms do almost nothing: the key jiggles, or just follows your hand. One rhythm, about once every 1.4 s for a half-metre thread, builds a big steady swing out of the same tiny hand movements. Now lengthen the thread to a metre and find the rhythm again. It should be about once every 2 s. Longer thread, slower rhythm.
:::
