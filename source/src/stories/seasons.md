:::scene Harvard, 1987

In 1987 a film crew went to Harvard University on graduation day and asked the new graduates, and some of their professors, a question a child might ask: why is it hotter in summer than in winter? The film is called *A Private Universe*, and by the usual count twenty-one of the twenty-three people they asked got it wrong. Most gave the same wrong answer, the one most people give: in summer the Earth is closer to the Sun.

The film is famous because the people in it were not ignorant. They had just finished degrees at one of the hardest universities in the world. They had a wrong idea that felt obviously right, and nobody had ever made them check it. Let's check it.

#### Step 1: the distance idea cannot be right

Earth's path around the Sun is very nearly a circle, but not quite, so its distance from the Sun does change through the year. Here is the first problem for the distance idea: Earth is *nearest* the Sun in early January, at about 147.1 million km. That point is called ==perihelion==. It is *farthest*, at ==aphelion==, about 152.1 million km, in early July. January is Mumbai's coolest month and the depth of winter across the northern half of the world. If closeness made summer, January would be summer.

Here is the second problem. When it is summer in Mumbai it is winter in Australia, and they are the same distance from the Sun; it is one planet. Distance cannot give two halves of the world opposite seasons at the same moment.

:::work
How much does the distance matter at all?
Farthest ÷ nearest = 152.1 ÷ 147.1 = 1.034, so Earth is 3.4% farther away in July than in January.
Sunlight weakens with the square of the distance (the same pattern as in the gravity story), so the Sun's light is 1.034² = 1.07 times stronger in January: about 7% more, and it arrives in the coolest month. Whatever makes seasons, it is not this.
:::

#### Step 2: the tilt

The real cause is a tilt. Earth spins round an axis, the line through the North and South Poles, and that axis is not upright with respect to its orbit. It leans over by 23.4°, and, this is the important part, it leans the *same way in space* all year round, pointing at the same distant stars. Astronomers call it the ==axial tilt==.

So as Earth goes round the Sun there is a time of year when the northern half leans towards the Sun (June) and a time when it leans away (December). In between, at the two ==equinox== points in March and September, neither half leans towards the Sun, and day and night are each about twelve hours long everywhere on Earth. The June and December extremes are the ==solstices==.

:::fig s_tilt_year() | Earth's axis leans 23.4° and keeps pointing the same way in space all year. In June the northern half leans towards the Sun and Mumbai's noon Sun stands 86° high; in December it leans away and the noon Sun is 47° high. The orbit is drawn as a circle seen at a slant. The real orbit is nearly circular, and Earth is nearest the Sun in early January, not in June.

The tilt does two things to the sunlight, and both matter.

#### Step 3: the slanting torch

Shine a torch straight down at the floor and you get a small bright patch. Tilt the torch and the same light spreads over a longer, dimmer patch. Every square centimetre in the slanted patch gets less light, because the same light has been shared over more floor.

The Sun is the torch. In June the noon Sun stands very high over Mumbai, so its light falls almost straight down. In December it is much lower and its light arrives at a slant, spread over more ground, so each square metre of Mumbai is warmed less.

:::work
**Mumbai's noon Sun.** Mumbai is at latitude 19.1° N. At the June solstice the Sun stands overhead at latitude 23.4° N; at the December solstice at 23.4° S; at the equinoxes over the equator.
June: the Sun is 23.4 − 19.1 = 4.3° *north* of overhead, so it stands 90 − 4.3 = 85.7° high. Almost straight up.
Equinoxes: 90 − 19.1 = 70.9° high.
December: 90 − (19.1 + 23.4) = 47.5° high.
The light landing on each square metre of flat ground goes with the sine of the Sun's height: sin 85.7° ÷ sin 47.5° = 1.35. Noon light is about 35% stronger in June than in December, before the clouds have their say.
:::

:::fig s_slant_light() | The same width of sunlight landing on Mumbai at noon in June (86° high) and in December (47° high). In December it is spread over 1.35 times as much ground, so each square metre gets about a quarter less.

#### Step 4: longer days

The second effect is time. When your half of Earth leans towards the Sun it spends more of each turn in the sunlit half, so the days are longer and the Sun has more hours to warm the ground. In Mumbai the longest day, in June, is about 13.2 hours from sunrise to sunset, and the shortest, in December, about 10.9 hours: a swing of about 2.3 hours. Far from the equator the swing is enormous, and near the poles the Sun does not set at all in midsummer. Mumbai's swing is mild, which is one reason Mumbai's year feels so different from Europe's.

#### Step 5: Mumbai's year

Mumbai lies inside the tropics, the band of the Earth where the Sun can pass directly overhead. Twice a year, once in May on its way north and once in July on its way back, the noon Sun stands exactly overhead and a straight pole casts no shadow at all. Between those two dates the noon Sun is in the *northern* half of the sky, which surprises people who have learned that the Sun is "always in the south".

Near the equator, astronomers say, the seasons are the wet one and the dry one; sunlight hardly changes. Mumbai is not on the equator, but its year is marked more by the monsoon, which normally arrives around 11 June, than by any swing in temperature. One more subtlety: the warmest weeks of a year come some time after the solstice, not on it, because land, sea and air take weeks to heat up.

:::careful
> "It's summer because the Earth is closer to the Sun."

Earth is closest in January. Australia has summer in December at the same distance as Mumbai's winter. The distance changes the Sun's light by about 7%, in the wrong direction. The tilt changes the height of the noon Sun over Mumbai from 47° to 86°, and the day from 10.9 to 13.2 hours. That is the cause.
:::

:::careful
> "All right, it's the tilt: the half that leans towards the Sun is a bit closer to it, so it's hotter."

The lean brings the northern half nearer the Sun by at most a few thousand kilometres, out of 150 million. That is a change of a few thousandths of one per cent: nothing. The tilt matters because it changes the *angle* the light arrives at and the *hours* it shines for, not the distance.
:::

:::school
The IGCSE syllabus says that Earth orbits the Sun once in about 365 days, and uses the orbit to explain why the seasons repeat each year. That is right about the repeating. The reason seasons happen at all is the tilt.
:::

:::home Count the squares
You need a phone torch and a sheet of squared paper in a dim room. Hold the torch 20 cm above the paper, pointing straight down, and count roughly how many squares the bright patch covers. Now keep the torch at the same height but tilt it to about 45°, so the light falls on the paper at a slant. Count again. The same light is spread over many more squares, and each square is dimmer. That is what December's noon Sun does to Mumbai.
:::
