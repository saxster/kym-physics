:::scene Cairo, about 1011

The story goes that Ibn al-Haytham came to Cairo with a promise he could not keep, a scheme to tame the floods of the Nile for the Caliph al-Hakim, and that when it failed he pretended to be mad to escape the Caliph's anger, and was shut up in his house for years with nothing to do but think. Historians doubt that story. What nobody doubts is the book he wrote in Cairo in the years around 1011 to 1021, the ==Book of Optics==, and the question at the heart of it: how do we see?

The old answer came from Euclid and, later, Ptolemy, two of the greatest names in Greek science. The eye, they said, does the seeing by sending something out: rays that leave the eye, travel to the object and feel it, rather as a stick feels the ground. The idea is called ==extramission==, "sending out", and before you smile at it, know that most people still believe it. When students were asked to draw how a person sees a balloon, 86 in every 100 drew arrows going from the eye to the balloon. Among adults asked directly, 69 in 100 said that something leaves the eye. Even for a lit bulb, a third of adults said so. It feels right. You look *at* things, and looking feels like an action, something you do to the world.

Ibn al-Haytham argued the other way: nothing leaves the eye, and light comes in. He gave reasons. Look at the Sun or a very bright lamp and your eyes hurt; if the eye were the one sending out rays, why should a brighter object hurt it more? And every point of a lit object, he said, sends out light of its own in every direction, so light from every point of it can reach your eye. Then he did what the Greeks had not done. He tested his ideas with experiments, including one in a dark room.

#### Step 1: the dark room, first version

Try his question yourself tonight. Go into a windowless bathroom, shut the door, switch off the light, and wait a full minute for your eyes to adjust. Now hold your hand in front of your face. Can you see it?

If your eyes sent out rays, there is no reason you should not. Your hand is right there, and your eyes are working perfectly. But you see nothing at all. The waiting matters, because it rules out the excuse "my eyes just needed time". With no light in the room, time does not help. Your eyes are collectors, not torches, and there is nothing to collect.

#### Step 2: the chain

So here is the chain that seeing needs. A source makes light: the Sun, a lamp, a screen. The light travels to your hand. Your hand is not a mirror; its rough surface sends the light off in every direction, which is why everyone in the room can see your hand from wherever they happen to be standing. A tiny share of that scattered light enters your eye. Three links, and the light moves towards the eye at every step: from the source, off the hand, into the eye. Break any link and you see nothing. In the dark bathroom the first link is missing.

Your eye can work with astonishingly little. It copes with brightness from the faintest light it can detect up to ten thousand million times brighter. That is why "a bit of light from somewhere" is nearly always enough, and why a truly dark room is such a rare experience that the bathroom test surprises people.

#### Step 3: the dark room, second version

Ibn al-Haytham's second use of a dark room is still famous, and still works. Make a room completely dark except for one small hole in the shutter, and look at the wall opposite the hole. The sunlit scene outside appears on it: dim, in colour, moving, and upside down. People in the street walk along the wall with their feet in the air. The Latin name for such a room, ==camera obscura==, means "dark chamber", and it is where the word camera comes from.

Why upside down? Because light travels in straight lines. Light from the top of a tree outside passes straight through the hole and keeps going straight, so it lands low on the wall. Light from the bottom of the tree lands high. Every point outside sends light through the hole to exactly one point on the wall, and the picture is the scene turned over. The hole must be small. A big hole lets light from one point outside land in many places on the wall, and the picture blurs into a glow.

This is a strong argument against rays from the eye, because the picture forms whether anyone is looking or not. A wall does not look at anything. Light is arriving there, through the hole, in straight lines, and making the picture by itself. A shoebox with a pinhole in one end, a ==pinhole camera==, does exactly the same, and you can build one this afternoon.

:::work
**How big is the picture?** Suppose a doorway 2 m tall stands 5 m from the pinhole of a shoebox camera 30 cm deep. Light from the top and the bottom of the doorway crosses at the hole and spreads out again inside the box. The two triangles, outside and inside, have the same shape, so
image height ÷ 0.30 m = 2 m ÷ 5 m, and the image is 2 × 0.30 ÷ 5 = 0.12 m: a 12 cm doorway, upside down.
:::

> "My eyes need light to work, the way a torch needs batteries."

That puts light inside the eye's job. Light is not fuel for the eye; it is the thing the eye collects. Light has to travel *from* an object *into* your eye, and if nothing is sending light your way, the best eyes in the world see nothing. Every arrow in a drawing of seeing should end at the eye.
:::

:::home A camera obscura from a shoebox
Cut a window about the size of a playing card in one end of a shoebox and tape tracing paper, or thin white paper, over it as a screen. Cover the other end with kitchen foil and prick one small hole in the middle of the foil with a pin. Put the lid on. In a dim room, point the pinhole end at a bright window and look at the paper screen: the window appears on it, upside down, with the room's ceiling at the bottom. Try pricking a second, bigger hole with a pencil point: brighter, and blurred. Never point the camera at the Sun, and never look at the Sun through the hole.
For the chain's first link, fill a plant sprayer with water, darken the room, and shine a torch across it while you spray a fine mist. The beam appears only where droplets are sending light into your eye, and vanishes where the air is clear.
:::
