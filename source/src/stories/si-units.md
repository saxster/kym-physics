:::scene Sèvres, near Paris, 20 May 2019

On 20 May 2019 the last of the world's measuring units that still depended on a physical object, the kilogram, was redefined in terms of a constant of nature, and every one of the seven base units of the international system came to rest on something that cannot drift or tarnish. The tale of the metal cylinder it replaced is told elsewhere in this book. This is about the system itself, and about why three of its units turn up on a bill that arrives at your flat every month.

#### Step 1: why units at all

"The pitch is 20" means nothing. "The pitch is 20 metres" means something, and "20 yards" means something slightly different. A measurement is always a number *times a unit*, and the unit is what lets two people in two cities agree. You cannot add quantities with different units: 10 km plus 5 kg is nonsense, and even 10 km plus 20 m has to be made into one unit before it can be added.

The system almost every country uses is the ==SI==, from its French name, Système international. It rests on seven base units: the metre, the kilogram, the second, the ampere, the kelvin, the mole and the candela. For everything in the first half of this book you need only the first three.

#### Step 2: building new units from old ones

Every other unit in mechanics is a combination of the metre, the kilogram and the second. Speed is metres per second. Acceleration is metres per second each second, m/s². Some combinations come up so often that they are given names, and the names honour the people who did the work.

The ==newton== is the force that gives one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared. So a one-kilogram bag of atta, which falls at 9.8 m/s² when you drop it, weighs about 9.8 newtons.

The ==joule== is one newton pushed through one metre. Lifting that bag of atta by about 10 cm takes about one joule. It is the unit of energy, of every kind: of movement, of height, of heat, of food.

The ==watt== is one joule every second: the unit of how *fast* energy moves. A 60 W bulb moves 60 J every second from the mains into light and warmth, and only about 5 of those watts come out as light; the rest warms the room.

:::fig s_unit_chain() | The chain from force to the bill. A newton gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²; a joule is a newton pushed through a metre; a watt is a joule every second; a kilowatt-hour is a thousand watts for an hour, which is 3.6 million joules. Each one is built from the one before, and all of them from the metre, the kilogram and the second.

#### Step 3: converting

Prefixes are powers of ten: kilo means a thousand, so a kilowatt is 1,000 W. And multiplying a power by a time gives an energy, because joules per second, times seconds, is joules.

:::work
**The unit on the bill**
1 kilowatt for 1 hour = 1,000 J/s × 3,600 s = 3,600,000 J = 3.6 MJ.
That is one ==kilowatt-hour==, and when your electricity bill says "units consumed", one unit means one kilowatt-hour.
A 10 W LED bulb burning for 100 hours: 10 × 100 = 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh, one unit.
A 500 W mixer-grinder running for 2 hours: 500 × 2 = 1,000 Wh = 1 kWh, the same unit. (Read the labels on yours; those wattages are typical, not certain.)
:::

#### Step 4: comparing

Once everything is in joules, odd things can be compared. One kilowatt-hour is about 860 kilocalories, the "calories" printed on food packets. A person eating 2,000 kcal a day is running on about 2.3 kWh a day, roughly the energy that lights a flat for an evening. A 30 kcal biscuit holds about 35 Wh, enough to run a 10 W LED for three and a half hours. And a labourer working steadily at about 100 W needs ten hours to deliver one kilowatt-hour. One unit on the bill is a full working day of human effort.

:::careful
> "A kilowatt-hour is a unit of power. Or is it watts per hour?"

Neither. A watt is already "per second": it is a joule every second, a rate. Multiply a rate by a time and you get back to an amount, so a kilowatt-hour is an *energy*, 3.6 million joules. "Watts per hour" is not a unit of anything useful. Bills count energy; the appliance label gives power; the hours you run it join the two.
:::

#### Step 5: units as a check

Carry the units through every calculation. Area times depth gives m² × m = m³, a volume; a volume times kg/m³ gives kg, a mass. If you set out to find an energy and the units come out as kg or m/s, you have made a mistake somewhere, and the units found it before you did.

:::home Read the labels
Find the wattage printed on the mixer-grinder, the fridge, an LED bulb and a phone charger. Then find the "units consumed" on last month's electricity bill. Work out how many hours of each appliance one unit buys: 1,000 Wh divided by the wattage. Finally, read the kilocalories on a biscuit packet and convert one biscuit to watt-hours (multiply by 4,184 J, divide by 3,600). How many biscuits is one unit on the bill?
:::
