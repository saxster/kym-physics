:::scene Paris, September 1676

Jupiter has moons, and one of them, Io, slips into Jupiter's shadow on a schedule as regular as a clock. At the new observatory in Paris, Ole Rømer had been timing these eclipses for months, and the schedule would not hold. While the Earth, on its own orbit round the Sun, was moving away from Jupiter, each eclipse came a little later than the last one had predicted. While the Earth was moving back towards Jupiter, they came early.

Nothing was wrong with Io. Rømer saw what the drift meant. The light that showed him each eclipse had to travel from Jupiter to his telescope, and when the Earth was farther away it had farther to travel, so it arrived later. Light was not instant. It took time, and the drifting schedule was that time showing itself. In September 1676 he announced that the eclipse due that November would come about ten minutes later than the tables said. It did.

#### Step 1: a time, not a speed

People today say Rømer measured the speed of light. He did not, and the honest version is better. He never stated a speed. In a letter the following year he put his result as a time: about 22 minutes, he reckoned, for light to cross the full width of the Earth's orbit, from one side of the Sun to the other. His friend Christiaan Huygens did the arithmetic that turned the time into a speed, and his answer came out well short of the truth, because Rømer's 22 minutes was too long. The real figure is about 16.6 minutes. But it was the first number anyone had, and it was enormous. Galileo, a generation earlier, had concluded only that light was extremely fast, perhaps instantaneous. Now it had a size.

#### Step 2: how fast is that?

Today the ==speed of light== in empty space is known exactly: 299,792,458 metres every second. Exactly, because since 1983 nobody measures it any more; the metre itself is defined from it, as the distance light covers in a certain tiny fraction of a second. In round numbers, light goes nearly 300,000 kilometres in one second, which would take it round the Earth seven and a half times.

At that speed, distances turn into times. Light from the Moon takes 1.3 seconds to reach you. Light from the Sun takes 8 minutes and 19 seconds, so the sunshine on your arm left the Sun before you started reading this page. Light crossing the whole width of the Earth's orbit, Rømer's distance, takes 16.6 minutes, and that is the delay he saw in Io's timetable.

:::work
**How old is sunlight?** The Sun is 149,597,870,700 m away (an exact value, fixed by astronomers).
Time = distance ÷ speed = 149,597,870,700 ÷ 299,792,458 = 499 s.
499 s = 8 min 19 s. Across the Earth's orbit, twice that: 998 s, or 16.6 min.
:::

#### Step 3: a cogwheel across Paris

For a hundred and seventy years the speed of light was known only from the sky. Measuring it on the ground meant timing something that crossed a city in a hundred-thousandth of a second, and no clock could do that. In 1849 Hippolyte Fizeau found a way to make a wheel do it instead.

:::scene Paris, 1849

He set a lamp behind a spinning cogwheel with 720 teeth on a hill at Suresnes, on the edge of Paris, and a mirror on Montmartre, about 8.6 km away. Light flashed out through a gap between two teeth, crossed to the mirror, and came back to the wheel. If the wheel turned slowly, the returning light came back through the same gap and he saw the mirror glow. Spin the wheel faster, and there comes a speed at which, in the time the light took to go there and back, the wheel has turned by exactly half a tooth, so the returning flash meets a tooth and is blocked. The mirror goes dark. The wheel's spin was his clock. The round trip is about 17 km, and the mirror first goes dark at about twelve turns a second, which makes each flash's journey about sixty millionths of a second. From that Fizeau got about 315,000 km/s, around 5% too high, which for a cogwheel and a lamp is remarkable.

After him, Léon Foucault did it with a spinning mirror in 1862 and came within 0.6%, and Albert Michelson kept refining the method until 1926. Then the measuring stopped, for the reason in Step 2: in 1983 the value was fixed by definition, and the metre was redefined to fit it.

:::careful
> "Lightning comes before thunder, so that proves light is incredibly fast."

It proves only that light is faster than sound. The flash and the bang leave the lightning together; the bang arrives seconds later because sound crawls along at about a third of a kilometre a second. The flash also takes time, about three millionths of a second for each kilometre, and that is far too short for you to notice. "Too fast to notice" is not the same as "instant". Rømer's whole discovery was the difference between the two.
:::

:::home Count the storm
You can do this on paper with three flash-to-thunder delays: 3 seconds, 6 seconds and 15 seconds. Work out the distance for each. If you happen to notice a flash while already safely indoors, you can count until the thunder, but stay away from windows, doors and balconies; do not go looking for a storm to measure. Sound travels about 349 metres a second in warm air, so every three seconds of counting is roughly one kilometre between you and the lightning. Fifteen seconds is about five kilometres. If the count shrinks from one flash to the next, the storm is coming nearer. The light's own delay over five kilometres is seventeen millionths of a second, which is why you can treat the flash as the moment it happened.
:::
