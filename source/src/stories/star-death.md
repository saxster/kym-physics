:::scene Bombay, 1930

In 1930 Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, nineteen years old, boarded the SS *Pilsna* at Bombay to sail to England. He had been born in Lahore in 1910, had finished college at nineteen, had a scholarship to Cambridge, and had an uncle, C. V. Raman, who was about to win the Nobel Prize. The voyage was long. He spent it on a calculation.

The question was what happens to a star like the Sun when its fuel runs out. Astronomers already knew the answer in outline: it shrinks into a white dwarf, a star's worth of mass squeezed into something far smaller, held up by a new kind of pressure that comes from electrons refusing to be crowded. Chandrasekhar added one ingredient the existing theory had left out. In a star squeezed that hard, the electrons move at speeds close to that of light, and Einstein's relativity changes how they push back. When he put that in, the theory did something unexpected. Above a certain mass, the pressure could not win. A star heavier than the limit could not become a white dwarf at all. His first figure for the limit was 0.91 times the Sun's mass; with better values for the star's make-up it became about 1.4, which is the number used today. He kept refining the work through his years at Cambridge.

:::scene London, January 1935

In January 1935 he was invited to present the result at a meeting of the Royal Astronomical Society in London. Arthur Eddington, famous across the world for the 1919 eclipse expedition that had confirmed Einstein, spoke immediately after him, with a paper attacking the whole idea. "I think there should be a law of Nature to prevent a star from behaving in this absurd way!" he said. Chandrasekhar was not allowed to reply from the floor. He wrote instead to the physicists who understood relativity and quantum theory, found that they agreed with him, and published his rebuttal. Eddington never accepted it. Chandrasekhar later said that his old hero, for all his insight, "has always operated with preconceived ideas". The Nobel Prize in Physics came to Chandrasekhar in 1983, fifty-three years after the voyage.

#### Step 1: what holds a star up

A star is a ball of gas pulled inwards by its own gravity. What stops it collapsing is the heat from fusion in its core, which keeps the gas pushing outwards. For as long as the fuel lasts, the two balance. When the core runs out of hydrogen, and later of whatever it fused next, the push weakens and gravity begins to win. The core contracts.

#### Step 2: electrons that refuse to be crowded

Squeeze matter hard enough and a rule of quantum physics takes over: no two electrons can be in the same place at the same time doing the same thing. Pack them closer and they are forced into faster and faster motion, and that motion is a pressure, called degeneracy pressure. It has nothing to do with electrons repelling each other because they are both negative; it is a different and far stronger effect. A white dwarf is a dead star held up by nothing but this refusal: the mass of a star, no longer making any heat, kept from collapsing by crowded electrons.

#### Step 3: the strange rule and the limit

White dwarfs break a rule that everything else in daily life obeys. Add mass to one and it gets *smaller*. Gravity squeezes harder, the electrons are packed tighter, and the star shrinks. Follow the models upwards and the shrinking runs away: at about 1.4 times the Sun's mass the calculated radius drops to zero. That is the mathematics' way of saying that no white dwarf can exist above that mass; even the force of degenerate electrons cannot stop the collapse of a star with more mass than this. The mass at which a star can still end its life as a white dwarf, about 1.4 Suns, is the Chandrasekhar limit.

:::fig s_white_dwarf_mass_radius() | More mass, smaller star: how a white dwarf's radius falls as its mass rises, in the shape the models give (the curve is schematic, not measured). At about 1.4 times the Sun's mass the radius reaches zero, which means no white dwarf can exist beyond that point.

The Sun, at exactly one solar mass, is safely below the limit, and will end as a white dwarf. So will many stars born much heavier, because stars shed a great deal of mass in their old age before the core settles.

#### Step 4: what happens above the limit

When the core of a very massive star passes the limit, degenerate electrons can no longer hold it, and it shrinks rapidly. The collapse does not stop until the core is as dense as an atomic nucleus, at which point the electrons have been crushed into the protons to make neutrons, and the neutrons, obeying the same quantum rule, push back far harder than the electrons could. What remains is a neutron star: about a Sun's mass of matter in a ball less than twenty kilometres across. The outer layers of the star, at least five Suns' worth, are blasted into space in the explosion we call a supernova.

Neutrons have a limit too, perhaps about three times the Sun's mass; nobody knows the number exactly. A core heavier than that cannot be stopped by anything, and keeps collapsing into the state of ultimate compaction: a black hole. Every one of these endings follows from the calculation a teenager began on a ship from Bombay.

:::careful
> "At 1.4 Suns a white dwarf really shrinks to a point."

The zero in the model is a signpost, not a description. It says the white dwarf solution stops existing there; what actually happens is that the star collapses into something else, a neutron star or a black hole.
:::

:::careful
> "The electrons hold the star up because negative charges repel."

Electrical repulsion is there, but it is not what does the work. Degeneracy pressure comes from the quantum rule that electrons cannot share the same state, and it is much stronger than their electrical push.
:::

:::careful
> "He worked it all out on the ship."

He began it there, and it is a fine beginning. The limit as we know it took years of further work at Cambridge, a correction to his own first number, and a long fight to be heard.
:::
