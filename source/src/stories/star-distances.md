:::scene Harvard, 1912

In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt was one of the women employed at Harvard College Observatory as "computers": people who measured and catalogued the stars on photographic plates, at a third or a quarter of what the observatory paid men. Her plates showed the Small Magellanic Cloud, a smudge of stars visible from the southern hemisphere, photographed night after night. Some of the stars in it were variable: they brightened and faded, over and over, each on its own steady rhythm of days or weeks. Stars of this kind are called Cepheids, after the first one found, in the constellation Cepheus.

Leavitt measured twenty-five of them, and saw a pattern. The brighter a Cepheid appeared, the longer it took to go through one cycle of brightening and fading. The slow blinkers were always the bright ones.

The key to why that mattered is where the stars were. All of them sat in the same small cloud, which meant all of them were at about the same distance from Earth. For stars at the same distance, looking brighter means *being* brighter. So Leavitt had found a rule about the stars themselves: a Cepheid's rhythm tells you its true brightness. She could not turn that into distances herself, because in 1912 nobody knew how far away the Cloud was. Others calibrated her rule later, using Cepheids closer to home. A reanalysis published in 2025 found that her original relation holds up.

#### Step 1: the jump of a thumb

Hold your thumb up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the wall behind it. Bring it closer and the jump grows; hold it farther away and the jump shrinks. That jump is parallax, and it is the oldest way of measuring a distance you cannot walk. Surveyors do it with a baseline and two angles; your eyes do it with the few centimetres between them. For a thumb at arm's length the jump is about six degrees.

#### Step 2: a baseline the size of the Earth's orbit

For stars, the two "eyes" are the Earth in January and the Earth in July, on opposite sides of its orbit, 300 million kilometres apart. Photograph a nearby star against the distant ones six months apart and it has shifted. Astronomers call half that total shift the star's parallax, so that it corresponds to a baseline of one Earth–Sun distance, and that is the number to remember when you read a table.

:::fig s_parallax() | Parallax. Seen from the Earth in January and again in July, a nearby star appears to shift against the far stars behind it. The angle p, half the total shift, is the star's parallax; the nearer the star, the bigger p. The drawing exaggerates the angle enormously.

The shifts are tiny. The unit astronomers invented for them is the parsec: the distance at which the parallax is one arcsecond, one 3,600th of a degree. A star one parsec away is 206,265 times as far as the Sun, which is 3.26 light-years.

:::work
**How small is one arcsecond?**
A ₹1 coin is 22 mm across. Seen from 4.5 km away it would look one arcsecond wide:
0.022 m × 206,265 ≈ 4,540 m.
That is a coin held up at the far end of the Bandra–Worli Sea Link, seen from the near end.
The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, 4.25 light-years away, has a parallax of 0.77 arcsecond: smaller than that coin.
:::

Friedrich Bessel made the first trustworthy measurement of a star's parallax in 1838, for the star 61 Cygni. The space telescope Gaia now measures the parallaxes of nearly a billion stars to a few millionths of an arcsecond. Even so, parallax runs out: for stars in other galaxies the shifts are far too small to measure, and that is most of the universe.

#### Step 3: a light-year is a distance

The light-year sounds like a time and is not. It is how far light travels in a year: about 9.5 million million kilometres. The Sun's light takes 8.3 minutes to reach Mumbai, so the Sun is 8.3 light-minutes away. Proxima's light takes 4.25 years, so when you look at it you see it as it was 4.25 years ago. A light-year is 9.5 × 10¹² km, and one parsec is 3.26 of them.

#### Step 4: the measuring tape

Here is where Leavitt's rule becomes a tape. Find a Cepheid anywhere, time its blinking, and her rule tells you how bright it truly is. Measure how bright it *looks*, and the gap between true and apparent brightness tells you how far away it must be, because light spreads out and dims with distance in a known way. The farther the star, the dimmer it appears for its rhythm. A Cepheid in another galaxy is a lamp of known wattage seen across the void.

Within twenty years Edwin Hubble had used Cepheids to measure the distances to other galaxies, and found something in those distances that nobody expected: the story *The stretching universe* is what he found.

:::careful
> "A light-year is a length of time."

It is a distance: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 million million kilometres. When astronomers say a galaxy is a million light-years away, they mean its light is a million years old when it arrives, and that is a consequence, not the definition.
:::

:::careful
> "Leavitt measured the distances to the stars."

She found the rule, that slower-blinking Cepheids are truly brighter, and she could prove it because her stars were all at one distance. Turning the rule into distances needed a calibration she did not have. Others supplied it; the tape was hers.
:::

:::home Parallax on a rooftop
Ask an adult to come up to the terrace with you. Stand at one end and line up a nearby water tank with something far away, a tower or a tree on the horizon. Walk to the other end of the terrace and look again: the tank has jumped against the far background. Pace the length of the terrace, and the farther the tank, the smaller the jump. That is Bessel's measurement, with your terrace as the Earth's orbit.
:::
