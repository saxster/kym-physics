:::scene Calcutta, 1919

In 1919 Meghnad Saha was a young lecturer at the University College of Science in Calcutta, a few years out of Presidency College, and he had a puzzle in front of him that astronomers had been staring at for sixty years. Split a star's light with a prism and you get a rainbow crossed by dark lines, a stellar spectrum. The lines are the fingerprints of elements. But different stars show different fingerprints: some are crowded with hydrogen lines, some have almost none and show lines of other elements instead. The obvious reading was that different stars are made of different stuff.

Saha thought the difference was temperature, and he worked out exactly how. In a very hot gas, collisions knock electrons clean off their atoms, a process called ionisation, and an ionised atom absorbs a completely different set of lines from a whole one, or none at all in visible light. Saha wrote an equation, now the Saha equation, that gives the fraction of atoms ionised at any temperature and pressure. With it, a star's spectrum stopped being a list of ingredients and became a thermometer. He left for Europe in September 1919 to finish the work; in London Alfred Fowler at Imperial College helped him revise the papers, and the first of them, "On Ionization in the Solar Chromosphere", appeared in the *Philosophical Magazine* in 1920.

#### Step 1: the fingerprint

Light from a hot, dense body is a smooth rainbow. Pass it through a cooler gas and the gas's atoms swallow light at a few exact colours, leaving dark lines; heat a thin gas on its own and it glows at exactly those same colours, as bright lines. In 1815 Joseph von Fraunhofer counted about 600 dark lines in sunlight. In 1860 Gustav Kirchhoff matched one set of them to sodium, the first element identified in the Sun. Helium was found in the Sun's spectrum before anyone found it on Earth.

#### Step 2: the wrong idea, taken seriously

So a star's lines tell you what it is made of. Half true, and the false half misled astronomers for decades. Hydrogen is the most abundant element in nearly every star, yet its lines are faint or missing in the very hottest stars and in the very coolest. Saha's equation says why. In the hottest stars, hydrogen is almost entirely ionised, its single electron stripped away, and an ionised hydrogen atom, a bare proton, has no lines to show. In the coolest stars the electrons sit on the lowest rung of hydrogen's energy ladder, and from there the jumps that make visible-light lines cannot happen. Only in stars of middling temperature, where plenty of atoms are whole and plenty of electrons sit one rung up, do the famous hydrogen lines show strongly.

:::fig s_hydrogen_lines_temperature() | How strongly hydrogen's visible lines show, against a star's surface temperature. The curve is schematic. In the coolest stars the electrons are on the bottom rung; in the hottest the atoms are ionised; the lines are strong only in between, though hydrogen is abundant everywhere.

The same logic runs for every element. Which lines show depends on which atoms are whole, which are ionised, and where their electrons sit, and all of that is set by temperature. The letters astronomers use to sort stars, O B A F G K M, worked out at Harvard by Annie Jump Cannon, are a sequence of falling temperature, not a list of recipes.

#### Step 3: Cecilia Payne reads the recipe

:::scene Harvard, 1925

In 1925 Cecilia Payne, a young Englishwoman finishing a doctorate at Harvard, did what Saha's equation made possible. If temperature explains which lines appear, you can correct for temperature and work backwards to how much of each element is really there. She did it for star after star. The answer was that stars are overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium, with everything else, the oxygen, silicon and iron that make up the Earth's crust, present only in traces.

Everyone expected the Sun to be made of much the same stuff as the Earth. Henry Norris Russell, one of the most influential astronomers in America, told her the result was "clearly impossible". Her thesis was printed with the hydrogen and helium figures labelled "spurious". Within a few years the evidence had piled up, Russell himself reached the same conclusion in 1929, and the result became the foundation of how stars are understood. The astronomer Otto Struve later called it "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy".

#### Step 4: the recipe

By mass, the Sun is about 73 per cent hydrogen and 25 per cent helium. Everything else, every other element in the periodic table together, makes up the remaining 2 per cent. The Earth's crust, by contrast, is mostly oxygen, silicon and aluminium. The Sun is not a bigger Earth that happens to be hot; it is made of different stuff, and so are nearly all the stars.

It is not fire, either. At those temperatures the gas is ionised, a mixture of free electrons and bare nuclei, which physicists call a plasma. A star is a ball of plasma held together by its own gravity and lit from inside, as the story *A fire that has lasted four and a half billion years* explains, by fusion in its core.

:::careful
> "Stars with different spectra are made of different things."

Mostly they are made of the same things, in nearly the same proportions. Their lines differ because their temperatures differ, which changes which atoms are ionised and where their electrons sit. That is Saha's point, and it is why Payne could read the recipe at all.
:::

:::careful
> "Payne was ignored and proved right only decades later."

She was doubted, and told to call her result spurious, which was wrong of her elders. But the confirmation came within a few years, not decades, and Russell published the same conclusion in 1929.
:::

:::home A spectroscope from a CD
Hold an old CD or DVD at a slant so that it reflects a street lamp into your eye, and tilt it until the light spreads into a rainbow. A yellow sodium street lamp gives a few sharp coloured bands; a white LED gives a smooth rainbow. The sharp bands are the lamp's fingerprint, the same idea Fraunhofer and Kirchhoff used on the Sun. Never try this with the Sun itself, directly or in a reflection.
