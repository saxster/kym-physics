:::scene London, 1600

In 1600 a book came out in London with a long Latin title that everyone shortens to *De Magnete*, "On the Magnet". Its author, William Gilbert, had spent years testing the stories people told about magnets, and about amber. Amber was the puzzle. Rub a piece of it on cloth and it picks up chaff and scraps, as people had known since ancient Greece. Was that the same pull as a magnet's?

Gilbert built a tool to find out: a light metal needle balanced on a point, which he called a *versorium*, so that it would swing towards anything that pulled on it. Then he rubbed every substance he could lay hands on. Some swung the needle as amber did; many did not. The Greek word for amber is *elektron*, so he called the substances that behaved like amber *electricus*, amber-like. Our words "electric" and "electricity" grow out of that one adjective.

He also noticed what the magnet did *not* do. A lodestone pulls on iron, rubbed or not, and ignores almost everything else. Rubbed amber pulls on nearly anything light: paper, leaves, even a trickle of water. Two different things were going on. Gilbert kept them apart, and so will this book.

#### Step 1: nothing is made, something is moved

Every atom holds two kinds of electric charge: protons, with a positive charge, in its nucleus, and electrons, with a negative charge, around it. A neutral object has equal amounts of each, which is why it pulls on nothing. Now press two different materials together. One of them holds its electrons more tightly than the other, and when the two part, it keeps a few extra. Rubbing is a way of pressing the surfaces together more closely, over and over, so that more electrons cross.

Count the charge before and after and the total is the same. The balloon has gained electrons and is negative; your hair has lost exactly that many, and the positive charge left behind is exactly as large. Charge is never created or destroyed; it is only transferred from one body to another. That is the whole of this story's title. Rubbing doesn't make charge. It separates charge that was already there.

:::fig s_rub_moves_charge() | Before rubbing, balloon and hair each hold equal numbers of positive and negative charges. After, four electrons have moved to the balloon: it is negative by four, the hair positive by four, and the total is unchanged. (Real numbers are enormously larger; the picture keeps the count small enough to check.)

In solids it is always the electrons that move. Nothing positive travels onto the hair; the positive charge is simply what remains when electrons leave.

#### Step 2: two kinds, and who named them

:::scene Philadelphia, 1752

Like charges push each other apart; unlike charges pull together. Which kind to call positive and which negative was a free choice, and Benjamin Franklin made it: glass rubbed with silk, he said, is positive. We still use his convention. Since it is electrons that leave the glass, the electron ended up carrying the minus sign.

Franklin is also the man in the story about the kite: a kite flown into a thunderstorm, a key on the string, a spark from the key, and so proof that lightning is the same stuff as the spark from rubbed glass. The story goes that he did it in June 1752. Perhaps he did. But no account of it survives in Franklin's own handwriting; the first full description was written by Joseph Priestley fifteen years later, and the only witness was Franklin's son. Historians still disagree about whether it happened at all. What is certain is the idea, which was right. Lightning is an enormous version of the carpet spark: in one bolt about 10²⁰ electrons can move in two thousandths of a second, a current of 8,000 amperes.

#### Step 3: where charge can go

In some materials, metals above all, some electrons are free to wander from atom to atom, so charge placed on one spot spreads through the whole object at once. These are conductors. In others, such as rubber, glass, plastic and dry air, the electrons stay with their atoms, and charge placed on one spot stays there. These are insulators. A balloon is an insulator, which is why the charge you rub onto one side stays on that side and can hold the balloon against a wall for an hour.

Clinging to a *neutral* wall needs one more idea. The wall has no net charge, but it is full of charges. Bring a negative balloon close and the electrons in the wall's surface are nudged slightly away while the positive nuclei stay put; the surface nearest the balloon is left slightly positive, and the balloon is pulled towards it. This shuffling inside a neutral object is called polarisation. It is why a rubbed comb bends a thin stream of tap water, and why a charged balloon lifts scraps of paper that carry no charge at all.

#### Step 4: how much charge is that?

Charge is measured in coulombs, and one coulomb is a great deal: about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Everyday static is a tiny imbalance on an otherwise neutral object. When an ink-jet printer puts −1 × 10⁻¹⁰ coulombs on a single drop of ink, that is about 600 million extra electrons, which sounds like a lot until you count the atoms in the drop: there is one extra electron for every ten million atoms.

:::careful
> "Rubbing makes electricity."

It moves it. The balloon and the cloth start neutral and end with equal and opposite charges, because electrons crossed from one to the other. Nothing new came into existence.
:::

:::careful
> "Static is a different kind of electricity from the kind in wires."

Same charge, same electrons. On the balloon the extra electrons sit still, because it is an insulator and they have nowhere to go. In a wire they drift. "Static" means sitting still, nothing more.
:::

#### Why the trick fails in July

Try the balloon on a dry January evening in Mumbai and it clings and crackles. Try it in the monsoon and almost nothing happens. The sources this book trusts say only this much for certain: in damp air the extra charge leaks away faster, so the effect is weaker. How moist air carries the charge off is not something we can tell you from a source we have checked, so we leave it there. You can measure the difference yourself: the same balloon, the same hair, July and then January.

:::home Two strips of tape
Take two pieces of sticky tape about 4 cm long. Stick both to a table, side by side, then peel them off quickly and hold them near each other. They push apart: both lost electrons to the table in the same way, so both carry the same kind of charge. Now stick one strip on top of the other, peel the pair off the table together, and then peel them apart. Hold them near each other again. This time they pull together: one strip took electrons from the other. Best on a dry day.
:::

In Chapter 12 the charge stops sitting still. The same electrons, drifting round a closed loop of wire, are what we call a current, and the first question there is what, if anything, gets used up.
