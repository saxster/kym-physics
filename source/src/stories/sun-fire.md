:::scene Kungälv, Sweden, Christmas 1938

Lise Meitner spent Christmas 1938 in Kungälv, a small town on the west coast of Sweden. That summer she had fled Berlin, where she had worked for thirty years, because the Nazi laws had reached her; she was sixty, with no laboratory and almost no money. Her nephew Otto Frisch, also a physicist and also in exile, came to visit, and she showed him a letter from her old colleague Otto Hahn.

Hahn's letter made no sense. He and Fritz Strassmann had been firing neutrons at uranium, the heaviest natural element, and had found barium in the results. Barium is barely more than half as heavy as uranium. Tapping a nucleus with a single neutron and getting something half its size was like tapping a boulder and watching it fall cleanly into two rocks.

They went out walking in the snow to think about it. The story goes that Frisch was on skis and Meitner on foot, and that they sat down on a tree trunk to work with pencil and paper; those details come from Frisch's memoir years later, so hold them loosely. What they worked out is not in doubt. Picture the uranium nucleus as a wobbling drop of liquid. An extra neutron sets it wobbling harder, it stretches into a waist, and the electrical repulsion between its two positively charged ends tears it in two. Barium could be one of the pieces. Then Meitner did the sum that mattered. The two pieces together should weigh slightly less than the uranium nucleus had. By Einstein's rule, E = mc², that lost mass had to appear as energy, and it came to about 200 million electron-volts for every nucleus that split, roughly forty million times what a single molecule gives up in burning. They published it in *Nature* in February 1939. Frisch borrowed a word from biology and called the process fission: the splitting of a nucleus.

It is the nucleus that splits. Not an atom's bonds, not the atom itself: the tiny core, a hundred-thousandth of the atom's width.

#### Step 1: why the Sun cannot be burning

The Sun pours out about 4 × 10²⁶ joules every second, and the Earth's rocks say it has done so for a very long time: there was liquid water here nearly four billion years ago, and the Sun is about four and a half billion years old. No fire can do that. A Sun made of coal, burning at its present rate, would be used up in a few thousand years, and nothing like solid coal could even survive at the Sun's temperature. The nineteenth century's best alternative, that the Sun shines by slowly shrinking under its own gravity, lasts longer, but only about a hundred million years. The rocks said four billion. Something else had to be going on, and nobody knew what until mass turned out to be a form of energy.

#### Step 2: mass is energy

Einstein's 1905 equation says that a mass m is worth an energy mc², where c is the speed of light. Because c² is so enormous, nine times ten to the sixteen, a very little mass is worth a very great deal of energy. In the Sun's core, four hydrogen nuclei combine, in steps, into one helium nucleus. That joining is nuclear fusion, and the helium weighs 0.71 per cent less than the four hydrogens did. The difference leaves as energy.

:::work
**How much energy does one kilogram of hydrogen give when it fuses into helium?**
0.0071 kg of it turns into energy.
E = 0.0071 × (3.0 × 10⁸)² ≈ 6.4 × 10¹⁴ J.
That is enough to run a 10-watt LED bulb for about two million years.
:::

#### Step 3: why it needs the centre of a star

Hydrogen nuclei are protons, and protons repel each other. To fuse, two of them must get close enough for the nuclear force to grab, which means they must be moving fast enough to overcome the push. That takes a temperature above about 12 million kelvin. The Sun's centre is at about 15 million kelvin, so fusion happens there, in the core, and nowhere near the surface. The surface, at a few thousand degrees, is merely glowing with the energy that has worked its way out.

#### Step 4: the Sun's budget

:::fig s_sun_budget() | What the Sun does every second: about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen fuse into about 596 million tonnes of helium, and the missing 4 million tonnes leaves as light and heat. Over 4.5 billion years that has used up about 0.03% of the Sun's mass.

Each second the Sun converts about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium. Of that, about 4 million tonnes becomes energy, which is exactly the 4 × 10²⁶ joules we see arriving. Four million tonnes a second sounds like a frightening rate of loss until you set it against the Sun's mass, which is about two thousand million million million million tonnes. Over its whole four and a half billion years, the Sun has converted about three parts in ten thousand of its mass into energy. Most of its protons have not yet taken part in a single fusion.

The energy made in the core does not reach us quickly. It staggers outwards as light, absorbed and re-emitted countless times, for somewhere between a hundred thousand and a million years before it escapes the surface and then takes eight minutes to reach Mumbai. The ghostly particles called neutrinos made in the same reactions do not stagger; they leave the Sun in about two seconds. Since January 2024, India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft has watched the Sun from a point about 1.5 million kilometres sunward of the Earth, looping around that point once every 178 days.

#### Step 5: fission and fusion, the same bookkeeping

Meitner's split uranium and the Sun's fusing hydrogen look like opposites: one breaks a heavy nucleus in two, the other joins light nuclei into one. They release energy for the same reason. In both, the pieces at the end weigh less than the pieces at the start, and the missing mass leaves as energy, exactly as E = mc² says. Which way a nucleus "goes" depends on its size: very heavy nuclei give up energy by splitting, very light ones by joining.

:::careful
> "The Sun is burning."

Burning is chemistry: atoms rearranging, with the Sun's whole supply used up in a few thousand years. The Sun is fusing: nuclei joining, at its centre, and a nucleus rearranging releases hundreds of thousands of times the energy of any chemical change. Say "shining", or "fusing", and keep "burning" for the kitchen.
:::

:::careful
> "E = mc² destroys mass."

Nothing is destroyed. The total energy, counting mass as one of its forms, is exactly the same before and after; it has changed form, from mass into light and motion. Think of it as bookkeeping with a very large exchange rate.
:::

:::careful
> "E = mc² is only about bombs."

The Sun has been running on it for four and a half billion years, and every bit of warmth on your arm at Marine Drive is mass that left the Sun eight minutes ago as light. The equation is about where sunlight comes from.
:::
