:::scene Between the Earth and the Sun, 6 January 2024

On 6 January 2024, about 1.5 million km from Earth in the direction of the Sun, an Indian spacecraft called ==Aditya-L1== fired its engines and settled into an orbit around a point in empty space. Not around a planet, not around a moon: around a point, with nothing there. ISRO calls the path a halo orbit, and one loop of it takes about 178 days.

The point has a name, ==L1==, the first ==Lagrange point==, and to understand why anyone would park a spacecraft there you need two ideas you have already met: Kepler's third law, and the adding-up of pulls.

#### Step 1: the problem

Aditya-L1 is a Sun-watcher, built for an uninterrupted view, day and night, year after year. From the ground that is impossible: night, clouds and the whole blurring atmosphere get in the way. From a low orbit round Earth it is not much better, because for part of every lap the Earth itself is between the spacecraft and the Sun. What you want is a spot that stays on the sunward side of Earth, out of Earth's shadow, for ever. The obvious idea is to put the spacecraft a little nearer the Sun than Earth and let it travel round the Sun alongside us.

#### Step 2: the catch

Kepler's third law says that cannot work, at first glance. A body orbiting the Sun on a smaller circle takes less time to go round. Put a spacecraft 1.5 million km sunward of Earth, give it an ordinary orbit of its own, and it would lap the Sun in about 360 days instead of 365. It would gain five and a half days on Earth every year, drift steadily ahead, and within a few decades be on the far side of the Sun.

:::work
Period of a free orbit at Earth's distance from the Sun: 365.3 days.
Period of a free orbit 1.5 million km closer in, with Earth's pull switched off: about 359.8 days.
Difference: about 5.5 days a year. Half a lap is 183 days, so the spacecraft would be on the far side of the Sun after 183 ÷ 5.5 ≈ 33 years.
:::

#### Step 3: Earth's pull fixes it

Now switch Earth's pull back on. From that spot, Earth lies directly away from the Sun, so Earth pulls the spacecraft *away* from the Sun, in exactly the opposite direction to the Sun's pull. Earth's pull there is small, about 3% of the Sun's, but it subtracts. The spacecraft feels a little less inward pull than the Sun alone would give it.

And a little less inward pull is exactly what a slower orbit needs. To go round in 365 days rather than 360 at that radius, the spacecraft must be pulled inward slightly less hard than a free body would be. Earth's backward tug provides precisely that reduction at one particular distance. There, and only there along the line, the combined pull matches a one-year lap, and the spacecraft goes round the Sun in step with Earth, staying on the sunward side for ever. That spot is L1.

:::work
Where the pulls combine in this way: about 1.5 million km from Earth, which is 1% of the way to the Sun, or about four times the Moon's distance.
Notice what L1 is *not*. It is not where the Sun's pull and Earth's pull are equal; that point is only about 260,000 km from Earth, inside the Moon's orbit, and a spacecraft there would not keep pace with anything. At L1 the Sun's pull is about thirty times Earth's. L1 is where the two pulls *combine* to give exactly a one-year orbit.
:::

:::fig s_l1_balance() | Not to scale. At L1 the Sun pulls the spacecraft sunward and Earth pulls it the other way; the Sun wins, but by slightly less than it would with no Earth there, and that slightly smaller combined pull is just right for a one-year orbit at that distance. Aditya-L1 does not sit on the point; it loops round it every 178 days.

#### Step 4: a halo, not a parking spot

Nothing sits still at L1. Aditya-L1 travels in a wide loop around the point, the halo orbit, completing one loop in about 178 days, roughly six months, while the whole arrangement, Earth, L1 and spacecraft, goes round the Sun once a year. From the spacecraft the Sun is always in view, and Earth and the Moon are always behind it, never blocking the light.

#### Step 5: what it is for

From that vantage point Aditya-L1 watches the Sun's outer layers and the stream of particles the Sun blows past Earth, continuously, without the gaps that any telescope on the ground must suffer. :::careful
> "Aditya-L1 is parked at L1, where gravity cancels out, so it just stays there."

Gravity does not cancel at L1; the Sun's pull there is about thirty times Earth's. The two pulls *combine* to the exact value a one-year orbit needs. And the spacecraft does not sit on the point; it loops round it every 178 days.
:::

:::careful
> "A spacecraft nearer the Sun than Earth must go round faster than Earth."

On its own, yes: that is Kepler's third law. At L1 it is not on its own. Earth's backward tug takes away just enough of the Sun's pull to let it dawdle round in a full year, keeping pace with us.
:::
