:::scene Florence, about 1589

Vincenzo Galilei was a musician, a player of the lute, with a scientist's habit of checking things. He also had a son in his twenties called Galileo.

The thing Vincenzo checked was two thousand years old. The story goes that Pythagoras, walking past a blacksmith's, heard the hammers ringing out notes in harmony, weighed the hammers, and found that their weights stood in simple ratios, two to one for the octave. From that, so the story says, came the laws of harmony. It is a legend. It was first written down by Nicomachus about six hundred years after Pythagoras lived, and the physics in it is wrong: the weight of a hammer does not set its note.

Vincenzo tested the string version of the idea. Everyone knew that a string half as long sounds an octave higher. The legend's logic says that a string pulled twice as tight should do the same. So Vincenzo hung weights from strings to stretch them, and listened. Twice the weight did not give an octave. Four times the weight did. There was a simple ratio in it, but it was the square of the one in the story, and only an experiment could have found that.

That one measurement is the first true thing in this story. The rest is about why a string sings a note at all, why a drum usually does not, and how drum-makers in India made one that does.

#### Step 1: what pitch is

Pluck a string and it vibrates, swinging back and forth too fast to see. The air beside it is shoved back and forth at the same rate, and that is what reaches your ear. The number of vibrations each second is the frequency; what you hear is ==pitch==. Fast vibration, high note; slow vibration, low note. Human ears hear from about 20 vibrations a second up to about 20,000.

Pull a string tighter and it vibrates faster, so its note rises. That is what Vincenzo's weights were doing, and it is what a tabla player does when tuning: the head is tightened or slackened until the note is right.

#### Step 2: a string's ladder of notes

A string fixed at both ends cannot vibrate in just any way. Its ends are held still, so the simplest pattern is the whole string swinging as one hump, out and back. That gives the lowest note the string can make, its fundamental. But the string can also swing in two halves, with the middle point still and the two halves moving opposite ways; or in three thirds; or four quarters. Each pattern has its own frequency, and because a half is half as long as the whole, the halves vibrate exactly twice as fast; the thirds three times as fast; the quarters four times.

When you pluck a string you set off many of these patterns at once, so the string sounds its fundamental and a stack of higher notes with it. The higher notes are called ==overtones==. Overtones that are whole-number multiples of the fundamental (two times, three times, four times, and so on) are called ==harmonics==. For an ideal string every overtone is a harmonic: the rungs of its ladder sit at 1, 2, 3, 4 times the lowest note, evenly spaced.

Your ear does something clever with a ladder like that. It hears the whole stack as one note, the fundamental, with a particular colour. How strong each rung is sets the colour, and that is why a violin and a piano playing the same note sound nothing alike.

Now Vincenzo's result makes sense. A wiggle travels along a tighter string faster, and the speed grows with the square root of the pull. To double every frequency on the ladder you must double the speed, and to double the speed you need four times the pull. Four times the weight: an octave.

:::work
**A string's ladder.** If the whole-string hump vibrates 100 times a second:
halves → 200 Hz · thirds → 300 Hz · quarters → 400 Hz · fifths → 500 Hz.
Every rung is a whole-number multiple, so the ear hears one note at 100 Hz, coloured by the rest.
:::

#### Step 3: why a drum thuds

A drumhead is a skin held still all round its rim, and it too can only vibrate in certain patterns: one hump in the middle; two halves rocking like a seesaw; a ring moving opposite to the centre; quarters; and so on. The catch is in the frequencies. Work them out for a perfectly even, flat drumhead and the ladder comes out at 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30 and 2.65 times the lowest note. Not one of those is a whole number. The rungs are out of tune with one another, so the ear cannot gather them into a single note, and what you hear is a thud with a vague sense of height. That is most drums, from an upturned bucket to a bass drum.

:::work
**A plain drumhead's ladder.** If its lowest pattern vibrates 100 times a second, the next ones are at about
159 Hz, 214 Hz, 230 Hz and 265 Hz.
No whole numbers. Played together they clash, and the ear gives up on finding a note.
:::

:::scene Calcutta, 1920

Strike a tabla and you get something different: a clear, singing note you could hum back. In January 1920 C. V. Raman and Sivakali Kumar published a short paper in *Nature* on why. Most drums, they showed, give overtones that are out of tune, just as the arithmetic says. The tabla and the mridangam give overtones that are harmonic, in whole-number steps like a string's. The reason is the black patch.

Look at a tabla's head. In the middle is a dark disc, the syahi, a patch that loads the centre of the skin and makes it heavier than the rest. Loading the middle changes the rhythms of the different patterns, most of all the ones that move the middle most, and the patch is made to just the size and weight that pull the out-of-tune rungs onto whole numbers. The drum-makers found this by ear, generations before anyone could write down why it worked. Raman and Kumar wrote it down.

Middle: the ladder of overtones for a string (whole-number rungs) and for an ideal flat drumhead (1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30 times the lowest: out of tune). Right: the tabla's syahi loads the middle of the head and pulls its overtones onto the whole-number rungs.

:::careful
> "Hit the tabla harder and the note goes up."

Harder gives a bigger vibration, and a bigger vibration is louder, not higher. Pitch is set by how fast the head vibrates, and that depends on how tight it is and how big it is, which a harder blow does not change. Loudness and pitch are separate knobs. Turning one does not turn the other.
:::

:::home Notes from a bottle, and a string cut in half
Blow gently across the top of an empty glass bottle until it toots. Pour in some water and blow again: the note is higher. The air in the neck moves against the springiness of the air inside, and less enclosed air makes a stiffer spring. More water, higher still. If there is a guitar or any stringed instrument at home, try this too: pluck a string, then pluck it again while touching it very lightly at its exact middle with a fingertip. The note jumps an octave higher and sounds thinner. Your finger has held the middle still, so the string can only vibrate in halves, and you are hearing the second rung of its ladder on its own.
:::
