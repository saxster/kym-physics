:::scene The Tacoma Narrows, Washington State, 7 November 1940

The bridge was four months old. It had opened on 1 July 1940 across a narrow arm of the sea near Tacoma, with a main span of 2,800 feet, about 850 metres, the third-longest in the world. It was also unusually slim. Its designer, Leon Moisseiff, had stiffened the two-lane deck, only 12 metres wide, with solid steel girders 2.4 metres deep instead of the much deeper open framework first proposed, and the result was elegant and light.

It was also lively. From the first weeks, in quite mild winds, the deck rose and fell in slow waves, several feet high, every four or five seconds. The workers who built it called it Galloping Gertie. People drove across for the ride. Engineers tied it down with cables, which snapped, and fitted dampers, which failed. A professor of civil engineering from the University of Washington, F. B. Farquharson, was hired to study the motion and find a cure, and his wind-tunnel tests on a model finished on 2 November.

#### The morning

On 7 November a steady wind of about 42 miles an hour, 68 km/h, was blowing down the Narrows. It was not a storm, and it was not gusting. At about a quarter to ten the deck began its usual rise and fall. Then it did something new. It began to twist, one side of the roadway lifting while the other dropped, about twelve times a minute, once every five seconds, and the twisting grew.

Leonard Coatsworth, an editor at the local newspaper, was driving across towards Tacoma with his daughter's dog in the back seat. Tubby was a cocker spaniel who had lost a leg and was partly paralysed. Halfway across, the heaving became too much to drive through. Coatsworth got out and was thrown to the road. He tried to reach Tubby, who was far too frightened to leave the car, and in the end crawled on hands and knees to the shore.

Others tried. The last to try was Farquharson himself, who had come that morning with cameras to film the motion he had been studying. He walked out along the twisting deck to the car, keeping to the middle, where the twist moved least. Tubby, terrified, bit him. Farquharson made his way back. A few minutes later, at about eleven o'clock, the main span tore loose and fell into the water, which was more than a hundred feet deep, and Coatsworth's car and Tubby went with it. Neither was ever found. Tubby was the only life lost that day. A frightened animal bites the hand that reaches for it; nobody blamed him. "What appals me most," Coatsworth wrote afterwards, "is that within a few hours I must tell my daughter that her dog is dead."

Two men from a camera shop in Tacoma filmed the whole collapse on colour film. Most copies you will see run about half again too fast, because the film was shot at a slower frame rate than it is usually played, so the real twisting was even more stately than it looks.

#### What it was not

Nearly every physics textbook, and some you may own, says the bridge was destroyed by resonance: gusts of wind arriving in time with the bridge's natural rhythm, like a parent pushing a swing. The wind that day was not gusting. Two engineers, Billah and Scanlan, showed in 1991 that the textbook story does not work, and the course notes this book is built on are blunt about it. This book does not repeat it.

#### What it was

What happened is called flutter, and it is the same thing that makes a flag flap in a steady breeze. The deck twisted slightly. A twisted deck meets the wind at an angle, and the wind then pushes on it differently: harder on the side that has lifted. That changed push twists the deck further. The larger twist changes the push again. Each twist feeds the next, and the bridge makes its own rhythm out of a wind that has none. Engineers call it self-excitation, or negative damping: instead of the air soaking up the motion as it normally would, the air was feeding it. Resonance needs a push that keeps time. Flutter needs only a steady wind and a deck that is too slim and too flexible to resist the first small twist.

:::fig c9_flutter() | Left: what the textbooks describe, gusts arriving in time with the bridge's swing, which would be resonance. Right: what happened at Tacoma, a steady wind, a twist, and a push that changes with the twist, so each one feeds the next. That is flutter.

The sunken deck lies where it fell and is now an artificial reef, listed as a historic place. A new bridge opened on the same piers in 1950, deeper and stiffer, and every long bridge since has been tested in a wind tunnel for flutter before it is built. The collapse that cost one dog's life is the reason bridge engineers study the wind at all.

:::careful
> "The wind just happened to match the bridge's rhythm."

There was no rhythm in the wind to match. A steady wind and a flexible deck are enough: the deck's own twisting changes the push on it, and the push grows the twist. Watch a flag in a steady breeze. The breeze is not flapping. The flag is.
:::

Hold a strip of paper about 3 cm wide and 30 cm long by one end in front of a fan, lying along the airflow. The air is steady. The paper flaps. Try a strip of stiff card: it holds still. Flexibility is what lets the first small bend change the push and feed the next, and that is Galloping Gertie in miniature.
