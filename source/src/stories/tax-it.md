:::scene London, 1899

Here is the story. The politician William Gladstone, the most powerful man in Britain, is shown round Michael Faraday's laboratory. Faraday explains his latest discovery. Gladstone listens, frowns, and asks the question politicians ask: "But, after all, what use is it?" And Faraday replies: "Why, sir, there is every probability that you will soon be able to tax it."

It is a perfect story. The scientist is witty, the politician is dull, and electricity did indeed turn out to be useful, and taxable. It is in textbooks, on posters and in speeches. And it almost certainly never happened.

#### Following the trail

A good test of any quotation is to ask where it was first written down. This one first appears in 1899, in a book called *Democracy and Liberty* by the historian W. E. H. Lecky, who says he had it from "an intimate friend of Faraday". Faraday had died in 1867. So the earliest record is a second-hand memory, written down thirty-two years after the man was dead, of a conversation nobody dated.

It gets thinner. Lecky's version does not say what the discovery was; "electricity" was added by later tellers, in 1918 and again in 1977, when the tidier wording "One day, sir, you may tax it" appeared. Nothing like it is in Faraday's own letters or notebooks, or in anything written by the people who knew him. Gladstone became prime minister in 1868, the year after Faraday died, so the grand version is impossible; he had earlier been Chancellor of the Exchequer, the minister in charge of taxes, so a meeting is possible. Possible is not evidence. The fact-checkers who have looked call it almost certainly invented.

Its twin is the same. "What use is a new-born baby?", which Faraday is supposed to have said when asked the same question, has no source from his lifetime either. One widely used textbook has Queen Victoria asking the question; that version is unverified too.

#### What did happen

The true story needs no quip. On 3 and 4 September 1821, at the Royal Institution in London, Faraday made a wire hang in a dish of mercury beside a magnet and, with a current flowing, watched the wire go round and round the magnet by itself. That was the first electric motor. Ten years later, on 29 August 1831, he wound two coils of wire on an iron ring, connected one to a battery, and saw the needle of a meter on the *other* coil jump at the moment he closed the switch, and jump the other way when he opened it. That is electromagnetic induction, and that ring is the ancestor of every generator and every transformer in the world. The machines that make Mumbai's electricity, in Chapter 13, are Faraday's ring and spinning wire grown very large. The use of it was never in doubt. The tax came later.

#### Three questions

You can run the same test on any story in this book, and you should. Ask: who first wrote it down? How long after the event? Were they there?

- Faraday's tax: written down in 1899, thirty-two years after his death, by someone who was not there. Legend.
- Galileo on the Leaning Tower of Pisa: written down in 1654 by his assistant Viviani, twelve years after Galileo's death, not there. Legend, told on its own page.
- Hertz saying his radio waves were "of no use whatsoever": no source from his time at all. The story goes.
- Newton's apple: told by Newton himself, to a friend who wrote it down in 1726, sixty years after the event. True in outline, polished in the telling.
- Franklin's kite: written up by a friend fifteen years later; the only witness was Franklin's son. Historians disagree.
- Stevin's drop at Delft: done by Stevin, written by Stevin, printed by Stevin in 1586, the same year. Documented.

Notice that the best stories cluster at the top of the list and the best evidence at the bottom. That is not an accident. A story that is sharpened each time it is told gets better and less true, and the ones that survive are the ones that were sharpened most.

:::careful
> "It's in the textbook, so it must be true."

Textbooks copy each other, and a quotation can travel through fifty of them without anyone going back to the start. The chain always ends somewhere. When it ends thirty years late with an unnamed friend, you have a story, not a fact, and the honest thing is to say so, which is what this book does.
:::
