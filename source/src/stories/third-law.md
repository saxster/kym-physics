:::scene London, 1687

In the summer of 1687 a book in Latin came off a London press, paid for out of the pocket of the astronomer Edmond Halley, because the Royal Society had spent its money on a lavish history of fishes that did not sell. The book was Isaac Newton's *Principia*. Near its front stand three laws of motion, and the third is the one that people have found hardest ever since; the physics course at MIT still calls it the most subtle of the three. Newton gave two everyday examples. "If you press a stone with your finger, the finger is also pressed by the stone." And if a horse pulls a stone along by a rope, the horse, he wrote, "will be equally drawn back towards the stone": the stretched rope pulls the horse backward exactly as hard as it pulls the stone forward.

Read that again, because it says something strange. The horse is strong and the stone is dead weight, yet the rope pulls each of them with the same force. The law puts it generally: whenever one body pushes or pulls on another, the second pushes or pulls back on the first, equally hard and in the opposite direction. Always. At the same instant. For every force in the universe there is a partner force.

#### Step 1: One interaction, two forces

The cleanest way to hold the law in your head is this: a force is never a thing that one object has. It is an *interaction* between two objects, and like a handshake it has two ends. When your hand pushes a door, the door pushes your hand; you can feel it in your palm. When the Earth pulls you down, you pull the Earth up. The two forces of a pair are always the same size, always opposite in direction, and always act on *different* objects: one on the door, one on your hand. That last point matters more than any other. Because the two forces act on different things, they never cancel each other out. The door moves because of the force on the door; your hand feels the force on your hand.

Which of the two you call the action and which the reaction is up to you. Nothing happens first. The stone does not wait for the finger to press before it presses back; both forces exist together, and vanish together.

#### Step 2: The marble and the train

Here is the test MIT puts to its students. A marble hits a moving train. At the instant of impact, which feels the bigger force, the marble or the train? Nearly everyone says the marble, because the marble is smashed and the train is not. Wrong: the forces are equal. What differs is the *effect*. The same force gives a marble an enormous change in velocity and gives a train a change too small to measure, because the train has millions of times the marble's mass. Equal forces; wildly unequal accelerations. When a truck and a car collide, the same thing is true: the forces on the two are equal, and the car is wrecked because it is lighter and weaker, not because it was pushed harder. In a study of about 900 university students in Pittsburgh, 73 per cent said the truck pushes harder before their physics course, and 39 per cent still said so after it.

:::work
**Same force, different effect** (the masses are round guesses).
A car of 1,000 kg and a loaded truck of 10,000 kg push on each other with equal forces during a collision.
Chapter 3 shows that the same force changes velocity in inverse proportion to mass. The truck has ten times the mass, so its velocity changes one-tenth as much as the car's.
Now the extreme case. You stand on the floor. The Earth pulls you down with your weight, about 392 N if your mass is 40 kg, and you pull the Earth up with 392 N. The Earth's mass is 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, so its acceleration towards you is 392 ÷ (5.97 × 10²⁴) = 6.6 × 10⁻²³ m/s². The force is there. The effect is invisible.
:::

#### Step 3: Prove it with two luggage scales

You can measure the law in your hallway. Hook two hanging luggage scales together, the kind families use for airline bags. You pull one handle and an adult pulls the other, as hard as either of you likes. Both scales read the same, to within about a tenth of a kilogram, however much harder one of you "pulls". Hook one scale to a door handle instead and pull the other: the two still agree. A luggage scale reads in kilograms, but it is measuring a force; a reading of 10 kg means a pull of about 98 N, and the door handle is pulling back with 98 N too.

:::fig s_two_scales() | Two luggage scales hooked together and pulled from both ends. Each scale reads the pull on it, and the two readings agree whatever the two pullers do: where the hooks meet, the pair of forces are equal and opposite. Hook one end to a door handle and nothing changes.

#### Step 4: Which pairs are not pairs

The commonest slip, found in plenty of textbooks, is to pick the wrong partner. Stand still on the floor. The Earth pulls you down (your weight) and the floor pushes you up. Those two forces are equal and opposite, and they are *not* a third-law pair, because both of them act on the same object: you. They balance, which is why you are not accelerating, but balancing is a different thing from partnering. Your weight's partner is you pulling the Earth up. The floor's push has its partner in you pushing the floor down. Write pairs out by name, always with two objects: "the Earth on Kym, Kym on the Earth"; "the floor on Kym's shoe, Kym's shoe on the floor". If a sentence has only one object in it, it is not describing a pair.

#### Step 5: Getting about by pushing the world

Once you see pairs everywhere, you see how anything moves at all. When you walk, your foot pushes the floor backward, and the floor pushes your foot forward: friction, pointing the way you want to go. A swimmer pushes the wall backward and the wall sends her down the pool. A car's tyres push the road backward. A helicopter pushes air downward and the air pushes it up. A rocket throws hot gas out of its tail and the gas pushes the rocket the other way, which is why rockets work in empty space, where there is nothing else to push on. That last example has a story of its own, *Rockets from a church*.

:::careful
> "The truck hits the car harder than the car hits the truck."

It does not. The force of the truck on the car and the force of the car on the truck are one interaction seen from its two ends, and they are equal at every instant. The car comes off worse because the same force produces a bigger change in velocity in a lighter body, and because a car's body crumples more easily than a truck's. "Harder" belongs to the damage, not to the force.
:::

:::school
Many school books word the law as "to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction", which is close to Newton's own wording. The two words can mislead: there is no delay, and "reaction" does not mean a reply. In an exam answer, name both objects and both forces, and say which object each force acts on.
:::

:::home Two scales, one pull
Borrow two luggage scales. Hook them together and pull against a friend or a parent; read both. Now one of you pulls gently and the other pulls as hard as possible: read again. Hook one scale to a sturdy door handle and pull the other. Then sit on a chair with wheels on a smooth floor, push flat-handed against a wall, and notice which way you go and what pushed you.
:::
