:::scene Haji Ali, Mumbai, any morning

The dargah of Haji Ali stands on a rock in the sea off Worli, joined to the shore by a narrow causeway. Twice a day the sea rises until the causeway disappears under it, and twice a day it falls and the path reappears and people walk out. If you went every day you would notice something: the times shift. Each ==high tide== comes about fifty minutes later than it did the day before. So does each ==low tide==.

Fifty minutes a day is a number you have met before. It is how much later the Moon rises each day. The tides are keeping time with the Moon. The person who first explained why was Isaac Newton, in 1687, in the same book that explained the Moon's orbit, and he did it with the same pull.

#### Step 1: the Moon does not lift the water

The usual picture is that the Moon pulls the sea up towards itself, like a magnet lifting iron filings. That picture is wrong, and the first reason is simple: the Moon's pull on a bucket of water here is about three millionths of Earth's pull on it. Nothing is being lifted. Whatever the Moon does to the sea, it does by nudging, not hoisting.

#### Step 2: the pull is not the same everywhere

Here is the real idea. Earth is 12,742 km across. Its near side is nearer the Moon than its centre is, and its far side is further away. Gravity weakens with distance, so the Moon pulls each kilogram on the near side a little harder than it pulls Earth as a whole, and each kilogram on the far side a little less.

:::work
The Moon's pull per kilogram, worked out from its mass and distance:
on the side of Earth facing the Moon: 3.43 × 10⁻⁵ m/s²
at Earth's centre: 3.32 × 10⁻⁵ m/s²
on the far side: 3.21 × 10⁻⁵ m/s²
The near side is pulled about 3% harder than the centre, the far side about 3% less. Those *differences* are the tide-raising forces, and each is about one ten-millionth of Earth's own gravity.
:::

The whole Earth, land and sea together, responds to the pull at its centre; that is the pull that keeps it circling with the Moon round their shared balance point. The water on the near side gets a little more than that, so it is drawn a little ahead, towards the Moon. The water on the far side gets a little less, so it lags a little behind, away from the Moon. Two sides, two small leftover nudges, pointing in opposite directions.

#### Step 3: two heaps of water

A nudge of one ten-millionth of gravity cannot lift anything. But it acts sideways as well as outward, and it acts for hours on water that is free to move. Over those hours the water flows, slowly, over Earth's surface, piling up into two low, wide heaps: one under the Moon and one on the opposite side of Earth. The heaps are not water being lifted or stretched; they are water that has flowed in from the sides.

Earth turns underneath those two heaps once a day. A place like Mumbai is carried through the first heap, then through a low in between, then through the second heap, then another low: two high tides and two low tides a day.

:::fig s_tide_bulges() | Left: the Moon pulls the near side of Earth a little harder than the centre and the far side a little less; over hours those small differences make the water flow into two heaps, one facing the Moon and one opposite. Right: at new and full Moon the Sun's two heaps line up with the Moon's and the tides are at their biggest (spring tides); at the quarters they sit at right angles and partly cancel (neap tides). The heaps are drawn hugely exaggerated, the pull differences too, and the Moon is really thirty Earth-diameters away.

Why fifty minutes late? While Earth turns once, the Moon moves on about 12° along its orbit, and Earth has to turn for another fifty minutes to bring Mumbai back under it. So the Moon's day, and the tide's, is 24 hours 50 minutes, and the two highs come about 12 hours 25 minutes apart.

#### Step 4: the Sun joins in

The Sun pulls on Earth too, far harder than the Moon does; but it is so far away that its pull is almost the *same* on both sides of Earth, and tides come from differences. Worked out from the masses and distances, the Sun's tide-raising effect is about 46% of the Moon's: real, but less than half.

At new Moon and at full Moon the Sun, Earth and Moon lie in a line, so the Sun's two heaps sit on top of the Moon's two heaps and the tides run higher than usual. These are ==spring tides==, and the name has nothing to do with the season: the water "springs up". A week later, at the quarter Moons, the Sun is at a right angle to the Moon, its heaps sit in the Moon's hollows, and the tides are tamer: ==neap tides==. Spring tides return roughly every fifteen days, twice a month.

#### Step 5: the real sea, and Mumbai

If Earth were covered in one smooth ocean, the heaps would be under a metre high. The real sea sits in basins with coasts, shallows and channels; water sloshes against land, rubs on the seabed, is pushed by wind and swung by Earth's turning. So the simple two-heap picture gives you the *rhythm* of the tides but not their size or their exact timing at any one place. For those, every port publishes a tide table worked out from years of measurement. At Mumbai the two highs of a day are usually of unequal height, and high water does not arrive at the moment the Moon is overhead but some hours after.

For heights in metres and the times at Haji Ali, read a published table: the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) and the Survey of India both issue them. Do not take a number from a news report or a website that gives no source; this book doesn't.

Two more things the tides do, slowly. The friction of all that moving water is braking Earth's spin, lengthening the day by about two thousandths of a second each century. And the Moon is drifting away from us by about 3.8 cm a year, a distance measured by bouncing laser beams off mirrors that the Apollo astronauts left on its surface.

:::careful
> "The Moon pulls the water up towards itself."

The Moon's pull on the sea is about three millionths of Earth's. It lifts nothing. What raises a tide is the *difference* between the Moon's pull on the near side, the centre and the far side of Earth, acting sideways for hours on water that is free to flow. Hence two heaps, not one, and hence a high tide on the side facing away from the Moon as well.
:::

:::careful
> "High tide is when the Moon is overhead."

The simple picture says so; the real sea runs late. Water takes time to flow into the heaps, coasts get in the way, and basins slosh to their own rhythms, so at any port high water lags the Moon by hours. The Moon sets the beat; the local coastline sets the delay. That is why tide tables exist.
:::

:::home A month of tides
Ask an adult to help you find an official tide table for Mumbai (INCOIS or the Survey of India). Each day for a month, write down the time of the morning high tide and, from your own eyes or a calendar, the Moon's phase. Two things will show up: the high tide creeping later by about fifty minutes a day, and the highest highs clustering around new and full Moon. If you go to see the causeway, watch from the shore with an adult, and never walk out onto it when the tide is coming in.
:::
