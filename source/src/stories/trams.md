:::scene Colaba, Bombay, 1904

At the southern tip of Bombay, past the docks, stood an observatory that had been watching one thing since 1841: the direction and strength of the Earth's magnetism. From 1846 someone read its instruments every hour, day and night. From 1872 the reading was done by light, a beam reflected from a mirror on the magnet tracing a line on photographic paper, so that every twitch of the needle was recorded. Charles Chambers, the superintendent, published Bombay's magnetic results in the Royal Society's journal. His successor, Nanabhoy Moos, the first Indian to direct the observatory, would gather the whole record, 1846 to 1905, into two enormous volumes, and find in it that the needle's disturbances follow the spots on the Sun.

The record showed things nobody could have seen any other way. The compass in Bombay had been pointing 57 minutes of arc east of true north in 1880, and by 1904 the drift had brought it to 10 minutes; today it points almost exactly true north. Storms on the Sun shook the needle. Sixty years of hourly readings from one spot is one of the longest such records in the world, and it was about to be ruined by public transport.

#### The problem with trams

Bombay had horse trams from 1874, and the tram company wanted to run them on electricity. An electric tram draws a large steady current from an overhead wire and sends it back through the rails and the ground. Chapter 13 tells you what Ørsted found in 1820: a current makes a magnetic field, in circles around the wire. A tram line is a wire the length of a city.

:::work
**How much would a tram disturb the needle?** No record survives of the planned tram currents, so take a guess of 500 amperes flowing along a line 200 metres from the observatory. The field of a long straight current is B = μ₀I ÷ (2πr), with μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ in SI units.
B = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 500) ÷ (2π × 200) = 5 × 10⁻⁷ tesla, which is 500 nanotesla.
The sideways part of the Earth's field in Mumbai is about 38,400 nanotesla. So each passing tram would swing the needle by about 1.3 per cent of the whole field. The changes the observatory existed to track, the daily wobble and the slow drift, are far smaller than that. Every tram, every few minutes, all day: the record would have drowned.
:::

:::fig s_tram_field() | A tram's current makes a magnetic field in circles around the wire and rails. Two hundred metres away the field of a 500 A current (a guess; no record of the planned current survives) is about 500 nT, a swing of over one per cent in the Earth's field, far larger than the small changes the observatory was built to record.

Moos understood what was coming and helped to find the answer, which was not to fight the trams but to get out of their way. A new observatory was built at Alibag, about thirty kilometres south across the harbour, far from any wire. It was running by 1904. For two years, 1904 to 1906, both observatories recorded at once, side by side, so that the two records could be matched and the sixty-year series continued without a break. Then Colaba's magnetic recording stopped. On 7 May 1907, at half past five in the afternoon, the first electric tram ran from the Municipal Office to Crawford Market. The date varies a little between sources, but the order of events does not: the magnets left first.

Alibag is still recording. The Earth's field there today is about 43,400 nanotesla, dipping 27° into the ground, and the station is one of the reference points by which the whole planet's magnetism is mapped.

#### The lesson of the sensitive instrument

Notice what kind of problem this was. The trams were not going to break anything. They were simply going to add a disturbance, small by any ordinary standard, that was large compared with the signal. It is the same problem Cavendish solved with his shed and his holes in the wall, and that Faraday solved with his foil box: when your instrument is sensitive enough to be interesting, the world around it becomes noise. You can shield, or you can move. Bombay moved.

:::careful
> "A compass just points north, so a magnetic observatory must be a very boring place."

It does not point exactly north, and it does not point the same way from year to year, and it twitches when the Sun erupts. Those changes are the whole point. In Bombay the needle drifted by nearly a degree in a quarter of a century, and a great storm on the Sun can swing it by far more in an hour. The observatory tracked the changes; a tram would have buried them.
:::
