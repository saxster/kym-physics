:::scene Leiden, 1722

A cannonball in flight can do things a cannonball at rest cannot. It can knock down a wall, dent a shield, stop a charging horse. So a moving thing must carry *something*, and in the early 1700s the cleverest people in Europe were quarrelling about how to measure it.

One side followed René Descartes. Multiply the mass by the speed, they said: twice the speed, twice the "quantity of motion". The other side followed Gottfried Leibniz. Multiply the mass by the speed *squared*, they said: twice the speed, four times as much. Leibniz's followers called their quantity *vis viva*, living force. Both sides used the word "force" for what they were measuring, which made the argument harder, because today that word means a push or a pull, and neither mv nor mv² is a push.

At Leiden, in the Netherlands, Willem 's Gravesande decided to ask the clay.

#### Step 1: the dent test

He took brass balls and dropped them into a tray of soft clay. A ball hits the clay, the clay pushes back, and the ball stops after sinking in some distance. That distance, the depth of the dent, is what he measured.

Why the dent? Because it measures how much pushing it takes to stop the ball. The clay resists with a roughly steady force all the way down, so a dent twice as deep means the clay had to push for twice the distance. If a moving body "carries" something, a fair measure of it is how much work it takes to bring it to rest.

#### Step 2: making a ball arrive twice as fast

You cannot throw a ball at an exact speed, but you can drop it, and a dropped ball's speed depends only on the height it falls from. The catch is that doubling the height does not double the speed. To arrive twice as fast, a ball must fall from four times the height, because the speed of a falling object grows with the square root of the drop (Chapter 1 explains why).

:::work
**Two drops, one twice as fast as the other** (our heights, not his)
From 25 cm: speed at the clay = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.25) = 2.2 m/s.
From 1 m, four times the height: √(2 × 9.8 × 1.0) = 4.4 m/s. Twice the speed.
:::

#### Step 3: what the clay said

The ball arriving twice as fast made a dent about four times as deep. Not twice. Four times. A ball arriving three times as fast would make a dent about nine times as deep. The dent followed the *square* of the speed.

So, if the thing a moving body carries is measured by the pushing needed to stop it, that thing goes as mv², Leibniz's way, not mv. 's Gravesande had started out on the other side of the argument, and the clay changed his mind.

:::fig s_clay_dents() | Two brass balls dropped into soft clay, one from a height, one from four times that height. The second arrives at twice the speed and makes a dent about four times as deep. The dents are drawn to that 1 : 4 ratio; the clay's steady resistance is what makes depth a fair measure of the stopping work.

:::careful
> "The deeper dent *is* the energy. The second ball had exactly four times as much."

Roughly four times. The dent is a fair measure only because the clay pushes back with a roughly steady force as the ball sinks. Real clay (and real flour, if you try this at home) stiffens as it is squashed, so the ratio comes out near four, not exactly four. Say "about four times, as the square law predicts", never "exactly".
:::

#### Step 4: a book in a château

:::scene Cirey, 1740

Eighteen years later, in a château in eastern France, Émilie du Châtelet published her *Institutions de physique*, a book meant to lay out the foundations of physics for her son. She came down firmly on Leibniz's side, and 's Gravesande's dents were among her evidence. People sometimes say she "proved" that the true measure is mv². She did not, and she did not claim to. She argued the case, clearly and with experiments behind her, in a quarrel that had already run for half a century.

The quarrel took most of another century to settle, and it settled in an unexpected way: both sides had been right.

#### Step 5: both were right

Mass times speed, mv, is real. Physicists now call it *momentum*, and it is the quantity that stays fixed when two things collide, as you will see in Chapter 3. Mass times speed squared is also real. With a half in front of it, ½mv², it is the ==kinetic energy==, the energy a thing has because it is moving, and it is the quantity that tells you how much work it takes to stop something, or how much work it took to get it going. (The half appears when you work out the pushing it takes to bring a body up to speed; Chapter 7 shows where it comes from.)

The two quantities answer different questions. How long must a force act to stop the ball? That is set by its momentum. How far must the force push? That is set by its kinetic energy. The clay answered the second question, and the second question is the one that goes as the square.

:::work
**A car at 50 km/h and the same car at 100 km/h**
Momentum, mv: twice as much at 100 km/h.
Kinetic energy, ½mv²: 2² = 4 times as much at 100 km/h.
If the brakes push with the same force both times, the stopping distance is four times as long. The brakes are doing the clay's job.
:::

:::home A marble in the atta
Pour atta or maida into a tray to a depth of a few centimetres and smooth the top. Drop a marble into it from 25 cm, then push a toothpick into the crater and mark the depth. Smooth the flour again and drop the same marble from 1 m, four times the height, so that it arrives twice as fast. Measure again. You should get a crater roughly four times as deep, not exactly four times, for the reason in the warning above. If you have a bigger marble, drop it from 25 cm and compare: double the mass, and the dent only doubles.
:::

The word *vis viva* has gone, and nobody now calls either quantity a force. But every time an engineer works out how fast a car can safely take a bend, or how much energy a cricket ball delivers to a bat, she is using the quantity that 's Gravesande read off the clay: mass times speed squared. In Chapter 3 you will meet mv under its own name, momentum, with a law all of its own. In Chapter 7, ½mv² joins the family of energies, and you will see why doubling your speed on a bicycle costs you four times the braking.
