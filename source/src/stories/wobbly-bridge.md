:::scene The Millennium Bridge, London, 10 June 2000

The new footbridge across the Thames was meant to look like a blade of light: 325 metres long, only four metres wide, designed by the engineers Arup with the architects Foster + Partners and the sculptor Anthony Caro, and opened, a month late, on 10 June 2000. About ninety thousand people crossed it that day, as many as two thousand at a time.

And as the crowds packed on, it began to sway. Not up and down but from side to side, the deck moving as much as seven centimetres each way. People stopped and grabbed the handrails. The sway did not die down while the crowd stayed. Two days later, on 12 June, the bridge was closed, and Londoners had already given it the name that stuck: the Wobbly Bridge.

#### The first explanation

Arup's engineers worked out what had happened and published it the next year. Walking is not only a push forwards. Each step also pushes the ground a little to one side, then the other, as your weight shifts from foot to foot. On solid ground the sideways pushes of a crowd point every which way and cancel. But a deck that sways even slightly changes how people walk. To keep their balance they adjust their steps to the movement, and that adjustment times their pushes with the sway. The pushes stop cancelling and start adding. More sway, more people adjusting, more push. The engineers called it synchronous lateral excitation; the plain description is a feedback loop, and it has nothing to do with anyone marching. Nobody was in step when they walked on. The sway came first and the steps followed.

:::fig s_wobble_loop() | The loop. A small sideways sway makes walkers adjust their steps; the adjusted steps push sideways in time with the sway; the sway grows. A damper breaks the loop by draining energy out of the motion faster than the walkers can put it in.

That makes it different from the old rule about soldiers, who are ordered to break step on a bridge because hundreds of boots landing together are a push that keeps time, and a push that keeps time at the bridge's own rhythm is resonance, the subject of Chapter 9. The Millennium Bridge supplied its own timing.

#### The cure

The cure was friction, of a controlled kind. Between May 2001 and January 2002 the engineers fitted 37 fluid-filled dampers, which work like the piston in a door-closer, and 52 tuned mass dampers, weights on springs that swing against the deck's motion and soak it up. The job cost £5 million. The bridge reopened on 22 February 2002, and the sway was gone. In the language of Chapter 9: more damping, lower peak. A loop that feeds a little energy in with every step cannot grow if the dampers take that energy out faster.

#### The second explanation

That might have been the end of it, but in 2021 a group of mathematicians led by Igor Belykh published a different account in the journal *Nature Communications*, and its title gives it away: the instability can emerge "without synchronisation". Their argument is that you do not need people to fall into step at all. Each walker, simply keeping balance on a surface that moves, adjusts their steps in a way that on average feeds a little energy into the sway, whether or not anyone else is doing the same. Engineers call that negative damping: a walker is a damper working backwards. Add enough walkers and the energy they feed in beats the energy the bridge can lose on its own, and the sway grows. The falling-into-step that everyone saw on the film, they say, was a result of the sway, not its cause. Their formula predicts a critical crowd size, and the group has suggested the Millennium Bridge could carry about 164 people steadily and that one more tipped it.

Which account is right is still argued, and you should know that honestly. Both agree about the cure, because both are about energy: walkers put it in, dampers take it out, and the bridge stands or sways depending on which wins.

:::careful
> "The crowd marched in step and set up a resonance, like soldiers on a bridge."

Nobody marched. Ninety thousand people walking at their own pace do not share a rhythm. The sway came first, and either the sway pulled their steps into time with it or, in the newer account, each walker fed it without any timing at all. Resonance needs a push that keeps time from outside; this bridge made its own.
:::
